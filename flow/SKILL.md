# Flow

Csikszentmihalyi's thesis: happiness "does not depend on outside events, but, rather, on how we interpret them." It "is a condition that must be prepared for, cultivated, and defended privately by each person. People who learn to control inner experience will be able to determine the quality of their lives." Happiness cannot be pursued directly ("it must ensue"); the path to it begins with **control over the contents of consciousness**.

**Optimal experience** is the times when we feel in control of our actions, masters of our own fate: a deep enjoyment that becomes "a landmark in memory for what life should be like." It is not passive or relaxing. The best moments "usually occur when a person's body or mind is stretched to its limits in a voluntary effort to accomplish something difficult and worthwhile. Optimal experience is thus something that we *make* happen."

**Flow** is "the state in which people are so involved in an activity that nothing else seems to matter; the experience itself is so enjoyable that people will do it even at great cost, for the sheer sake of doing it." Old women in Korea, teenagers in Tokyo, Navajo shepherds, Alpine farmers and Chicago assembly-line workers all describe it in essentially the same words. *What* they do differs; *how* it feels does not.

The book gives principles and examples, not recipes: "a joyful life is an individual creation that cannot be copied from a recipe." Apply each principle to the person's own goals, skills and circumstances, and let them build the method that suits them. Such wisdom "cannot be condensed into a formula": "It is not enough to *know* how to do it; one must *do* it, consistently." Aim to "increase the frequency and intensity of optimal experiences, rather than (or in addition to) decreasing the incidence of negative ones."

## The model of consciousness

- **Consciousness** is "intentionally ordered information." Its capacity is small: about seven bits at once, and about 1/18 of a second to tell one set of bits from the next, so at most 126 bits a second. Understanding one speaker takes 40 bits a second. So "the information we allow into consciousness... is, in fact, what determines the content and the quality of life."
- **Intentions** are "the force that keeps information in consciousness ordered." They are arranged in a **hierarchy of goals**.
- **Psychic energy** is attention: "without it no work can be done, and in doing work it is dissipated. We create ourselves by how we invest this energy." It is the main tool for improving experience. The mark of a person in control of consciousness is "the ability to focus attention at will, to be oblivious to distractions, to concentrate for as long as it takes to achieve a goal, and not longer."
- **The self** "represents the hierarchy of goals that we have built up, bit by bit." **Attention shapes the self, and is in turn shaped by it.** When Sam Browning was fifteen, a chance dive on a reef led to biology courses and then a career as a marine scientist. Even an external windfall (a lottery win, the right spouse) must connect positively to the self before it changes the quality of life.
- **Psychic entropy** (*psychic disorder*): "Whenever information disrupts consciousness by threatening its goals we have a condition of inner disorder... a disorganization of the self that impairs its effectiveness." Julio's flat tire, with no money until payday, kept intruding on his work and made him snap at coworkers. Attention goes to the threat in proportion to how central the goal is and how severe the threat.
- **Flow, or negentropy**, is the opposite: information that fits one's goals, so "attention can be freely invested to achieve a person's goals, because there is no disorder to straighten out, no threat for the self to defend against." Rico Medellin had a 43-second assembly task that he repeated about 600 times a day. He treated it like an Olympic event and got his best average down to 28 seconds. When he saw the limit coming, he took electronics courses to move to a more complex job.
- **Complexity** is how the self grows. It comes from **differentiation**, a "movement toward uniqueness, toward separating oneself from others", and **integration**, "a union with other people, with ideas and entities beyond the self." Differentiation alone risks "self-centered egotism." Integration alone gives connection and security without autonomous individuality. The self becomes complex only when equal psychic energy goes into both, avoiding "both selfishness and conformity." Every episode of flow does both. It leaves a person with rarer skills, and it pulls thoughts, feelings and senses together onto one goal.

## Pleasure and enjoyment

- **Pleasure** is "a feeling of contentment that one achieves whenever information in consciousness says that expectations set by biological programs or by social conditioning have been met." Food when hungry, rest, TV with a drink. Pleasure is homeostatic: it "helps to maintain order, but by itself cannot create new order in consciousness," and it needs no investment of psychic energy.
- **Enjoyment** comes when a person has "gone beyond what he or she has been programmed to do and achieved something unexpected, perhaps something even unimagined before." It is marked by forward movement, novelty and accomplishment: a close tennis game, a book that reveals something, a conversation that produces new ideas, a contested deal, any work well done. It may not be pleasant at the time, but afterward the self has grown. Anyone can take pleasure in eating, but enjoying food the way a gourmet does takes "unusual investments of attention."
- Signor Orsini, a Neapolitan antique dealer, refused to sell to a tourist who would not haggle: "since I am not [starving], why should I make a deal that isn't any fun?"
- When learning is imposed from outside, and new uses of psychic energy seem wasted without an extrinsic reward, "pleasure becomes the only source of positive experience."

## The eight elements of enjoyment

These are the same in every activity and culture studied. Use them as a checklist when diagnosing or designing an experience.

1. **A challenging activity that requires skills.** Most optimal experiences happen "within sequences of activities that are goal-directed and bounded by rules." **Challenges** are "a bundle of opportunities for action", and without the skills to act on them an activity "is not challenging; it is simply meaningless." Any capacity to handle symbolic information counts as a skill (reading, conversation). **Competition**: "Our antagonist is our helper" (Burke). But "competition is enjoyable only when it is a means to perfect one's skills; when it becomes an end in itself, it ceases to be fun."
2. **The merging of action and awareness.** Every relevant skill is in use, so no psychic energy is left over and people "stop being aware of themselves as separate from the actions they are performing." It feels effortless but takes strenuous effort, and "any lapse in concentration will erase it."
3. **Clear goals** and
4. **immediate feedback.** Creative work, whose goals are vague, needs internalized criteria: after each brush stroke the painter must be able to say "Yes, this works; no, this doesn't." The *kind* of feedback hardly matters. What matters is its symbolic message, that I have succeeded at my goal. "Almost any kind of feedback can be enjoyable, provided it is logically related to a goal in which one has invested psychic energy." People differ in which feedback they seek. Surgeons want the incision closed with no blood in the cavity, while psychiatrists read posture and hesitation. Fragile selves count only winning, and people invested in being liked count only approval.
5. **Concentration on the task at hand.** "The clearly structured demands of the activity impose order" and leave no room for everyday worries.
6. **The paradox of control.** People feel control, or "more precisely... lacking the sense of worry about losing control." What they enjoy "is not the sense of *being* in control, but the sense of *exercising* control in difficult situations," and that requires giving up "the safety of protective routines." Climbers distinguish **objective dangers** (storms, falling rock), which they avoid, from **subjective dangers** (their own lack of skill or misjudgment), which they eliminate through discipline. The thrill comes from minimizing danger, not from the danger itself.
7. **The loss of self-consciousness.** It "does not involve a loss of self, and certainly not a loss of consciousness, but rather, only a loss of consciousness *of* the self. What slips below the threshold of awareness is the *concept* of self." The self is very active: a violinist is aware of every finger. Investing all psychic energy in an interaction makes one "part of a system of action greater than what the individual self had been before." This is **self-transcendence**, and afterward the self comes back "enriched by new skills and fresh achievements." The **true believer** in a mass movement does not interact with the belief system but is absorbed by it: "an order imposed rather than achieved... a crystal: strong and beautifully symmetrical, but very slow to grow."
8. **The transformation of time.** Hours pass in minutes, or a dancer's turn stretches out. Where timing is itself a skill (a surgeon moving between operations, a runner pacing), the person tracks time exactly.

The result is an **autotelic experience** (*auto*, self + *telos*, goal): an activity "done not with the expectation of some future benefit, but simply because the doing itself is the reward." An **exotelic** activity is done only for external reasons. Most activities mix the two, and exotelic ones can become autotelic. Surgeons enter the field for other reasons, and then the surgery itself becomes the reward. "Most enjoyable activities are not natural; they demand an effort that initially one is reluctant to make," so beginners often need external incentives until feedback on their skills starts to reward them. The author's office friend was forced to attend concerts from age three. At about seven, during a Mozart overture, he suddenly discerned its melodic structure: the three painful years had built the skills.

## The flow channel

Plot challenges (vertical axis) against skills (horizontal axis), each running from 0 (Low) to ∞ (High). The **Flow Channel** is the diagonal band where challenges and skills match, at every level from low to high. Above it, where challenges outrun skills, is **Anxiety**; below it, where skills outrun challenges, is **Boredom**. Alex learning tennis shows how a person moves through it:

- **A1** (in the channel, low on both axes): Alex has almost no skill and one challenge, getting the ball over the net. He is in flow.
- **A2** (straight right of A1, in Boredom): his skills improve while the challenge stays the same, and he becomes **bored**.
- **A3** (straight above A1, in Anxiety): with the same skills he meets a more practiced opponent and becomes **anxious**.
- From boredom there is "essentially only one choice: to increase the challenges" (A2 up to A4). From anxiety the way out is to increase skills (A3 across to A4). Lowering the challenge, back to A1, works in theory, "but in practice it is difficult to ignore challenges once one is aware that they exist."
- **A4** (in the channel, higher on both axes): Alex is back in flow at a higher level. A4 is as enjoyable as A1 but more *complex*, and it is not stable either.

"One cannot enjoy doing the same thing at the same level for long. We grow either bored or frustrated; and then the desire to enjoy ourselves again pushes us to stretch our skills, or to discover new opportunities for using them." This is why flow is the engine of growth: "Enjoyment seems to be the mechanism that natural selection has provided to ensure that we will evolve and become more complex." Enjoyment appears "at the boundary between boredom and anxiety." Hussar, the author's hunting dog, kept their chase game at constant difficulty: he ran tighter circles when the author was tired and wider ones when he was fresh.

As measured in the book's research, flow means challenges and skills that are balanced *and above the person's own average*. When both are below average the state is **apathy**: passive, weak, dull, dissatisfied. Anxiety is rarer in daily life than boredom, but it feels much worse.

**The mechanistic fallacy** is expecting a flow activity to produce flow automatically. "It is not only the “real” challenges presented by the situation that count, but those that the person is aware of. It is not skills we actually have that determine how we feel, but the ones we think we have." A professional football player can be bored on the field, while a parent finds flow in raising children.

**Flow activities** (play, art, ritual, sport) are designed to make flow easy. "They have rules that require the learning of skills, they set up goals, they provide feedback, they make control possible," and they set themselves apart from "paramount reality" with uniforms and special places. Roger Caillois sorted them into four kinds:

- *agon*: competition, from *con petire*, "to seek together."
- *alea*: chance.
- *ilinx*: vertigo, altering consciousness by scrambling perception. But "consciousness cannot be expanded; all we can do is shuffle its content."
- *mimicry*: alternative realities, as in dance, theater and the arts.

Each of them "transformed the self by making it more complex."

## Diagnosing why someone cannot find flow

**In the person's attention:**

- **Too fluid and erratic**: attentional disorders and stimulus overinclusion. The extreme is schizophrenic *anhedonia*: "I am attending to everything at once and as a result I do not really attend to anything."
- **Too rigid and tight**: **excessive self-consciousness**, constant worry about how one looks to others, or **self-centeredness**, which values everything only for its use to one's current desires ("a self-centered self cannot become more complex"). Both lack the attentional fluidity needed to relate to activities for their own sake.
- Frequent-flow subjects *reduce* cortical activation when they concentrate, shutting down every channel but the one in use. Flexible, autonomous attention may be the neurological basis of the autotelic personality.

**In the social setting:**

- **Anomie** (no clear rules, so it is unclear what is worth investing psychic energy in) corresponds to anxiety.
- **Alienation** (being made to act against one's goals) corresponds to boredom.

**In the culture of reward:**

- **Socialization** makes people respond predictably to rewards and punishments, and the "thoroughly socialized person" wants only what others agreed he should. Pleasure-seeking genes serve the species, not the person.
- Wealth, status and power are *symbols* of happiness that distract from experience itself. In the paradox of rising expectations, the problem arises "when people are so fixated on what they want to achieve that they cease to derive pleasure from the present."
- The remedy is **autonomy**: "learn how to substitute for them rewards that are under one's own powers." "The most important step in emancipating oneself from social controls is the ability to find rewards in the events of each moment."
- Piecemeal fixes (a diet, a club, more possessions) leave the person back where they started.

## The autotelic personality

The **autotelic self** "easily translates potential threats into enjoyable challenges, and therefore maintains its inner harmony": "never bored, seldom anxious, involved with what goes on, and in flow most of the time." Its primary goals "emerge from experience evaluated in consciousness, and therefore from the self proper." Its key trait is **nonself-conscious individualism**, "a strongly directed purpose that is not self-seeking." Such a person does their best in every circumstance and keeps enough free attention to see the situation objectively. Narcissists, whose attention turns inward, fall apart. Bertrand Russell gave the best short recipe: "Gradually I learned to be indifferent to myself and my deficiencies; I came to center my attention increasingly upon external objects." It is partly a gift of biology and upbringing, but it is also "a skill one can perfect through training and discipline."

## Applying the book

1. **Locate the experience.** Find which state the person is in, at the moments that matter: flow, anxiety, boredom or apathy. Ask whose goals the activity serves, theirs or someone else's, and whether it is autotelic or exotelic. Check whether their attention is too fluid or too rigid. Done when you can name the state, the goals and the main obstacle.
2. **Restructure the activity.** The book's procedure for turning a physical act into flow applies to any activity:
   - (a) "set an overall goal, and as many subgoals as are realistically feasible"
   - (b) "find ways of measuring progress in terms of the goals chosen"
   - (c) "keep concentrating on what one is doing, and... keep making finer and finer distinctions in the challenges involved"
   - (d) "develop the skills necessary to interact with the opportunities available"
   - (e) "keep raising the stakes if the activity becomes boring"

   Even mowing the lawn or waiting at the dentist can be enjoyable "provided one restructures the activity by providing goals, rules, and the other elements of enjoyment." Heinz Maier-Leibnitz, a physicist, sat through dull lectures tapping a finger sequence with 888 combinations, which took exactly 12 minutes, and used it to time his thoughts. Such **microflow** (doodling, humming, private rituals) gets people through dull stretches but adds little complexity. Point the person toward harder challenges. Done when the activity has goals, a source of feedback, and a challenge set just above current skill.
3. **Build the person's side** with the four rules for an autotelic self:
   1. **Set goals.** Make choices from lifelong to trivial "without much fuss and the minimum of panic." Goals and challenges imply each other, and together they define a *system of action* and the skills it needs. Watch feedback constantly. Because the person knows *she* chose the goal, she is "both more consistent and more flexible": committed to it, yet free to change it when its reasons stop making sense.
   2. **Become immersed in the activity.** Find "a relatively close mesh between the demands of the environment and one's capacity to act." Avoid unrealistic goals, like saving the world or becoming a millionaire before twenty, and avoid "the safety of trivial goals." At a party, the non-autotelic person hides in a corner or performs. The autotelic person shifts attention "away from himself to the party," to "the “action system” he wishes to join": they watch the guests, guess at shared interests, open a topic, and change topic or partner when the feedback is bad. "To be distracted against one's will is the surest sign that one is not in control."
   3. **Pay attention to what is happening.** Self-consciousness is the most common distraction. The elements reinforce one another, so "one can start anywhere": goals, skills, concentration or dropping self-consciousness. Viewing the world "exclusively from the little window that one's self affords is always limiting." The book's example is a personal-injury lawyer at the unveiling of a Picasso sculpture, estimating the city's payouts to children who will climb on it.
   4. **Learn to enjoy immediate experience.** "Literally anything that happens can be a source of joy": a breeze, a cloud reflected in a glass tower, a child playing with a puppy. This takes "determination and discipline." A relaxed, "laissez-faire attitude is not a sufficient defense against chaos."
4. **Open the file for the domain** (below). Done when the person has concrete goals, feedback and next challenges for their situation, in that domain's terms.
5. **Check the use of flow.** Flow "is not “good” in an absolute sense." It can become addictive: "instead of being a conscious choice, it becomes a necessity that interferes with other activities." The player who can attend to nothing but his game "loses the ultimate control: the freedom to determine the content of consciousness." Crime, cruelty and war also produce flow for people denied more complex challenges. Democritus's answer was "learning to swim": tell the useful forms of flow from the harmful ones, make the most of the first, and limit the second. The task is "to enjoy everyday life without diminishing other people's chances to enjoy theirs."
