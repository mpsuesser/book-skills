# Cheating chaos: flow in adversity

"Subjective experience is not just one of the dimensions of life, it *is* life itself. Material conditions are secondary: they only affect us indirectly, by way of experience." Health and wealth are therefore no prerequisite for flow, and "a person who knows how to find flow from life is able to enjoy even situations that seem to allow only despair." What decides the outcome is whether a person "sees challenges as threats or as opportunities for action."

## Tragedies transformed

- **Paraplegics** often name their accident as both the most negative *and* the most positive event of their lives. It "presented the victim with very clear goals while reducing contradictory and inessential choices." Lucio was a purposeless gas-station attendant before his motorcycle accident. Afterward he earned a degree in languages, worked as a tax consultant and became a regional archery champion from his wheelchair: "Everybody must have a purpose." Franco, an electrician and acrobatic dancer, now counsels other paraplegics.
- **The blind**: Pilar's blindness freed her from a violent family. As a telephone operator she felt the calls "meshing like the instruments of an orchestra." Paolo listed four outcomes of losing his sight: accept limitations but keep trying to overcome them; always try to change situations I don't like; don't repeat mistakes; have no illusions, and be tolerant with myself so I can be tolerant with others. Dino, 85 and blind from birth, restores cane chairs: "so great when the 'pull' is right."
- **Reyad**, a homeless Egyptian sleeping in Milan's parks, called his whole wandering "search for identity" since 1967 a flow experience: "I have tried first to conquer myself; I don't care if I lose the world."

## The people of flow

Richard Logan studied survivors of polar isolation and prison camps. They turned bleak conditions into controllable experience by following **the blueprint of flow activities**:

1. They paid close attention to the smallest details of their environment, finding hidden opportunities for action that matched the little they could do.
2. They set goals that fit their precarious situation.
3. They monitored their progress closely through feedback.
4. When a goal was reached, they "upped the ante, setting increasingly complex challenges."

Examples:

- Christopher Burney, in Nazi solitary confinement, questioned each of his few objects: "Does it work? How? Who made it and of what?... what else does it remind me of?" He measured his bed, blankets and window.
- Eva Zeisel, imprisoned in Moscow's Lubyanka, worked out how to make a bra, played chess against herself, held imaginary conversations in French and composed poems in memory.
- A prisoner mapped the world on his cell floor and traveled a few kilometers across it each day. Albert Speer, in Spandau, walked an imaginary route from Berlin to Jerusalem.
- An American pilot held prisoner in North Vietnam lost 80 pounds. He played a mental round of 18 holes every day, choosing clubs and varying the course, and played superb golf on his release.
- Tollas Tibor's fellow inmates in a Hungarian jail held a poetry-translation contest on soap-coated shoe soles carved with a toothpick.

"When adversity threatens to paralyze us, we need to reassert control by finding a new direction in which to invest psychic energy, a direction that lies outside the reach of external forces." Solzhenitsyn: "For me there was no barbed wire... I was actually away on a distant flight."

## Coping with stress

A catastrophe that blocks a central goal either destroys the self or will "provide a new, more clear, and more urgent goal: to overcome the challenges created by the defeat." There are three resources against stress:

1. **External support**, especially a social network.
2. **Psychological resources**: intelligence, education, personality.
3. **Coping strategies**. These are the most important resource, because external supports "tend to help only those who can help themselves," and psychological resources are mostly fixed. Coping is "the most flexible resource, the one most under our personal control."

There are two styles of response. **Mature defense**, or **transformational coping**, is the positive one. **Neurotic defense**, or **regressive coping**, is the negative one. Take Jim, fired at forty. The regressive response is to withdraw, sleep late, deny the problem, avoid thinking, turn on family and friends, and drink more. The transformational response is to "keep his cool by suppressing temporarily his feelings of anger and fear, analyzing the problem logically, and reassessing his priorities... redefine what the problem is, so that he can solve it more easily," for instance by moving to where his skills are in demand, or by retraining. Most people do some of both (get drunk the first night, then plan). Some give up and scale down their complexity for good.

**Dissipative structures** (Ilya Prigogine's term) are systems that harness energy which would otherwise be lost to random motion, the way plants feed on sunlight. "The integrity of the self depends on the ability to take neutral or destructive events and turn them into positive ones. Getting fired could be a godsend." Courage, resilience, perseverance, mature defense and transformational coping are "the dissipative structures of the mind." Salvatore Maddi and Suzanne Kobasa call it *hardiness*: transforming threats into manageable challenges through "commitment to one's goals, a sense of being in control, and enjoyment of challenges." No trait is more useful than "the ability to transform adversity into an enjoyable challenge," which is why people admire courage above everything else. This skill develops in adolescence. On average a healthy teenager stays depressed about half an hour and an adult twice as long, and by seventeen or eighteen most can put setbacks in perspective. Coping peaks with "a strong enough sense of self, based on personally selected goals, that no external disappointment can entirely undermine who he or she is."

## The three steps for transforming a hopeless situation

"Those who know how to transform a hopeless situation into a new flow activity that can be controlled will be able to enjoy themselves, and emerge stronger from the ordeal."

1. **Unselfconscious self-assurance.** Survivors believe their fate is in their own hands, yet their egos are "curiously absent." Their energy is "not bent on dominating their environment as much as on finding a way to function within it harmoniously." This humility, "the recognition that one's goals may have to be subordinated to a greater entity, and that to succeed one may have to play by a different set of rules from what one would prefer", "is a hallmark of strong people."
   - *The car that won't start*: the ego-involved driver curses, turns the key frantically and slams the dashboard. But "it makes no difference to the car that you have to be downtown in a hurry. The car follows its own laws." Call a cab, or choose another goal.
   - *The pilot* in a storm succeeds "not because she will force the plane to obey her will, but because she will be the instrument for matching the properties of the plane to the conditions of the air." "One must trust oneself, one's environment, and one's place in it."
2. **Focusing attention on the world.** People who transform stress "spend very little time thinking about themselves." Their attention is set by a goal but is "open enough to notice and adapt to external events." This works in two ways. "To experience psychic entropy one must concentrate on the internal disorder," so attention turned outward makes frustration less disruptive. And attention immersed in the system learns its properties: the driver absorbed in being late misses what the car is telling him (a flooded engine, a dead battery). Yvon Chouinard, a day up El Capitan, watched a tiny red bug for fifteen minutes: "How could one ever be bored with so many good things to see and feel!" The opposite case: during Korean War training, a right-handed soldier was given a left-handed parachute. He tore at his uniform and gouged his own chest searching for the ripcord where he expected it, while the real one hung inches to the left: "fear was so intense that it blinded him to the fact that safety was literally at his fingertips." Drawing energy inward to defend against threat "reduces the flexibility of response... isolates a person."
3. **The discovery of new solutions.** There are two ways to deal with a blocked goal. One is to attack the obstacle directly. The other is to "focus on the entire situation, including oneself, to discover whether alternative goals may not be more appropriate." Phil was passed over for vice-president. He could try to change the CEO's mind, or move divisions, change careers, scale back, or invest in his family, his community or himself. Neither path is better in itself; the right one depends on his overall goals and on what maximizes enjoyment. If Phil takes his own needs too seriously, he has no attention left to see the options. Work like the **original artist**, who "commences with a deeply felt but undefined goal in mind, keeps modifying the picture in response to the unexpected colors and shapes emerging." The conventional artist paints the picture already in his head, and the result is trite. Judge events by how they actually feel, not by preconceived goals, and one may discover that "it is more satisfying to help another person than to beat him down, or that it is more enjoyable to talk with one's two-year-old than to play golf with the company president." "If one operates with unselfconscious assurance, and remains open to the environment and involved in it, a solution is likely to emerge."
