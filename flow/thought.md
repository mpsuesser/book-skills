# The flow of thought

**Symbolic** activities order the mind directly, through languages, mathematics, notation and history. "A symbolic system is like a game in that it provides a separate reality," and it runs on the same conditions as flow: skill in the domain, rules, a goal, feedback, and challenge matched to skill. "Every mental operation is able to provide its own particular form of enjoyment."

## The normal state of the mind

"Contrary to what we tend to assume, the normal state of the mind is chaos. Without training, and without an object in the external world that demands attention, people are unable to focus their thoughts for more than a few minutes at a time." Left with nothing to do, the mind drifts to "real or imaginary pain, on recent grudges or long-term frustrations. Entropy is the normal state of consciousness."

- **Television** fills the mind cheaply and structures attention at very low cost in psychic energy. That is why the habit is so hard to break.
- The better route is "habits that give control over mental processes to the individual, rather than to some external source of stimulation."
- **Daydreaming** (Jerome Singer's research) is a skill many children never learn. Used well, it creates emotional order (picturing an aggressor punished), rehearses situations, weighs alternatives, and turns up unexpected consequences.
- Once a person knows a symbolic system well enough to use it, "she has established a portable, self-contained world within the mind." When the outside world shows no mercy, such a system "can become a salvation": in extreme deprivation "poets, mathematicians, musicians, historians, and biblical experts have stood out as islands of sanity."
- "People without an internalized symbolic system can all too easily become captives of the media. They are easily manipulated by demagogues, pacified by entertainers, and exploited by anyone who has something to sell."

## Memory

"Remembering is enjoyable because it entails fulfilling a goal and so brings order to consciousness." A mind that holds stories, poems, lyrics, statistics, formulas or quotations is "independent of the order that may or may not be provided by the environment." The author's grandfather was still reciting the *Iliad* in Greek at seventy. "A mind with some stable content to it is much richer than one without," and rote learning does not conflict with creativity.

To build it:

1. Decide which subject really interests you: poetry, cuisine, the Civil War, baseball.
2. Pay attention to the key facts in that area. Grasping the subject tells you what is worth remembering.
3. Choose what *you* want to hold, so the information stays under your control: "you should not feel that you *have to* absorb a string of facts." A Civil War buff who cares about artillery needs only the battles where cannons mattered.
4. Carry chosen poems or quotations on a slip of paper to glance at when bored or dispirited.

Memorizing to impress others produces a bore. Memorizing to control one's own consciousness does not.

## Words

- **Crosswords** offer graduated challenges cheaply, but solving keeps the solver dependent on someone else's puzzle. "To be really autonomous in this domain, a better alternative is to make up one's own crosswords." Moving from consumer to producer is the general route to autonomy in every symbolic domain.
- **Conversation**: "the main function of conversation is not to get things accomplished, but to improve the quality of experience." Even "Nice day" holds the shared world in order. It recognizes the other person, reaffirms a rule and implies a shared value. Fluency and vocabulary can be learned.
- **Wordplay with children**: whenever a word can be read another way, "one switches frames, and pretends to understand the word in that different sense": "having Grandma for dinner," "a frog in the throat." Children catch on and learn to enjoy controlling words.
- **Poetry**: "Reading from a book of poems each night is to the mind as working out on a Nautilus is to the body." The poems need not be great; look for "at least a line, or a verse, that starts to sing." Kenneth Koch taught ghetto children and semiliterate elderly women to write moving poems.
- **Writing**: "If the only point to writing were to *transmit* information, then it would deserve to become obsolete. But the point of writing is to *create* information." It is "never a waste to write for intrinsic reasons." Journals and letters discipline expression, preserve experience, and are "a self-communication that brings order to them." Writing helps when it controls experience, and harms when it becomes an addiction that controls the mind.

## History

Everyone is the historian of their own life. Erik Erikson's last life stage, **integrity**, means bringing one's accomplishments and failures into a meaningful story claimed as one's own. "The past can never be literally true in memory: it must be continuously edited, and the question is only whether we take creative control of the editing or not." The levels of history as a flow activity are:

1. Keep a journal.
2. Write a family chronicle, going back as far as possible.
3. Study one's ethnic group: collect books and memorabilia, and record impressions. This makes a *real* amateur historian.
4. Study the community (neighborhood or state), or one aspect of it, like the friend who documented early industrial architecture: sawmills, foundries, railway depots.

"Knowledge that is seen to be controlled from the outside is acquired with reluctance, and it brings no joy." The person who picks the compelling parts of the past and records findings "in a personal style" turns history into flow.

## Science

"Philosophy and science were invented and flourished because thinking is pleasurable." In Thomas Kuhn's account, normal science is puzzle-solving within a paradigm's rules, driven by the "conviction that, if only he is skillful enough, he will succeed in solving a puzzle that no one before has solved or solved so well." Dirac called it "a game, a very interesting game one could play." Many great scientists were amateurs or hobbyists in their field: Copernicus was a canon, Mendel a clergyman, Lavoisier a tax official, Einstein a patent clerk. The mental framework of science is open to anyone: "curiosity, careful observation, a disciplined way of recording events, and finding ways to tease out the underlying regularities," plus humility toward past work and enough skepticism to reject unsupported beliefs. Amateur astronomers, rock collectors, plant breeders and tinkerers all qualify. "There is no better reason for doing science than the sense of order it brings to the mind of the seeker."

## Philosophy

The amateur philosopher's steps:

1. Decide which basic questions interest you (being? the good? the beautiful?).
2. Learn what others have thought about them, selectively, until you know the state of the art. "The importance of personally taking control of the direction of learning from the very first steps cannot be stressed enough."
3. Specialize in ontology, ethics or aesthetics. "Specialization is for the sake of thinking better, and not an end in itself."
4. Move from passive consumer to active producer. The aim is not posterity but answering "an inner challenge to express clearly the major questions by which one feels confronted."

## Amateurs and lifelong learning

**Amateur** comes from *amare*, "to love," and **dilettante** from *delectare*, "to find delight in." Both words once pointed "to experiences rather than accomplishments." They became insults when culture began to value behavior over subjective states. "To be a dilettante is to achieve what counts most—the enjoyment one's actions provide." The point is not to compete with professionals "but to use a symbolic discipline to extend mental skills, and to create order in consciousness." An amateur who uses knowledge to inflate the ego, and who lacks the discipline of skepticism, turns into a caricature. Nazi racial theory and Lysenko's biology were the result when ideology replaced science's rules.

"A person who forgoes the use of his symbolic skills is never really free. His thinking will be directed by the opinions of his neighbors, by the editorials in the papers, and by the appeals of television." "Ideally, the end of extrinsically applied education should be the start of an education that is motivated intrinsically."
