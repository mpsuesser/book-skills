# The making of meaning

Flow in separate activities does not add up to a meaningful life. Tennis players can be morose off the court, Picasso was unpleasant away from the canvas, and Bobby Fischer was inept at everything but chess. Careers dry up, spouses die and children leave. The last task is "transforming the entirety of life into a single flow activity, with unified goals that provide constant purpose."

**Unified flow**: "If a person sets out to achieve a difficult enough goal, from which all other goals logically follow, and if he or she invests all energy in developing skills to reach that goal, then actions and feelings will be in harmony, and the separate parts of life will fit together." Life has no built-in supreme goal, but "it does not follow that life cannot be *given* meaning," any more "than the fact that we lack wings prevents us from flying." "It does not matter what the ultimate goal is—provided it is compelling enough to order a lifetime's worth of psychic energy": the best beer-bottle collection, a cure for cancer, children who survive and prosper. "As long as it provides clear objectives, clear rules for action, and a way to concentrate and become involved, any goal can serve."

## Purpose, resolution, harmony

The word *meaning* has three senses, and they map onto three requirements:

1. **Purpose** (meaning as end or significance). Find a goal challenging enough to take up all one's energies, so that the separate flow activities "merge into an all-encompassing set of challenges that gives purpose to everything." Such a purpose "like a magnetic field attracts their psychic energy, a goal upon which all lesser goals depend." "The meaning of life *is* meaning: whatever it is, wherever it comes from, a unified purpose is what gives meaning to life." Napoleon and Mother Teresa may have had equal inner order, but their consequences for others differed: one spread chaos, the other reduced entropy.
2. **Resolution** (meaning as intention). "Intent has to be translated into action." "What counts is not so much whether a person actually achieves what she has set out to do; rather, it matters whether effort has been expended to reach the goal, instead of being diffused or wasted." Hamlet's "native hue of resolution" was "sicklied o'er with the pale cast of thought". Blake: "He who desires but acts not... breeds pestilence."
3. **Harmony** (meaning as the ordering of information). When an important goal is pursued with resolution, feelings, thoughts and actions become congruent, and psychic energy is "not being wasted on doubt, regret, guilt, and fear."

"Purpose, resolution, and harmony unify life and give it meaning by transforming it into a seamless flow experience."

## Cultivating purpose

- Hannah Arendt: the Greeks sought **immortality** through heroic deeds that would be remembered, and the Christians sought **eternity** through saintly deeds that surrendered the self to God. Both turned a life into a unified flow that others could model theirs on.
- Pitrim Sorokin's three alternating kinds of culture:
  - **Sensate** cultures are organized around satisfying the senses, judge by pleasure and practicality, and "identify the good with what feels good." Their rules are understood by all and their feedback is clear (health, money, power, sex).
  - **Ideational** cultures scorn the tangible and pursue supernatural ends through asceticism and abstract principle. Failure is nearly impossible to prove there, because the true believer distorts the feedback.
  - **Idealistic** cultures combine sensory experience with reverence for spiritual ends. This is "the most satisfying way to unify life," though it is hard to sustain inside a sensate culture.
  - A person can be judged by the complexity of their goals as well as by their content: "how differentiated and integrated" those goals are. "A well-thought-out sensate approach... would be preferable to an unreflective idealism, and vice versa."
- **The four stages of meaning**, which alternate between focus on the self and focus on others:
  1. Preserving the self: survival, comfort and pleasure.
  2. Adopting the values of a community (family, neighborhood, religious or ethnic group). This is more complex, but it tends toward conformity.
  3. **Reflective individualism**: authority moves inside, and the goal is growth and the actualization of potential. "Enjoyment, rather than pleasure, becomes the main source of rewards." This stage can bring a midlife crisis or a change of career.
  4. Integration with others and with universal values. "Having discovered what one can and, more important, cannot do alone, the ultimate goal merges with a system larger than the person—a cause, an idea, a transcendental entity."

  Most people stop at stage 2, and "precious few" reach stage 4. What counts is the alternation between **differentiation** and **integration**: life is a series of games that change with maturity. Invest both in one's own skills and autonomy and in understanding the forces beyond the self.

## Forging resolve

A person who changes goals whenever opposition appears may have a comfortable life, but "it is likely that it will end up empty and void of meaning." The Pilgrims, the Jews of Masada and the Christian martyrs acted "*as if* their values were worth giving up comfort, and even life itself, for. And because they acted thus, their goals in fact *became* worthwhile." "No goal can have much effect unless taken seriously." **"Goals justify the effort they demand at the outset, but later it is the effort that justifies the goal."** A marriage loses its value unless one behaves as if the spouse is worth it.

Today's wealth of options produces "uncertainty of purpose; uncertainty, in turn, saps resolution, and lack of resolve ends up devaluing choice." "There is no one *out there* to tell us" which goal is worth a life. Each person sorts it out by trial and error, and the ancient remedy is **self-knowledge**. Inner conflict is competing claims on attention. Reduce it by separating essential claims from inessential ones, then setting priorities among the rest. There are two paths:

- **Vita activa**, the life of action: total involvement in concrete external challenges, pursuing chosen goals "with such intensity that all potential competition is preempted." Its risk is restricted options. The engineer bent on being plant manager by forty-five finds that "postponed alternatives may reappear again as intolerable doubts and regrets": his health, his sullen adolescents, the question of what to do with power once he has it.
- **Vita contemplativa**, the path of reflection: detached weighing of options and their consequences, as on the psychoanalyst's couch or in the Jesuits' test of conscience, which reviews one's actions one or more times a day against long-term goals.

"Action by itself is blind, reflection impotent." Before investing great energy in a goal, ask:

- Is this something I really want to do?
- Is it something I enjoy doing?
- Am I likely to enjoy it in the foreseeable future?
- Is the price that I—and others—will have to pay worth it?
- Will I be able to live with myself if I accomplish it?

People who have lost touch with their own experience cannot answer these questions. With a well-developed habit of reflection, a person knows "almost intuitively" that a promotion will cost more stress than it is worth, or that a friendship would strain a marriage. "If goals are well chosen, and if we have the courage to abide by them despite opposition, we shall be so focused on the actions and events around us that we won't have the time to be unhappy."

## Recovering harmony

Animals are in flow most of the time, because what they perceive needs doing matches what they are prepared to do. The psychic entropy peculiar to humans "involves seeing more to do than one can actually accomplish and feeling able to accomplish more than what conditions allow." This is the cost of a mind that knows what *could be* as well as what *is*. Children act with total abandon until self-consciousness intrudes, and "boredom is something children have to learn the hard way, in response to artificially restricted choices." The harmony of simple systems is **order by default**, and it is fragile. "The order based on innocence is now beyond our grasp." The task is harmony "based on reason and choice."

## Life themes

A **life theme** is "a set of goals linked to an ultimate goal that gives significance to whatever a person does." The philosophers' **project** and the psychologists' **propriate strivings** name the same thing. Like a game, a life theme gives everything a meaning, "not necessarily a positive one, but a meaning nevertheless." If the goal is a million dollars before thirty, every event is a step toward it or away from it.

- An **authentic** project is freely chosen from a rational evaluation of one's own experience, and is intrinsically motivated. An **inauthentic** project is chosen because one feels one ought to, or because everybody else does it.
- **Discovered** life themes are written by the person "out of personal experience and awareness of choice." **Accepted** life themes take on "a predetermined role from a script written long ago by others."
- An accepted theme works while society is sound. Adolf Eichmann probably found flow in scheduling trains to the gas chambers, an accessory to crimes "without having to change his goals." A discovered theme is fragile because it lacks social legitimacy. Malcolm X discovered his in jail, through reading and reflection.

**How discovered themes are forged.** E. was a poor immigrants' son in New York. At seven he was hit by a car that ran a stop sign, and the wealthy driver broke his promise to pay. At fourteen or fifteen, reading the Constitution and the Bill of Rights, he decided his family's misery came from not knowing their rights. He became a lawyer, then a judge, then a cabinet official working on civil rights, and his life was "part of one great game." Gottfried's mother died of cancer before he turned ten, and he became a cancer researcher. The pattern:

1. The theme is often "a reaction to a great personal hurt suffered in early life." But what matters is the **interpretation** of the suffering, not the trauma. A child of a violent alcoholic can explain him as a bastard who deserves to die, as proof that all men are weak and violent, as a product of poverty (so the answer is to get rich), or as the result of helplessness and lack of education. Only the last points to a theme like E.'s.
2. The suffering is read as "a *possible challenge*" that one can "develop appropriate skills" to meet. This is the dissipative structure that draws order from disorder (see `adversity.md`).
3. "The challenge becomes generalized to other people, or to mankind as a whole." This altruistic generalizing is typical of negentropic life themes.

If people have a choice in what their suffering means, "the constructive response [is] normal and the neurotic one... a failure to rise to the challenge, as a breakdown in the ability to flow." Antonio Gramsci was born a hunchback in a peasant hovel, so sickly that his mother laid him in a coffin each night. He became a professor, a member of parliament and a leader against fascism. Childhood conditions do not predict later lives.

**Draw on the order of the past.** The shared strategy is "extracting from the order achieved by past generations patterns that will help avoid disorder in one's own mind." Music, art, poetry, philosophy and religion are "examples of how harmony can be imposed on chaos." Building meaning from scratch is "misguided hubris," like building an electron microscope without physics. Adults with coherent life themes remember parents who told stories and read to them, or a model or a book that opened new possibilities. One boy read *A Tale of Two Cities* and decided to spend his life understanding why people make each other miserable. Another, in an orphanage, read Horatio Alger and thought, "If he could do it, why not me?"

**For a midlife crisis**, the author opened seminars for executives with Dante: "In the middle of the journey of our life... I found myself inside a dark forest, for the right way I had completely lost." The lion, lynx and she-wolf stand for ambition, lust and greed. Virgil brings good news and bad: there is a way out, but it leads through hell, past those who never chose a goal and those whose goal was to increase entropy. No seminar ever produced as open a discussion. Dante's own faith was *discovered*, not *accepted*: Christianity combined with Greek philosophy and Islamic wisdom, and his Inferno is full of popes. To use any belief system, "first compare the information contained in it with... concrete experience, retain what makes sense, and then reject the rest."

A faith fit for the future must "account rationally for the things we know, the things we feel, the things we hope for, and the ones we dread," and must provide "rules for a way of life that can provide flow." Humanity has advanced far in differentiation. Its next task is integration: learning "to reunite ourselves with other entities around us without losing our hard-won individuality," until "the individual's purpose merges with the universal flow."
