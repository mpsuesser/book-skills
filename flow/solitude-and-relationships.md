# Solitude and other people

The quality of life depends on two things: how we experience work, and our relations with other people. People are "the most flexible, the most changeable aspect of the environment." They are both the best and the worst part of experience. Teenagers name time with friends as the most enjoyable part of their day, and also as the most anxious and the most boring. "When they are treated as valuable in their own right, people are the most fulfilling source of happiness." Treated as tools to manipulate, they are not.

**Human relations are malleable.** When the author's son Mark, at twelve, was robbed of his watch, he went back and asked for it, explaining that it was a birthday present. The three robbers voted two to one to return it. He refused the role of victim, treated the thieves as reasonable people, and turned a holdup into "a rational democratic decision" (with some luck). "If a person has the appropriate skills their rules can be transformed."

## Solitude

The worst moods come "when one is alone and there is nothing that *needs* to be done." Sunday mornings are the low point for people who live alone, until a decision about what to do brings purpose back. "Keeping order in the mind from within is very difficult. We need external goals, external stimulation, external feedback to keep attention directed." As soon as the mind relaxes, worries about love, health, money or work come in: "*zap!*"

The common escapes keep the mind from unraveling, but they do not make it grow:

- **TV** gives predictable order and leaves people passive, weak and irritable.
- **Drugs** "*reduce* our perception of both what can be accomplished and what we as individuals are able to accomplish, until the two are in balance": "a misleading simulation" of the enjoyment that comes from *increasing* both. They shuffle consciousness and do not expand control.
- **Depersonalized sex, risk and gambling**: "Even pain is better than the chaos that seeps into an unfocused mind."

**The ultimate test** "for the ability to control the quality of experience is what a person does in solitude, with no external demands to give structure to attention." "A person who rarely gets bored, who does not constantly need a favorable external environment to enjoy the moment, has passed the test for having achieved a creative life." Talented teenagers often fail to develop their gifts because they cannot stand being alone. Equally gifted math students who stayed involved spent about one hour more a week studying than socializing. Those who dropped out spent 21 hours more socializing than studying. Without some solitude there is no discipline, and a person without a discipline never learns "how to enjoy living."

To tame solitude, "a person must build his own mental routines":

- **Dorothy**, a former nurse, lives alone through the winter on a tiny island in northern Minnesota with wolves outside. She put flowers, gnomes and doggerel signs everywhere ("She has stamped her preferences on chaos"). She also keeps a strict schedule: up by five for the hens, the goat and the wood, then washing, sewing and fishing, with reading and writing in the evening. Like the colonial Englishmen who shaved and dressed impeccably every evening in their lonely outposts, she has learned that "to keep control in an alien environment one must impose one's own order on the wilderness."
- **Susan Butcher**, a sled-dog racer, lives 25 miles from a village of 62 people with 150 huskies, knowing each one's name, ancestry and temperament, "making life a continuous flow experience."
- A solo sailor becalmed in the doldrums smeared rotten eggs on his deck so he would have something to clean off.

What matters is the stance: "If being alone is seen as a chance to accomplish goals that cannot be reached in the company of others, then instead of feeling lonely, a person will enjoy solitude." If it is a condition to avoid at all costs, the person panics into distractions. As physical vigor fades, turn toward inner reality: "finally read Proust, take up chess, grow orchids, help one's neighbors, and think about God." Take "each new challenge not as something to be repressed or avoided, but as an opportunity for learning and for improving skills."

## The family as a flow activity

Every relationship requires "a reorienting of attention, a repositioning of goals." The bachelor whose goals were a sports car and the Caribbean must revise them when he marries, or suffer psychic entropy. Since the self is the organization of its goals, "entering any relationship entails a transformation of the self." Families once held together for extrinsic reasons. Today the reason has to be intrinsic: the members enjoy one another.

**Commitment frees.** The rules of marriage work like a game's rules, which "exclude a wide range of possibilities so that we might concentrate fully on a selected set of options." In Cicero's words, to be completely free one must become a slave to a set of laws. A person who commits willingly is "no longer needs to worry whether she has made the right choice, or whether the grass might be greener... energy gets freed up for living, instead of being spent on wondering about how to live."

Build the family on the flow elements:

1. **Goals.** "Everybody else is married" is no reason. The family needs **long-term goals** (a home, the children's education, a religious way of life) and a steady supply of **short-term objectives**: a new sofa, a picnic, Sunday Scrabble, Rick's motocross one weekend and Erica's aquarium the next. It must be **differentiated**, with each member developing unique traits and goals, and **integrated**: "each person's goals matter to all others."
2. **Feedback**: open channels of communication, because "entropy is the basic condition of group life." Check whether shared goals are still working. The zoo trips stopped when the ten-year-old son became upset by the caged animals. When children start calling family activities "dumb," forcing them is counterproductive, and abandoning them to the peer culture is common but worse. "The more fruitful, if more difficult, strategy is to find a new set of activities."
3. **Balance of challenges and skills.** In a couple, the early challenges ("Can I make her?" "Can I catch him?", then what movies she likes) run out, and routine takes over. "The only way to restore flow to the relationship is by finding new challenges in it." Start by varying the routines of eating, sleeping and shopping, finding new topics, places and friends. "More than anything else... [pay] attention to the partner's own complexity, getting to know her at deeper levels," and support the partner through inevitable changes. With children, parents raise the challenges naturally (from cradle to playpen to playground) until adolescence, when many stop paying attention.
4. **Teenagers** are physically mature but lack adequate challenges, so they turn to vandalism, joyriding, drugs or recreational sex. Lectures do not help: "What does help are living examples and concrete opportunities." Parents who are visibly absorbed in understandable complex activities at home (music, cooking, gardening, carpentry, engines), who talk about their ideals and their work, and who treat teenagers as young adults raise children who find such activities challenging. A father slumped in front of the TV teaches that adults are boring. One study found that teenagers spent about five minutes a day alone with their fathers, half of it watching TV.
5. **Trust and unconditional acceptance.** A parent who threatens to withdraw love replaces playfulness with chronic anxiety, because the child must spend psychic energy on self-protection. "Early emotional security may well be one of the conditions that helps develop an autotelic personality." Acceptance still needs standards: "When there is no risk attached to transgressing rules they become meaningless." Set specific consequences, and never put concern in question.

Kevin Rathunde found five characteristics in the **autotelic family context**, the families that raise autotelic children:

- **Clarity**: goals and feedback are unambiguous, and children know what is expected.
- **Centering**: parents are interested in what children are doing and feeling now, not just in college and careers.
- **Choice**: there are many possibilities, including breaking the rules if one accepts the consequences.
- **Commitment**: enough trust that the child can drop their defenses and become involved.
- **Challenge**: increasingly complex opportunities for action.

Such families waste no energy arguing about rules, and their teenagers show more flow at home, alone and at school.

In summary: "When a family has a common purpose and open channels of communication, when it provides gradually expanding opportunities for action in a setting of trust, then life in it becomes an enjoyable flow activity." Many believe that home life takes care of itself, and then wake to an alcoholic spouse and children who are cold strangers. A family "needs constant investments of psychic energy." "Unconditional acceptance... is meaningful only when it is accompanied by an unstinting investment of attention."

## Friends

Friends are the easiest people to enjoy, because they are chosen for shared interests and complementary goals. People report their most positive moods with friends, more than with a spouse. But friendship that only props up an insecure self gives pleasure, not enjoyment. **Drinking buddies** in a tavern are "rather like a collective form of television watching": rigidly scripted, a daily fix of superficial contact.

Popularity can be approached as a flow activity. **Christopher**, a shy fifteen-year-old, set out to become popular with "detached discipline". He got contact lenses, new clothes, the latest music and a practiced laid-back manner. He "chose realistic challenges he could master on his own" and "transformed the daunting, vague monster of popularity into a feasible flow activity," while his inner self stayed the same.

**Intimate friendship** needs common goals, reciprocal feedback and "new challenges in each other's company": learning more about the friend and revealing more of oneself. It takes "concentrated attention, openness, and sensitivity."

**Instrumental skills** cope with the environment (craft, professional knowledge). **Expressive skills** externalize subjective experience: a song, a dance, a joke. "When involved in an expressive activity we feel in touch with our real self," and a person with only instrumental skills is "indistinguishable from a robot." "Friendship is not enjoyable unless we take up its expressive challenges." Friends who only reaffirm a person's public persona, who "never question his dreams and desires, who never force him to try out new ways of being", waste the chance. "A true friend is someone we can occasionally be crazy with, someone who does not expect us to be always true to form... shares our goal of self-realization, and therefore is willing to share the risks that any increase in complexity entails." Families give emotional protection; friends give novelty and adventure. After youth, "one must cultivate them as assiduously as one must cultivate a job or a family."

## Community, institutions and cultures

"The larger the social arena one moves in, the greater the challenges." Seeking power, fame or righteousness is easy. The harder challenge is to benefit oneself while helping others, and politicians who work for the common good are "actually smarter, because they are taking on the higher challenges." "Any involvement in the public realm can be enjoyable, provided one structures it according to the flow parameters." That applies to the Cub Scouts, a Great Books group, an environmental cause or a union: "set a goal... concentrate one's psychic energy... pay attention to the feedback, and... make certain that the challenge is appropriate to one's skill. Sooner or later the interaction will begin to hum."

To judge or reform an institution, remember that "the true bottom line consists in the quality and complexity of experience":

- A community is good "if it offers people a chance to enjoy as many aspects of their lives as possible, while allowing them to develop their potential in the pursuit of ever greater challenges."
- A school is worth "the degree of the enjoyment of lifelong learning it can transmit."
- A good factory is one that most improves the quality of life of its workers and customers.
- Politics exists "to let as many as possible enjoy an increasingly complex existence."

A culture can be judged by the same standard, relative to its members' own goals. It is better when more people "have access to experiences that are in line with their goals," and when those experiences lead to the growth of the self, letting as many people as possible develop increasingly complex skills.

- **Cultures** are "defensive constructions against chaos." Like games, they channel attention to a limited set of goals and means, and that "allows effortless action within self-created boundaries."
- A culture can become a **"great game"**, with goals and rules so compelling and so well matched to its members' skills that flow becomes frequent. Athenian citizens and the Roman ideal of *virtus* are the book's examples.
- Cultures fail "either by making survival too strenuous a task, or by closing themselves off into rigid patterns that stifle the opportunities for action." The Shushwap of British Columbia, when life grew too predictable ("Without challenge, life had no meaning"), moved the whole village every 25 to 30 years.
- A flow culture is not necessarily a moral one. Sparta and Nazi Germany "set simple goals, clarified feedback, and allowed a renewed involvement with life." Flow "is a powerful motivator, it does not guarantee virtue."
- Leisure is not enjoyment. Americans have abundant free time but spend much of it watching TV, which rarely produces flow. "Potentiality does not imply actuality, and quantity does not translate into quality."

Carlyle's advice applies to each person: "Reform yourself. That way there will be one less rascal in the world."
