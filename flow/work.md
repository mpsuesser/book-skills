# Work as flow

Work can be the most entropic experience there is: "few things as entropic as unskilled work done under compulsion." It can also be "the most enjoyable part of life." Each day, a brain surgeon learns that he is in control, and a slave laborer learns "about his own helplessness."

## Autotelic workers

- **Serafina Vinon**, 76, a farmer in the Italian Alps, rises at five to milk the cows and then tends the herd, the orchard and the wool. She knows every tree and boulder and the family legends tied to them. With all the time and money in the world, she would do the same things. Her village's oldest residents draw no line between work and free time, and none would work less. Their grandchildren would, and that widens "the gap between jobs that are necessary but unpleasant, and leisure pursuits that are enjoyable but have little complexity."
- **Joe Kramer**, a welder in a deafening South Chicago railroad-car plant, left school after fourth grade and refused promotion to foreman. He learned every job in the plant and could repair anything from cranes to electronic monitors. His method was **empathic identification**: "If I were that toaster and I didn't work, what would be wrong with me?" At home he built a rock garden and lathe-turned sprinkler heads that made rainbows, then added floodlights so the rainbows showed at night. His fellow welders spent their evenings in saloons and in front of the TV. Joe has the autotelic personality, "the ability to create flow experiences even in the most barren environment."
- **Cook Ting** (Chuang Tzu) carved an ox for Lord Wen-hui "as though he were performing the dance of the Mulberry Grove." His method, in Watson's translation: "whenever I come to a complicated place, I size up the difficulties, tell myself to watch out and be careful, keep my eyes on what I'm doing, work very slowly, and move my knife with the greatest of subtlety, until—flop! the whole thing comes apart." The Taoist **Yu**, right living translated as "wandering," "flying" or "*flowing*," still "depends on the discovery of new challenges... and on the development of new skills." It is reached by "the gradual focusing of attention on the opportunities for action in one's environment." On this point East and West meet.

Autotelic workers change their jobs "by recognizing opportunities for action where others did not, by developing skills, by focusing on the activity at hand, and allowing themselves to be lost in the interaction so that their selves could emerge stronger afterward." The work then "feels as if it were freely chosen." Answer the charge of elitism directly: once enjoyment is seen to depend on growing complexity, the person who transforms their surroundings does have a richer experience than the person resigned to them.

## Autotelic jobs

"The more a job inherently resembles a game—with variety, appropriate and flexible challenges, clear goals, and immediate feedback—the more enjoyable it will be regardless of the worker's level of development."

- Hunting, fishing and herding on horseback are naturally flow-like. Farming is harder, because it gives few decisions a year and long waits.
- Home weavers before industrialization set their own hours and goals, quit for the orchard in good weather, and celebrated each finished cloth. The family weavers of Biella own a few looms, rotate among themselves, design their own patterns, choose materials and markets, and travel to keep up with technology. They rate weaving above travel, discos and fishing.
- **Surgery** has crystal-clear goals; continuous feedback (no blood in the cavity); a challenge of craft ("like carpentry"), with the joy of making things work and fit; exhilarating team coordination; and room for endless refinement (how the needle is held). The operating theater works like a stage, with ritual purification and special clothing. Before big operations, some surgeons go on "automatic pilot": the same breakfast, clothes and route, to save all their attention for the operation.
- Structure does not guarantee enjoyment. Routine specialists such as tonsil surgeons get bored, and competitive "supersurgeons" burn out chasing the spectacular. The surgeons who enjoy their work choose hospitals that allow variety, experimentation, research and teaching.

The **two complementary strategies** for any workplace are: "jobs should be redesigned so that they resemble as closely as possible flow activities," and people should be helped to "develop autotelic personalities... by training them to recognize opportunities for action, to hone their skills, to set reachable goals." Neither works alone. Together they can do a great deal.

**Flow and the quality of the work.** What counts is the quality of experience, which matters more for happiness than riches or fame, so do not sell flow as a route to success. Still, "a person who experiences flow in an activity will end up with a product that others will find more valuable." In Larson's study of high school juniors writing a month-long assignment, the bored students wrote essays that teachers found boring. The anxious students wrote disconnected, confusing ones, and those who enjoyed the task wrote essays that were enjoyable to read, controlling for ability. High achievers ranked "enjoyment of work" first among the qualities behind their success. Workers who enjoyed their jobs would likely also produce more efficiently.

## The paradox of work

In the ESM study, workers were paged at random about eight times a day for a week. Flow was defined as moments when both challenges and skills were above the person's weekly mean.

| | Flow | **Apathy** (both below the mean) |
|---|---|---|
| At work | 54% | 16% |
| In leisure | 18% | 52% |

Managers were in flow at work 64% of the time, clerical workers 51% and blue-collar workers 47%. Assembly workers were in flow twice as often at work as in leisure. In flow, every kind of worker felt happier, stronger, more creative and more satisfied. Yet everyone wished more often to be somewhere else at work than in leisure.

Here is the **paradox**: "when it comes to work, people do not heed the evidence of their senses. They disregard the quality of immediate experience, and base their motivation instead on the strongly rooted cultural stereotype of what work is *supposed* to be like." The cause is not exhaustion: the Alpine farmers work longer and still fill their free time with demanding pursuits. People discount good moments at work because they feel the time is "subtracted" from their own goals and spent on someone else's. Help the person notice what they actually experience, and connect the job to goals of their own.

## The three complaints

Salary is rarely the most pressing complaint. Each of the three main ones can be addressed subjectively:

1. **Lack of variety and challenge.** "Whether a job has variety or not ultimately depends more on a person's approach to it than on actual working conditions." Look at Ting, Serafina and Joe.
2. **Conflict with other people, especially bosses.** It usually comes from defensiveness and the fear of losing face, when each side holds rigid expectations of how the other should behave. The fix is to "set the challenge of reaching one's goals while helping the boss and colleagues reach theirs; it is less direct and more time-consuming... but in the long run it seldom fails."
3. **Burnout**: too much pressure, no time to think, no time for family. "Stress exists only if we experience it": the same pressure crushes one person and challenges another. Better organization, delegation and communication help, and so do home life, leisure and inner disciplines. "The only real answer to coping with work stress is to consider it part of a general strategy to improve the overall quality of experience." Stress in general is covered in `adversity.md`.

## The waste of free time

"Jobs are actually easier to enjoy than free time, because like flow activities they have built-in goals, feedback, rules, and challenges... Free time, on the other hand, is unstructured, and requires much greater effort to be shaped into something that can be enjoyed." Leisure becomes *re-creation* through hobbies that demand skill, habits that set goals and limits, personal interests and inner discipline.

People watch athletes instead of playing, listen to records instead of making music, and admire auctioned paintings instead of painting. "The flow experience that results from the use of skills leads to growth; passive entertainment leads nowhere." "Mass leisure, mass culture, and even high culture when only attended to passively and for extrinsic reasons—such as the wish to flaunt one's status—are **parasites of the mind**. They absorb psychic energy without providing substantive strength in return." In the ESM data, working people found flow on the job about four times as often, proportionately, as when watching television. "Unless a person takes charge of them, both work and free time are likely to be disappointing."
