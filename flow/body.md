# The body in flow

"Everything the body can do is potentially enjoyable," and controlling the body and senses is "the easiest step toward improving the quality of life." Untrained senses deliver chaos: a clumsy body, an insensitive eye, an unmusical ear, a coarse palate. Training turns that entropy into "enjoyable harmony." The body does not produce flow by movement alone, because "muscles and brain must be equally involved." Without skills, "the body remains indeed a lump of rather inexpensive flesh." Nobody can master many physical domains, but anyone can be a **dilettante**, "in the finest sense of that word," in all of them.

The general procedure is steps (a) to (e) in `SKILL.md`. Below is how the book applies it in each domain.

## Sport, exercise and walking

- *Altius, citius, fortius*: "Every person, no matter how unfit... can rise a little higher, go a little faster, and grow to be a little stronger." Sport's rationale is "to do something better than it has ever been done before." Treat the enjoyment as a sign of health. Explanations such as the "Icarus complex", which reduce it to a defense against repressed anxiety, miss the point.
- **Walking**, worked through steps (a) to (e):
  - *Goals*: the itinerary, places to stop, landmarks, a personal style, economy of motion.
  - *Feedback*: how fast and easily the distance was covered, how many interesting sights were seen, how many new ideas or feelings came up.
  - *Finer distinctions*: on a mountain trail, every step is a choice of foothold, momentum and center of gravity. In a city, there are architecture, store windows and the patterns of people; the shortest route versus the most interesting one; the same route walked with chronometric precision; the sunny side in winter and the shade in summer; crossings timed to the green lights.
  - "Unless one sets goals and develops skills, walking is just featureless drudgery."
- Exercising because one *must* (it is fashionable or healthy) puts a person on a "treadmill of physical activity" and confuses form with substance. "Enjoyment... does not depend on *what* you do, but rather on *how* you do it."
- Expensive leisure (power boating, driving, TV) leaves people significantly *less* happy than cheap leisure (talking, gardening, knitting, a hobby). Leisure that uses many material resources "often requires less attention, and... provides less memorable rewards."

## Dance and mimicry

Dance is the oldest and most significant form, and amateur dancers can have as much fun as professionals. Miming, acting and charades let people shed their usual identity and glimpse other ways of being.

## Sex and love

The same act can be painful, boring or ecstatic "depending on how it is linked to a person's goals." Unless it is turned into an enjoyable activity, "sex easily becomes boring with time... either a meaningless ritual or an addictive dependence." It has three dimensions to cultivate:

1. **Eroticism**, the physical skills. "Eroticism is to sex as sport is to physical activity."
2. **Romance**, the psychological dimension. The troubadours of southern France invented it in the late Middle Ages, and it opened a new range of challenges.
3. **Genuine care** for the partner: "to enjoy the partner as a unique person, to understand her, and to help her fulfill her goals." Care can provide flow for a whole lifetime.

"Any fool can fall in love when young." Keeping love alive takes the flow channel: "To be enjoyable, a relationship must become more complex. To become more complex, the partners must discover new potentialities in themselves and in each other. To discover these, they must invest attention in each other—so that they can learn what thoughts and feelings, what dreams reside in their partner's mind." Shared adventures follow (travel, the same books, raising children, plans), and "the specific details are unimportant." The couple and the family as a whole are covered in `solitude-and-relationships.md`.

## Yoga and the martial arts

**Patanjali's eight stages** (*Yoga* means "yoking"), read as "a very thoroughly planned flow activity":

1. *Yama*, "restraint" from harming others: falsehood, theft, lust, avarice.
2. *Niyama*, "obedience": ordered routines of cleanliness, study and obedience to God. These first two stages straighten out consciousness and channel attention into predictable patterns.
3. *Asana*, postures.
4. *Pranayama*, breath control.
5. *Pratyahara*, "withdrawal": withdrawing attention from outward objects, so that one sees and hears only what one admits.
6. *Dharana*, "holding on": concentrating for a long time on a single stimulus. This mirrors stage 5, which keeps things out where this one keeps them in.
7. *Dhyana*, meditation: forgetting the self in uninterrupted concentration without external stimuli.
8. *Samadhi*, "self-collectedness": meditator and object become one.

Yoga aims to abolish the self, where flow strengthens it. But seven of the eight stages build skill, and even surrendering the self requires control. Weigh the cost: "Is the control that Yoga makes possible worth the investment of psychic energy that learning its discipline requires?" The martial arts, shaped by Taoism and Zen, aim at a mental state in which a person acts with lightning speed without thinking. Fighting becomes a "joyous artistic performance," and the split between mind and body gives way to "one-pointedness of mind."

## Seeing

Most people use sight as a "distant sensing system", for not stepping on the cat or for finding the keys. Visual skills give constant access to enjoyment, and the visual arts are the best training. Trained eyes see a train-window view of rooftops as a precisionist painting, or call a sky "a Kodachrome sky." Without the skills "one cannot expect to take true enjoyment." Yoga trains the eyes *not* to see, but that contradiction only troubles people who think behavior matters more than experience: "It does not matter whether we see or we not-see, as long as we are in control of what is happening to us."

## Music

"Music, which is organized auditory information, helps organize the mind that attends to it, and therefore reduces psychic entropy." But "it is not the *hearing* that improves life, it is the *listening*. We hear Muzak, but we rarely listen to it." Live music can produce Durkheim's **collective effervescence**, a sense of belonging to a group that concretely exists.

People who enjoy music:

- set aside specific hours for listening
- deepen concentration with a ritual, such as dimming the lights or using a favorite chair
- plan the selection
- set specific goals for each session

Listening has three stages, and each multiplies the opportunities for enjoyment:

1. **Sensory**: pleasant physical reactions to the qualities of sound.
2. **Analogic**: feelings and images evoked by the patterns, such as a saxophone suggesting storm clouds.
3. **Analytic**: the structure and order of the work, the quality of the performance, comparisons with other works, performers and recordings. One example agenda is "how von Karajan's 1975 recording of the second movement of the Seventh Symphony differs from his 1963 recording." Each comparison gives feedback.

When making music, "too much emphasis is placed on how they perform, and too little on what they experience." Parents who push for Carnegie Hall turn music into psychic disorder. The pianist Lorin Hollander felt ecstasy playing alone and terror in front of his mentors, and his hands froze for years. It is never too late: piano after fifty, a choir, an amateur string ensemble.

## Tasting

- Cuisines grew by the flow sequence: take the opportunities (whatever is edible), attend carefully, draw finer distinctions (salt preserves, eggs bind), experiment, and form rules.
- To start: pay attention to what one eats, take up one cuisine and learn its history and peculiarities, then cook entire meals in that idiom.
- Approach food "in a spirit of adventure and curiosity... for the sake of the experience rather than as a showcase for one's expertise." Striving to be a gourmet because it is the *in* thing makes the challenge external, and the taste turns sour.
- On appetite and its dangers: "repression is not the way to virtue. When people restrain themselves out of fear, their lives are by necessity diminished. They become rigid and defensive, and their self stops growing. Only through freely chosen discipline can life be enjoyed, and still kept within the bounds of reason." Control desires "not because he *has* to, but because he *wants* to." The fanatical foodie is as dull as the ascetic.
