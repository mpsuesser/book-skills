# The mechanics of propaganda

"There is no means of human communication which may not also be a means of deliberate propaganda." The media include conversation, letters, the stage, the motion picture, the radio, the lecture platform, the magazine and the daily newspaper, and later television.

**The governing rule:** "the relative value of the various instruments of propaganda, and their relation to the masses, are constantly changing." To get full reach, the propagandist "must take advantage of these shifts of value the instant they occur." Fifty years earlier the public meeting was the instrument *par excellence*. By 1928 it was hard to draw more than a handful without extraordinary attractions: "the automobile takes them away from home, the radio keeps them in the home, the successive daily editions of the newspaper bring information to them in office or subway, and also they are sick of the ballyhoo of the rally." So before choosing channels, ask where the target groups' attention actually is now and which instruments have lost their value. Some instruments are new, and others are "old but so transformed that they have become virtually new."

## The newspaper

The newspaper "remains always a primary medium" for propaganda. Editors once resented "the use of the news columns for propaganda purposes", and some would kill a good story if it might benefit anyone. Leading editors now take news value as the criterion. "There is hardly a single item in any daily paper, the publication of which does not, or might not, profit or injure somebody. That is the nature of news." The paper strives for accuracy and for news "of interest and importance to large groups of its readers." Its editorial columns are a personality with a point of view; its news columns try to reproduce the outstanding events and opinions of the day. The editor "does not ask whether a given item is propaganda or not. What is important is that it be news," and he selects independently.

How the counsel works with it:

- "If the public relations counsel can breathe the breath of life into an idea and make it take its place among other ideas and events, [it] will receive the public attention it merits." He is not "contaminating news at its source": "He creates some of the day's events, which must compete in the editorial office with other events." He may create them with a particular newspaper's public in mind.
- Real developments are news, even when commercially advantageous to someone: transatlantic radiophone talks arranged by telephone companies, new inventions, Henry Ford's epoch-making cars.
- Material that is not news goes, "simply at the editor's discretion", to the wastebasket.
- "The source of the news offered to the editor should always be clearly stated and the facts accurately presented."

## The magazine

The magazine has no obligation to reflect current news. It "selects its material deliberately, in accordance with a continuous policy" and "tends rather to become a propagandist organ" for one idea: good housekeeping, smart apparel, beauty in home decoration, debunking, liberalism, health, English gardens, fashionable men's wear, Nietzschean philosophy. So find the magazine whose own propaganda the client's material advances, and help it "create the events which further their propaganda." A bank that wants to emphasize its women's department supplies a leading women's magazine with a series of investment articles by the woman expert who runs it, and the magazine gains prestige and circulation from the feature.

## The lecture

"The lecture itself may only be a symbol, a ceremony; its importance, for propaganda purposes, lies in the fact that it was delivered." Professor So-and-So may expound an epoch-making invention to five hundred people or fifty. If it is important, it will be broadcast, reported in the newspapers, and discussed. "The real value of the lecture ... is in its repercussion to the general public." Plan a talk for its coverage, not its hall.

## The radio

The radio is "one of the most important tools of the propagandist", with an uncertain future. It reaches millions at once and will draw advertising appropriations from newspapers. Newspapers have bought stations, and chains may sell space on the air and on paper together. Large political, racial, sectarian, economic or professional groups tend to control stations to propagandize their views, and America might adopt the English system in which the listener pays. Whatever the system, whether sold air time, straight entertainment and news, or special programs for particular groups, "the propagandist must be prepared to meet the conditions and utilize them." Radio is one instrument in a plan, not the plan: the low-tariff candidate who only argues over the radio is still using the old propaganda.

## The motion picture

"The American motion picture is the greatest unconscious carrier of propaganda in the world today." It "can standardize the ideas and habits of a nation." Because pictures are made to meet market demand, "they reflect, emphasize and even exaggerate broad popular tendencies, rather than stimulate new ideas and opinions." It uses only ideas and facts already in vogue. So it amplifies an idea once the idea is established, and does not launch one.

## The personality

The personality is an instrument too, and it can be overused. Coolidge photographed in full Indian regalia was "the climax of a greatly overreported vacation", and "a public personality can be made absurd by misuse of the very mechanism which helped create it." Yet "the vivid dramatization of personality will always remain one of the functions of the public relations counsel," because "the public instinctively demands a personality to typify a conspicuous corporation or enterprise." Private conduct counts. A great financier discharged a partner who had divorced his wife: "If you are not capable of managing your own wife, ... the people will certainly believe that you are not capable of managing their money."

Rules:

- "The propagandist must treat personality as he would treat any other objective fact within his province."
- Causation runs both ways. A personality may create circumstances, as Lindbergh created good will between the United States and Mexico, and events may create a personality, as the Cuban War created the political Roosevelt.
- "Once a public figure has decided what ends he wishes to achieve, he must regard himself objectively and present an outward picture of himself which is consistent with his real character and his aims."
- Dramatize the person in the function he represents, not the person at large (the Prince of Wales in `campaigns.md`), and keep the program above the personality (`politics.md`).

## The school and other avenues

The school may disseminate scientific facts. That a commercial concern may profit from wider understanding of its work "does not condemn the dissemination of such information, provided that the subject merits study." A baking corporation's pictures and charts showing how bread is made are unobjectionable "if they are accurate and candid", provided the school authorities accept or reject such offers "carefully on their educational merits."

Other forms are new or still coming: a new product announced by a motion picture of a parade a thousand miles away, or a manufacturer speaking "in a million homes through radio and television." "The man who would most effectively transmit his message to the public must be alert to make use of all the means of propaganda."

## The sophisticated public

"The public is becoming aware of the methods which are being used to mold its opinions and habits." A better-informed public is "so much the more receptive to reasonable appeals to its own interests." "No matter how sophisticated, how cynical the public may become about publicity methods, it must respond to the basic appeals, because it will always need food, crave amusement, long for beauty, respond to leadership." As the public grows more intelligent or tired of old methods, firms and leaders must "present their appeals more intelligently." "Propaganda will never die out."
