# Propaganda and political leadership

"The great political problem in our modern democracy is how to induce our leaders to lead." The dogma that the voice of the people is the voice of God makes elected officials "the will-less servants of their constituents." But the voice of the people "is composed of inherited prejudices and symbols and clichés and verbal formulas supplied to them by the leaders", so "the sincere and gifted politician is able, by the instrument of propaganda, to mold and form the will of the people." Disraeli: "I *must* follow the people. Am I not their leader?" To which Bernays adds: "I *must* lead the people. Am I not their servant?"

"The important thing for the statesman of our age is not so much to know how to please the public, but to know how to sway the public." Learned pamphlets will not do it. It can be done "only by meeting the conditions of the public mind, by creating circumstances which set up trains of thought, by dramatizing personalities, by establishing contact with the group leaders" who control opinion. "The political leader must be a creator of circumstances, not only a creature of mechanical process of stereotyping and rubber stamping."

## The diagnosis: politics lags business

Politics was "the first big business in America" and the first to use propaganda on a large scale, but its methods are now "as archaic and ineffective as the advertising methods of business in 1900 would be today." Business learned from politics and kept improving; politics clung to the old formulas. Voter apathy follows: the politician "cannot dramatize himself and his platform in terms which have real meaning to the public." Following slavishly, "he deprives his campaign of all dramatic interest. An automaton cannot arouse the public interest. A leader, a fighter, a dictator, can."

Business borrowed politics' sideshows (annual dinners, flags, speeches, honors for employees), but those are only its drums. "The real work and campaign of business consists of intensive study of the public, the manufacture of products based on this study, and exhaustive use of every means of reaching the public." Political campaigns are "all sideshows, all honors, all bombast, glitter, and speeches", unrelated to studying the public scientifically and supplying it with "party, candidate, platform, and performance."

The cost test (Emily Newell Blair): on a five-day speaking tour of nearly a thousand miles, she and a Senator addressed no more than 1,105 persons whose votes might conceivably have changed, at $15.27 per possible vote. What would an executive say to a sales manager who sent a high-priced speaker to fewer than 1,200 people at $15.27 per possible buyer? Apply the same test to any proposed campaign activity.

The politician "understands the public. He knows what the public wants and what the public will accept." But he "is not necessarily a general sales manager, a public relations counsel, or a man who knows how to secure mass distribution of ideas."

## The campaign plan

Work out "the entire campaign ... according to broad basic plans." Platforms, planks, pledges, budgets, activities and personalities must be "as carefully studied, apportioned and used as they are when big business desires to get what it wants from the public." In order:

1. **Determine the objectives and express them "exceedingly well in the current form—that is, as a platform."** Make it an honest platform. Pledges ought to carry "something of the guarantee principle and money-back policy" of an honorable business, because the public believes "campaign pledges are written on the sand."
2. **Analyze the public and its needs** as scientifically as possible, through a survey of public desires and demands, the way business surveys its market before making or selling. Waste nothing reselling a section already sold or courting a section irrevocably committed elsewhere. Let the analysis change the product, meaning the program, as well as its presentation. Know how often each section of the public is exposed to the appeal, and where national and local efforts duplicate.
3. **Budget.** Business knows what percentage goes to advertising (newspaper, magazine, outdoor, poster), to circularization and sales promotion (house organs, dealer aids), and to supervising salesmen. For a campaign, decide:
   - how much to raise, by careful analysis of campaign costs;
   - how to raise it, candidly and publicly like the war-fund and charity drives. Eliminating "the little black bag element" would raise the prestige of politics and bring earlier, more constructive public participation;
   - how to spend it, giving every step its proportionate importance and coordinating every medium with the whole plan. "Certain expenditures may be warranted if they represent a small proportion of the budget and may be totally unwarranted if they make up a large proportion."
4. **Plan the emotions.** Appealing to emotion is "an indispensable part of the campaign," but "unrelated emotions become maudlin and sentimental too easily", cost too much, and waste effort. Business uses "as many of the basic emotions as possible", while the politician has used only the emotions aroused by words, and "the emotions of oratory have been worn down through long years of overuse." The emotional content must:
   - (a) "coincide in every way with the broad basic plans of the campaign and all its minor details";
   - (b) "be adapted to the many groups of the public at which it is to be aimed"; and
   - (c) "conform to the media of the distribution of ideas."

   Parades and mass meetings work only "when the public has a frenzied emotional interest in the event." Taking babies on one's lap for a photograph is wise only "if this act epitomizes a definite plank": kissing babies "must be used as a symbol for a baby policy and it must be synchronized with a plank in the platform." An unrelated emotional event is like a hockey-skate maker advertising with a church in spring foliage: both are appealing, but neither sells skates.
5. **Put personality in its place.** "A charming candidate is the alchemist's secret that can transmute a prosaic platform into the gold of votes", yet "the party and its aims are certainly more important than the personality of the candidate." Emphasize the candidate's ability to carry out the program, and the program itself. "Even Henry Ford ... has become known through his product, and not for his product through him."
6. **Educate the emotions in terms of groups.** People are largely uninterested in politics, so tie the campaign to their personal interests through the interlocking groups: economic, social, religious, educational, cultural, racial, collegiate, local, sports. Coolidge's breakfast for actors aligned audiences with him. The Shepard-Towner Maternity Bill passed because its backers saw that mothers, educators and physicians were each a group that influenced other groups, and that together they could convince Congress the people wanted it.
7. **Allocate the media.** Give each medium "the work which it can do with maximum efficiency." "Events and activities must be created in order to put ideas into circulation." Every object that shows pictures or words, and everything that makes intelligible sounds, can be used. Beyond the radio, press, banquet hall, mass meeting, lecture platform and stump, use exhibitions, contests, institutes of politics, the cooperation of educational institutions, and "the dramatic cooperation of groups which hitherto have not been drawn into active politics." Synchronize the printed word (books, magazines, letters, posters, circulars, banners, newspapers), pictures (photographs, motion pictures) and the ear (lectures, speeches, band music, radio, campaign songs). "One dare not put all one's eggs into one basket."
8. **Dramatize the issue before addressing the public** (the low-tariff plan in `campaigns.md`).

Done when the platform states honest objectives, every expenditure and emotional appeal traces to a plank, every group has its appeal, and every medium has its assigned work.

Limit: propaganda works "only with the voter who makes up his own mind on the basis of his group prejudices and desires." Where boss loyalties exist, they override it, and that loyalty is the boss's strength.

## The trial balloon, and why to lead instead

The politician has "his ear to the ground", a "clinical ear" that hears disturbances without knowing whether they are superficial or fundamental. So he sends up a **trial balloon**, perhaps an anonymous interview in the press, and waits for reverberations: mass meetings, resolutions, telegrams, editorials. Then he adopts, rejects or modifies the policy. The method is modeled on wartime peace feelers. Bernays judges that it "has little justification": a real leader leads by skillful propaganda "instead of following the people by means of the clumsy instrument of trial and error." The propagandist's approach is the opposite: fix the objective and arrive at it through exact knowledge of the public and modified circumstances.

## Stage-managing the executive

The President's public actions are stage-managed, "but they are chosen to represent and dramatize the man in his function as representative of the people." Hero worship of the President reflects public desire and the nature of the office, not propaganda's fault. A leader without well-directed propaganda fares worse than one puffed up to fit his office (the Prince of Wales in `campaigns.md`).

## Propaganda in government administration

"Campaigning is only an incident in political life. The process of government is continuous." Expert propaganda is "more useful and fundamental, although less striking, as an aid to democratic administration, than as an aid to vote getting." "Good government can be sold to a community just as any other commodity can be sold."

- **Train politicians as propagandists.** The leader should be "as finely versed in the technique of propaganda as in political economy and civics." Bernays would apprentice a party's brightest young men to Broadway productions or professional propagandists before recruiting them.
- **Watch the politician's blind spot.** He is slow to adopt business methods because he has ready entry to the press: reporters depend on him for news, and by giving or withholding information he can censor political news.
- **Bridge the interval.** Buckle: "When the interval between the intellectual classes and the practical classes is too great ... the former will possess no influence, the latter will reap no benefits." "Propaganda bridges this interval."
- **Build interpretive offices.** Following David Lawrence, Bernays proposes an Assistant Secretary of State who knows how to dispense information to the press and has authority to persuade the Secretary to release what is "for insufficient reason, is suppressed." Beyond that, he proposes a **Secretary of Public Relations** in the Cabinet, to interpret America's aims abroad and keep citizens informed of government activities and their reasons, interpreting "the people to the government and the government to the people." Such an official is "a trained technician" who analyzes public thought and trends, not a press agent.

"Ours must be a leadership democracy administered by the intelligent minority who know how to regiment and guide the masses." Call it "government by education", but academic education is not enough: "it must be enlightened expert propaganda through the creation of circumstances, through the high-spotting of significant events, and the dramatization of important issues." That is how the statesman focuses the public mind "on crucial points of policy."

## Objections Bernays answers

- *Does the leader make propaganda, or propaganda the leader?* There must be fertile ground and a vital seed, a mutual need. A press agent cannot sell a man with nothing the public wants to hear.
- *Some propaganda is dishonest.* That does not condemn the methods, because "propaganda in some form will always be used where leaders need to appeal to their constituencies."
- *Will it defeat itself as people see the mechanism?* Only untrue or unsocial propaganda weakens itself.
