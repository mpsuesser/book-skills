# Business and the public

"Business today is taking the public into partnership." Its relationship to the public includes not only making and selling a product but "the selling of itself and of all those things for which it stands in the public mind." "Public opinion is the unacknowledged partner in all broad efforts."

## Why business depends on public opinion

Bernays gives five causes. Check which ones bear on the user's client:

1. **The public's conscience.** Business that ran its affairs regardless of the public met the muckraking reaction. If big business tried to throttle the public, "the public would rise and try to throttle big business with restrictive laws." Corporations had to give up the claim that their affairs were "none of the public's business" and convince the public they conform to its demands as to honesty and fairness.
2. **Mass production.** It "is profitable only if its rhythm can be maintained." Under handicraft, demand created supply. Now "supply must actively seek to create its corresponding demand." "To make customers is the new problem." A firm must understand "the structure, the personality, the prejudices, of a potentially universal public."
3. **Advertising technique.** Mass circulation and the modern advertising art put the businessman "in a personal relation with a vast and diversified public."
4. **The new competition.** There is competition between a firm and the rest of its industry, and between whole industries for the consumer's dollar. A soap maker who claims his soap preserves youth changes how the public thinks about all soap; the metal furniture industry trying to beat wood seeks to alter the taste of a generation. O. H. Cheney's terms: **intercommodity competition** is "between products used alternatively for the same purpose" (stone against wood, linoleum against carpets, oranges against apples, tin against asbestos), and **inter-industrial competition** is between "apparently unrelated industries which affect each other" (big fur collars force small, cheap hats; warmer homes mean lighter clothes, so heating fights textiles). In food it is keenest, "we cannot eat more than we can eat." Intercommodity competition "cannot be fought single-handed", so firms turn to their trade associations. Bernays predicts that future competition will be not only an advertising competition but "in addition ... a competition of propaganda."
5. **Finance.** New stock and bond flotations succeed only if the concern has gained the confidence and good will of the general public. Victor Talking Machine stock sold millions overnight, while some financially sound companies cannot float an issue because of "some unanalyzed prejudice against them." A merger "may stand or fall upon the public acceptance which is created for it", and genuine public acceptance creates real economic values, as distinct from "artificial value given to a stock by dishonest propaganda or stock manipulation." The same holds for foreign loans: an east European country's issue fared badly because of the ruling family's behavior.

## Dramatize the corporate personality at every point of contact

Business "must dramatize its personality and interpret its objectives in every particular in which it comes into contact with the community." "At whatever point a business enterprise impinges on the public consciousness", give it the character that fits the objective:

- An oil corporation offers not only good oil but a sound labor policy.
- A bank shows that its management is sound and that its officers are honorable in public and private life.
- A fashionable men's clothing store expresses the authenticity of its goods in its architecture.
- A bakery wraps loaves in dustproof paper, opens its factory to public inspection, and keeps its delivery wagons clean and attractive.
- A construction firm lets the public know its buildings are safe and that its employees are compensated when injured.
- A store selling volume in cheap goods preaches prices day in and day out. A store seeking a high margin per sale associates itself with the distinguished and elegant, through an exhibition of old masters or the social activities of the owner's wife.
- The New York Central appealed on speed and safety and also on elegance and comfort, personified by Chauncey M. Depew, "an ideal window dressing for such an enterprise."

"There is no detail too trivial to influence the public in a favorable or unfavorable sense." The president's personality may dramatize the whole concern, so it matters which charities he gives to and which civic offices he holds; "if he is a leader in his industry, the public may demand that he be a leader in his community." This is "not a question of ballyhoo, of creating a picturesque fiction", but of "finding the appropriate modes of expressing the personality that is to be dramatized." And it must be true. Public relations "cannot be a protective coloring to hide its real aims."

## Responsibilities to each public

Map each public the client touches and what it owes them:

| Public | How the responsibility is expressed |
|---|---|
| Stockholders (five or five hundred thousand) | The right to know how their money is used; frequent letters urging them to use the product and promote its sale |
| Dealers | Invite them, at the company's expense, to visit the home factory |
| The industry as a whole | Restraint from exaggerated and unfair selling claims |
| Retailers | Salesmen who express the quality of the product |
| Consumers | A clean, well-managed factory open to inspection |
| The general public | Financial dealings, labor policy, even the livableness of the employees' houses |

## The modifications counsel recommends

The counsel interprets the business's purpose to the public and suggests "those modifications which may make it conform to the public demand." They range from transforming entire lines of goods to the dress of the clerks. Examples:

- A corporation finds its labor policy causes public resentment and adopts a more enlightened one "solely for the sake of general good will."
- A department store with falling sales finds its clerks have a reputation for bad manners and starts formal instruction in courtesy and tact.
- A department store seeking high-class trade employs college graduates as clerks and engages well-known modern artists to design show windows and special exhibits.
- Public opinion was brought round to large mergers "not only by modifying the opinion of the public ... but often by modifying the business concern itself": a cement company maintains testing laboratories for road commissions free of charge, and a gas company runs a free school of cookery.

See also the jewelry store, the Fifth Avenue branch and Metropolitan Life in `campaigns.md`.

## Going beyond price

A standardized, mass-produced product competing on price alone leads to "a cut-throat competition which can end only by taking all the profit and incentive out of the industry." The way out is "to develop some sales appeal other than mere cheapness": some idea that modifies the product slightly, "some element of originality that will distinguish it", like typewriters painted in cheerful hues. Popularize it through "the principles of gregariousness, obedience to authority, emulation, and the like." "A minor element can be made to assume economic importance by being established in the public mind as a matter of style." This splits mass production and leaves room for the small specialty shop beside the huge department store. In many fields the escape is competition on aesthetic values, which gives the artist a new place in industry (see `causes-and-institutions.md`, art).

## The indirect approach

One reason to use an outside expert is that "the correct approach to a problem may be indirect." Ask which parties other than the consumer control the outcome, and direct the campaign at them (the luggage campaign in `campaigns.md`). Ask which broader socially constructive cause the client's success would further, and fight for that cause. Building public opinion for a socially constructive cause is often how the propagandist meets "his own problem which the socially constructive cause would further", and in doing so he "is actually fulfilling a social purpose in the broadest sense." The ideas of the new propaganda "are predicated on sound psychology based on enlightened self-interest."

## Defending against unfair competition and attack

- Against exaggerated, overemphatic competing advertising that is undermining confidence in a whole industry, "the only way to combat such unethical methods, is for ethical members of the industry to use the weapon propaganda in order to bring out the basic truths of the situation" through channels other than advertising (the toothpaste case).
- A small industry "is swamped unless it can find appropriate and relatively inexpensive means of making known the special virtues of its product." Large industries respond with cooperative advertising by associations.
- Favorable opinion is not permanent. Professor W. Z. Ripley exposed illusory stockholder voting power, misleadingly brief annual statements, nonvoting shares that hand control to a small clique, and refusals to give out enough information. Utilities "are always fair game for public discontent" and face demands for government or municipal ownership unless care is taken "to maintain the contact with the public at all points of their corporate existence." ## Emergencies

"Often the public relations is called in to handle an emergency situation." A false rumor "may occasion an enormous loss in prestige and money if not handled promptly and effectively" (Hudson Motor, Beech-Nut in `campaigns.md`).

## Lessons from the amusement business

The circus, the medicine show and the theater taught business the rudiments of advertising. Business refined their ballyhoo to precise ends, and the theater has since learned back from business. The modern publicity director of a theater syndicate or motion picture trust is responsible for millions in capital and "cannot afford to be a stunt artist." "As public knowledge increases and public taste improves, business must be ready to meet them halfway." "Modern business must have its finger continuously on the public pulse."

## When to use an outside counsel

"Some business men can be their own best public relations counsel." But in most cases "knowledge of the public mind and of the ways in which it will react to an appeal, is a specialized function." The counsel "is not an advertising man but he advocates advertising where that is indicated", and his work complements the advertising agency's without duplicating it. He goes by many titles: director, counsel, secretary, vice president, commissioner. His function may include "the discovery of new markets, the existence of which had been unsuspected."
