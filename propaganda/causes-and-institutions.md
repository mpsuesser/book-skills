# Causes and institutions

Bernays applies the same method to education, social service, women's organizations, art and science. "Charity, as well as business, and politics and literature, for that matter, have had to adopt propaganda." Organizations like the Near East Relief "have to work on public opinion just as though they had tubes of toothpaste to sell." "Approval of the public is essential to any large undertaking," so "a laudable movement may be lost unless it impresses itself on the public mind."

Propaganda also serves the masses' own unvoiced desires. A desire for reform, however widespread, "cannot be translated into action until it is made articulate." Millions of housewives may want harmful manufactured foods prohibited, but nothing happens until "their half-expressed demand can be organized, made vocal, and concentrated upon the state legislature or upon the Federal Congress." It is "the intelligent minorities which need to make use of propaganda continuously and systematically", especially "the active proselytizing minorities in whom selfish interests and public interests coincide."

## Social service

- Social service depends on the voluntary support of the wealthy, so it "is obliged to use propaganda continually." Its leaders were among the first to use propaganda in the modern sense.
- **Inertia is the enemy.** "The great enemy of any attempt to change men's habits is inertia. Civilization is limited by inertia." Attitudes continue under tradition, as mantles pass from Comstock to Sumner and from Carnegie to Schwab, and only "an active public opinion that has been directed consciously into movements against inertia" moves them. Today anyone may try to sway opinion and assume leadership for his own thesis.
- **Social service is propaganda.** Its activities are attempts to alter habits and opinions: a teeth campaign alters brushing habits; a parks campaign alters opinion on taxing oneself for parks; a tuberculosis campaign convinces everyone that tuberculosis can be cured and that people with certain symptoms should go to the doctor at once; an infant mortality campaign alters how mothers feed, bathe and care for babies. "Social service, in fact, is identical with propaganda in [many] cases." So frame a cause's goal as the specific habit or opinion to change, in the specific group.
- **Administrative reform needs it too.** Professor Harry Elmer Barnes found Pennsylvania prison reform hampered by politics: the legislature must be persuaded, which requires enlightened public opinion, so "the solution of prison problems ... seems to be fundamentally a problem of conscientious and scientific publicity."
- Churches use advertising committees, newspapers, billboards, pamphlets and their own periodicals; the Methodist Board of Publication and Information systematically sends releases to the press.
- The worked model is the NAACP convention in `campaigns.md`: dramatize the year's campaign in one event, place it where the view carries most authority, surround it with leaders who stand for weighty ideas (above all leaders from the opposing region), spotlight a dramatic agreement, then use the coverage as a weapon with selected groups.

"Social progress is simply the progressive education and enlightenment of the public mind in regard to its immediate and distant social problems."

## Women's organizations

Women's influence "is exerted when they are organized and armed with the weapon of propaganda", and it is far greater through organized groups than through office holding. The suffrage campaign "showed the possibilities of propaganda to achieve certain ends."

- **The legislative-program method.** In Washington the Legislative Committee of fourteen women's organizations (the League of Women Voters, the Young Women's Christian Association, the Women's Christian Temperance Union, the Federation of Women's Clubs and others) maps out a legislative program and then uses the modern technique of propaganda to make it law. They are credited with much welfare legislation: the eight-hour day, prohibition and its enforcement, and the Shepard-Towner Bill for federal support of maternity welfare.
- The National League of Women Voters propagandizes its measures (child welfare, education, the home and high prices, women in gainful occupations, public health and morals, independent citizenship for married women) through bulletins, calendars, election information, a correspondence course on government, demonstration classes and citizenship schools.
- **Why it works:** suffrage campaigns trained a professional class of executive and legislative secretaries, "where every device known to the propagandist had to be used to regiment a recalcitrant majority", and prominent suffrage and war-drive leaders moved into peacetime causes. Women are "a particularly striking example of intelligent use of the new propaganda to secure attention and acceptance of minority ideas", using the newest weapons to offset inexperience in practical politics. The Consumer's Committee price-tag store in `campaigns.md` is the model.
- **Clubs as organized channels.** The General Federation of Women's Clubs has 13,000 clubs: civic and city clubs, mothers' and homemakers' clubs, cultural clubs (art, music, literature), business and professional women's clubs, and general clubs. They work in health education, the fine arts, legislation for women and children, playgrounds and parks, social and political morality, and homemaking and education, often on efforts no existing agency covers. Matched projects:
  - homemaking club: a cooking school for young brides (the New York *Herald Tribune* cooking school filled Carnegie Hall, almost 3,000 seats, for several days, "rivaling the drawing power of a McCormack or a Paderewski");
  - mothers' and child-welfare club: milk in the public schools, a baby health station;
  - music club: work with the local radio station on better programs ("Fighting bad music can be as militant a campaign ... as any political battle");
  - art club: loan exhibitions for the city, traveling exhibits of members' or schools' work;
  - literary club: a school competition for the best essay on the city's history or its most famous son.

  Above all, the club "constitutes an organized channel through which women can make themselves felt as a definite part of public opinion." Women will supplement men in public life by concentrating on "those objects which men are likely to ignore", as "active protagonists of new ideas and new methods of political and social housekeeping."

## Education

"Education is not securing its proper share of public interest." It gets newspaper space only when an issue is created (the Gary School system, Harvard's business school).

**Two causes, two remedies:**

1. The educator is trained to stimulate individual students, not to be an "educator at large of the public", though he depends on that public "for his living, for the moral support, and the general cultural tone" of his work. Normal schools should teach that "his is a twofold job: education as a teacher and education as a propagandist."
2. The pedagogue, poorly paid and compared by his own pupils with successful businessmen, feels inferior and becomes "repressed and suppressed." The public will not soon change its standards, so the profession must change this itself by carrying on "a very definite propaganda" asserting its relation to the society it serves and raising "the general appreciation of the teaching profession." It must "raise itself by its own bootstraps" or lose the power to recruit talent. Propaganda cannot fix low pay or pensions directly, but "an intelligent appeal predicated upon the actual present composition of the public mind" can change the attitude, which will begin by insisting on more adequate salaries.

The National Education Association and others propagandize for the larger purposes of education and the prestige of teachers. Cases like McAndrew's, and the choosing of teachers on political or sectarian grounds rather than ability, would improve if propaganda made them a national public concern.

**The two financing types and their hazards:**

- The **state university** "prospers according to the extent to which it can sell itself to the people of the state" through the legislature, so it needs a president who is a propagandist and "a dramatizer of educational issues." The danger is shaping all policy toward the legislature: educating the state at the expense of its own pupils, becoming a tool of the party in power, or letting the president's personality outweigh the institution's function.
- The **endowed college** depends on key men of industry whose concrete, limited objectives are often at variance with abstract knowledge. "Men who, through a sense of inferiority, despise money, seek to win the good will of men who love money." The balance is public support. If the potential donor is apathetic, "enthusiastic public approval must be obtained to convince him"; if he tries to dictate educational policy, "public opinion must support the college." If either side dominates, the result is "a demagoguery or a snobbishness." A longer-term solution is propaganda that develops social consciousness among men of affairs as a class.

**The college news bureau.** About seventy-five bureaus (Yale, Wellesley, Illinois, Indiana, Wisconsin, Western Reserve, Tufts, California and others) belong to the American Association of College News Bureaus, which publishes a bimonthly news letter, upholds ethical standards and works in harmony with the press. Their agenda is a checklist for any institution:

- educate the alumnus, and through him the public and specific groups;
- educate the future student to choose this college;
- keep an esprit de corps in which athletic prowess does not come first;
- make the college's research familiar to those who may lend aid;
- make its aims and work understood so as to attract special endowments for specified purposes.

**Specific problems the counsel meets:** the pharmaceutical college whose profession is degraded as the drug store becomes a soda fountain and lunch counter; Cornell's rare endowments, because people think it a publicly supported state institution (a false picture to correct). Research results should also reach the public "in the dramatic form which the public can understand" (the Harvard Maya case in `campaigns.md`). Lending faculty to public investigations, as Cornell lent Professor Wilcox to the census and Yale's Irving Fisher advised on currency, also builds standing.

**The president's job** includes "the kind of mental picture his institution produces on the public mind." Apply the two-way diagnosis in `SKILL.md`: modify the impression if it is oblique, modify the institution if the impression is correct. Do this for extension courses, for the 80 percent research reputation, and for dramatizing an expedition that sheds light on a Biblical assertion, or a zoology department chasing a bacillus. Ceremonial inaugurations (Columbia's Casa Italiana, Harvard's Germanic Museum) high-spot an institution's standing.

Propaganda in education "may be abused. It may be used to over-advertise an institution and to create in the public mind artificial values."

## Art

- "In art as in politics the minority rules, but it can rule only by going out to meet the public on its own ground, by understanding the anatomy of public opinion and utilizing it." A gallery launching an artist must "create public acceptance for his works."
- **Industry's opening for the artist.** Mass production competing on price alone reaches an impasse, so it must create "a field of competition based on aesthetic values." The artist can collaborate with industry to put "beautiful instead of ugly motifs in the articles of common use" and win recognition and money. Almost no product in daily use (furniture, clothes, lamps, posters, commercial labels, book jackets, pocketbooks, bathtubs) is exempt from the laws of good taste. Propaganda can point out what is and is not beautiful, using "the authority of group leaders whose taste and opinion are recognized."
- **Make abstract beauty vital by association.** "The public must be interested by means of associational values and dramatic incidents." A technical or abstract beauty must be tied to "values which it recognizes and responds to" (Cheney silk at the Louvre, the modernist piano and the Greek vase on china, all in `campaigns.md`).
- **Movements through official and commercial bodies.** Bernays, as Associate Commissioner, helped organize industrial-art business leaders as delegates to the Paris Exposition of Decorative Arts, and within a few years the modern art movement had penetrated every field of industry. R. H. Macy held an Art-in-Trades Exposition with the Metropolitan Museum of Art as adviser, and Lord & Taylor a Modern Arts Exposition with foreign exhibitors. The stores, close to the life of the people, did the propagandizing; the museum used them to reach the public.
- **Museums.** The museum "suffers most from the lack of effective propaganda". Museums are seen as "morgues or sanctuaries" when they should be "leaders and teachers in the aesthetic life of the community." Their treasures "need to be interpreted to the public, and this requires a propagandist." The Metropolitan's 1926 record of a million and a quarter visitors, lectures, story hours, loan collections, facilities for commercial firms, outside lecturers and free chamber concerts is good but "not the whole of the problem":
  - "It is not merely a question of making people come to the museum. It is also a question of making the museum, and the beauty which it houses, go to the people."
  - Don't judge it by visitor counts; its function is "to project itself and what it stands for in the community."
  - It might establish a museum council of art to set standards in home decoration, architecture and commercial production, or a research board for applied arts.
  - Against the view that a museum's first duty is conservatism: its other duty is to interpret, and it "must bodily assume aesthetic leadership."

## Science

Pure science, once fostered by learned societies, now finds support in corporate laboratories that spend hundreds of thousands "for the sake of one golden invention or discovery." When the invention emerges, business takes on the responsibility of placing it at the public's service and "of interpreting its meaning to the public." Industry can, and is "under obligation to", furnish schools and colleges "the exact truth concerning the scientific progress of our age." American Telephone and Telegraph, Western Electric, General Electric and Westinghouse understood that their ideas must be made intelligible to the public, and they use television, broadcasting and loud speakers as propaganda aids. "Propaganda assists in marketing new inventions" and, by repeatedly interpreting new ideas, "is accustoming the public to change and progress."
