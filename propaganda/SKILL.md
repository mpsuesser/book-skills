# Propaganda

**Modern propaganda** "is a consistent, enduring effort to create or shape events to influence the relations of the public to an enterprise, idea or group." In the broad sense it is "an organized effort to spread a particular belief or doctrine"; at its simplest, "the establishing of reciprocal understanding between an individual and a group." Bernays holds it neither good nor bad in itself: that "depends upon the merit of the cause urged, and the correctness of the information published." It applies to building a cathedral, endowing a university, marketing a moving picture, floating a bond issue, electing a president, selling bacon, or lowering the infant death rate.

The practitioner is the **public relations counsel**: the propagandist "who specializes in interpreting enterprises and ideas to the public, and in interpreting the public to promulgators of new enterprises and ideas." He advises like a lawyer, but on the public contacts of the client's business rather than its legal ones, and he shapes the client's *actions*, not only its messages, so that they "gain the interest, the approval, and the acceptance of the public."

The core formula: "The whole basis of successful propaganda is to have an objective and then to endeavor to arrive at it through an exact knowledge of the public and modifying circumstances to manipulate and sway that public."

## How the public mind is organized

**Invisible government.** "We are governed, our minds molded, our tastes formed, our ideas suggested, largely by men we have never heard of." A small number who understand the mental processes and social patterns of the masses "pull the wires which control the public mind." They rule by natural leadership, by supplying needed ideas, and by their key position in the social structure. This is necessary, not sinister: no one can study every issue or chemically test every soap, so society consents to have its *field of choice* narrowed: party machines narrow candidates to two or three, leaders "high-spot the outstanding issue", and propaganda narrows the goods that reach attention. Having chosen open competition over "committees of wise men", "society has consented to permit free competition to be organized by leadership and propaganda." "Propaganda is the executive arm of the invisible government," and "anyone with sufficient influence can lead sections of the public at least for a time and for a given purpose." The idea is **relative**: different people rule different departments of life (one in politics, another in the discount rate, another in next season's dances), and every parent is a group leader to their children.

**The anatomy of society.** The public is not an amorphous mass but a structure of **cleavages** (social, political, economic, racial, religious, ethical, each with hundreds of subdivisions) along which "flow information and opinion carrying authority to the individual groups." Groups no longer need a shared place: the 22,128 periodicals and 5,500 conventions of 1928 tie together people of the same interest across the country, and informal groups count too (the bridge club where "opinions [are] stereotyped", the sorority that follows one society leader). Groups **interlace**: John Jones is a Rotarian, a churchman, a party member, a golfer, and what he hears in one group he carries into the others. This "invisible, intertwining structure of groupings and associations is the mechanism by which democracy has organized its group mind and simplified its mass thinking."

**The group mind** (Trotter, Le Bon) "does not *think*": "In place of thoughts it has impulses, habits, and emotions." Man is gregarious and feels himself a member of a herd even alone in his room. Five working laws follow:

- **Follow the leader.** The group's first impulse "is usually to follow the example of a trusted leader": "one of the most firmly established principles of mass psychology." It makes and breaks summer resorts, bank runs, panics, best-sellers. Hence: "If you can influence the leaders, either with or without their conscious cooperation, you automatically influence the group which they sway." And leaders are themselves led: the congressman by a district boss, the chamber of commerce president by a national authority.
- **Clichés.** With no leader at hand, the herd thinks by **clichés**: "pat words or images which stand for a whole group of ideas or experiences." Tagging a candidate with *interests*, or a policy with *Bolshevik*, stampedes millions. A cliché cannot be dissociated from the picture it evokes, so change the word instead: British *evacuation hospitals*, criticized for handling the wounded summarily, became *evacuation posts*, and the criticism vanished because no one expected more than emergency care from a "post."
- **Stereotypes and rubber stamps.** Universal literacy gave the common man not a mind but "rubber stamps", inked with slogans, editorials, scientific data and platitudes; millions exposed to the same stimuli receive identical imprints. Regimented groups "cling to [their] stereotypes" (Lippmann) so tenaciously that legislators and editors become "mere bits of driftwood in the surf." A picture that fits a group's existing prejudice is grasped and made its own.
- **True motives.** "Men are rarely aware of the real reasons which motivate their actions." The car buyer who believes he compared every make bought because a friend whose financial acumen he respects bought one, or his neighbors doubted he could afford it, or it carries his fraternity colors. After Freud, a thing is often desired as a symbol (social position, business success, pleasing a wife) of what one is ashamed to want openly. "The successful propagandist must understand the true motives and not be content to accept the reasons which men give for what they do." A judgment is "a mélange of impressions": the stock bought because it was in yesterday's headlines, its fast train served a good dinner, J. P. Morgan owns some.
- **Custom.** People buy what "it is at the moment the group custom to buy." The rival of the piano is not other pianos but the car everyone is buying.

"Human desires are the steam which makes the social machine work." Knowing the groups is the engineer knowing the cylinders; knowing desires is knowing how steam behaves under pressure.

**Limits on the public's malleability.** "The public has its own standards and demands and habits. You may modify them, but you dare not run counter to them." No one can put a generation of women back in long skirts, but through leaders of fashion they can be persuaded to wear evening dresses long in back. Propaganda also needs "fertile ground for the leader and the idea to fall on" and "some vital seed to sow": it "is of no use to the politician unless he has something to say which the public, consciously or unconsciously, wants to hear." Results carry a wide margin of error: the international flight planned to produce good will can be eclipsed by an unexpected event or a more spectacular aviator the day before. Propaganda is "scientific in the sense that it seeks to base its operations upon definite knowledge drawn from direct observation of the group mind", never an exact science, because its subject is human beings.

## The new propaganda versus the old

The **new propaganda** "takes account not merely of the individual, nor even of the mass mind alone, but also and especially of the anatomy of society, with its interlocking group formations and loyalties." Its technique came out of the war, when the government appealed to the individual "by means of every approach—visual, graphic, and auditory," secured "the cooperation of the key men in every group," and used "the mental clichés and the emotional habits of the public" to produce mass reactions. The individual is a cell organized into a social unit: "Touch a nerve at a sensitive spot and you get an automatic response from certain specific members of the organism."

| Old propaganda (old salesmanship) | New propaganda (new salesmanship) |
|---|---|
| Mechanistic reaction psychology: a repeated stimulus makes a habit, a reiterated idea makes a conviction | Group structure and mass psychology: set up "psychological and emotional currents which will work for him" |
| Printed word, aimed at fifty million people, each as an individual, to buy a definite article immediately: "YOU ... buy O'Leary's rubber heels—NOW." | Deals with men in the mass through their group formations |
| "Eat more bacon ... because it is cheap, because it is good" | First asks "Who is it that influences the eating habits of the world?" ("The physicians"), then has physicians say publicly that bacon is wholesome, knowing "the psychological relation of dependence of men upon their physicians" |
| Assaults sales resistance by direct attack | Removes sales resistance: "creates circumstances which will swing emotional currents so as to make for purchaser demand" |
| The seller says "Please buy a piano." | The buyer says "Please sell me a piano." |
| The politician on the radio argues, "assaulting, single-handed, the resistance of inertia" | The propagandist dramatizes the issue first, so that his speech answers "the spontaneous questions ... of a public already keyed to a certain pitch of interest" |

The signature move is the **music room**. A direct appeal for Mozart pianos competes with every other piano and with the radio and the motorcar. So the propagandist "sets to work to create circumstances which will modify that custom": an exhibition of period music rooms by decorators who influence buying groups, rare tapestries for prestige, a ceremony attended by a famous violinist, a popular artist and a society leader, the whole projected through publicity channels, while influential architects build a music room with a piano niche into their plans and lesser architects imitate them. "The music room will be accepted because it has been made the thing." Whoever has one will think of buying a piano, and "it will come to him as his own idea."

## Running a campaign

The counsel's work runs in order. Do each step with the user's real case before moving on.

### 1. Analyze the client

Examine the product or idea, its markets, how the public reacts to it, the employees' attitude to the public and to the product, and the cooperation of the distribution agencies. Then apply the soundness test: is it "something which the public accepts or can be brought to accept"? "It is futile to attempt to sell an idea or to prepare the ground for a product that is basically unsound." Where it fails, the remedy is to **modify the client**:

- An orphan asylum losing contributions finds the public subconsciously faults it for not using the new "cottage plan". The advice is to change the asylum.
- A railroad is urged to put on a fast train for the prestige it lends the road's name, stocks and bonds.
- Corsets cannot come back, since women have emancipated themselves from them, but a girdle without the corset's unhealthful features may be adopted.

Done when you can state what the client offers, whether the public can be brought to accept it, and what in the client must change first.

### 2. Analyze the public

Study "the groups which must be reached, and the leaders through whom he may approach these groups": social, economic, geographical, age, doctrinal, language and cultural groups. Study the field "in the spirit of the laboratory" with a clipping service, a corps of scouts, or personal study at a crucial spot: which features are losing appeal, which way taste is veering, who has the final word (does the wife choose her husband's car, suits and shirts?). Look past stated reasons to true motives, and identify the custom that governs the choice. Where opinion is hostile, survey it and sort it into the "points of irreducible opposition", the aspects "susceptible of logical explanation", how far the hostility is "a habitual emotional reaction", and what is "dominated by accepted clichés." Each kind gets its own remedy. Spend only where opinion can still move: nothing on a section already sold, nothing on one irrevocably committed elsewhere.

Done when you have the groups, the named leaders or kinds of leaders who carry authority to each, the true motives and governing custom, and, if there is opposition, its four-way sort.

### 3. Formulate policies

Only after this "double analysis" is collated, formulate "policies governing the general practice, procedure, and habits of the client in all those aspects in which he comes in contact with the public." The modifications may be the broadest matters of policy or the dress of the clerks. Sometimes the counsel's work ends here: "the stage at which many suppose he starts his activities may actually be the stage at which he ends them."

Done when every point where the client touches the public has a policy consistent with the objective.

### 4. Create circumstances

Only when the policies are agreed does the client's case go to the public, and it goes through created events rather than assertion:

- **Hunt for the vital spot.** Find the source from which the custom flows and attack there. Velvet could not be revived inside America, so the campaign went to Paris couturiers and Lyons silk makers ("Anatomical hunt for the vital spot!"), "to substitute purpose for chance."
- **Find where interests coincide.** "The leaders who lend their authority to any propaganda campaign will do so only if it can be made to touch their own interests. There must be a disinterested aspect of the propagandist's activities." The counsel's job is "to discover at what points his client's interests coincide with those of other individuals or groups." Artists and educators sponsored Ivory soap sculpture because it cultivated the aesthetic impulse in children. The principle of "a common denominator of interest between the object that is sold and the public good-will can be carried to infinite degrees."
- **Enlist the key people** whose word carries authority to their followers: committees of artists, distinguished women, editors, physicians, judges. Surround the event "with people who were stereotypes for ideas that carried weight." Their juxtaposition with the idea is itself the message.
- **Stage the event**: an exhibition, competition, ceremony, fête, convention, inauguration, bureau or expedition, built of component parts important enough to become news. Choose place and timing for authority and coverage: a southern view on a southern question, issued from a southern city, carries more weight; Masaryk proclaimed Czechoslovakia free on a Monday because the press had more space ("Cables make history").
- **Set motives in motion.** The soap contests engaged "the aesthetic, the competitive, the gregarious ..., the snobbish (the impulse to follow the example of a recognized leader), the exhibitionist, and ... the maternal." Elsewhere Bernays names "gregariousness, obedience to authority, emulation", the home instinct, and associational values.
- **Use the associative process.** Tie the client to what the public already esteems: a society benefit, a great museum, a Paris couturier, a famous date, a picturesque landmark building.
- **Project and synchronize.** Carry the event through every channel (press, magazines, radio, pictures, lectures, letters, stores, dealer broadsides). Each channel echoes the others, as when hundreds of department stores copied the first one that advertised velvet. "One dare not put all one's eggs into one basket," and every appeal must fit the whole plan.
- **Let the direct appeal come last, if at all.** Once the circumstance exists, editors report it, stores stock it and buyers ask for it.

Choose the plan's form: **continuous interpretation** means "trying to control every approach to the public mind in such a manner that the public receives the desired impression, often without being conscious of it"; **dramatization by high-spotting** "vividly seizes the attention of the public and fixes it upon some detail or aspect which is typical of the entire enterprise", like the office building made ten feet taller than the highest skyscraper. They "may be alternative or may be pursued concurrently", and which to use "can be determined only after a full study of objectives and specific possibilities."

Done when each target group has an event or channel reaching it through a leader whose own interest the campaign serves, and every element supports one objective.

### 5. Maintain

Often the work "must be continuous to be effective": "a careful system of constant, thorough and frank information." Keep "constant vigilance" and keep "its finger continuously on the public pulse." A single false rumor at a critical moment can cost millions, and "an air of secrecy or mystery" acts as "an invisible drag" on all the company's dealings. Against rumor, in rising order of strength: stop it at its source; counteract it promptly with correct or more complete information through the most effective channels; "or best of all establish such relationships of confidence in the concern's integrity that rumors and suspicions will have no opportunity to take root." When one must be answered, answer it with an event that makes the truth self-evident. Beech-Nut's town held a public day honoring its president for refusing to sell, and "a denial would not have carried as much conviction." The counsel should also "anticipate such trends of public opinion and advise on how to avert them", either by convincing the public its fears are unjustified or by modifying the client to remove the cause.

## Diagnosing a wrong public picture

If an enterprise does not produce the mental picture it should, one of two things is wrong. Either the media of communication are wrong or unbalanced, so the public has an oblique impression and the *impression* must be modified; or the public has a correct impression, and the *enterprise* must be modified. Both lie within the counsel's province. If the extension courses are unduly scholastic, don't publicize them; change them first. If a university is thought to be 80 percent postgraduate research, correct a false impression by high-spotting undergraduate life, and if it is true, make the most of it by dramatizing the discoveries of public interest.

## The counsel's code

Bernays sets these as the profession's ethics and its practical conditions:

- The ideal is "to make the producer ... understand what the public wants and to make the public understand the objectives of the producer", eliminating the waste and friction of making what the public doesn't want or offering what it doesn't understand.
- Refuse a client believed dishonest, a product believed fraudulent, a cause believed antisocial, a client whose interests conflict with another's, and a case believed hopeless or a product unmarketable. The counsel is never dissociated from his client in the public mind, and in "the court of public opinion" he is "judge and jury."
- "His business is not to fool or hoodwink the public." Propaganda material is "clearly labeled as to source", and the editor accepts or rejects it on its merits as news.
- Public relations "cannot be a protective coloring to hide its real aims." Featuring a few high-class articles over a cheap main stock gives a false impression and is "bad business as well as bad morals."
- On whether propaganda defeats itself once the public sees the mechanism: "The only propaganda which will ever tend to weaken itself as the world becomes more sophisticated and intelligent, is propaganda that is untrue or unsocial." However cynical the public becomes, it "must respond to the basic appeals, because it will always need food, crave amusement, long for beauty, respond to leadership."

## Reading propaganda

To see propaganda at work, "look under the surface of the newspaper." Of eight important stories on one *New York Times* front page, four were propaganda, each the statement of an interested party that the casual reader takes for a spontaneous happening (a State Department report "made public" that day, a Carnegie Foundation trustee's survey, the Real Estate Board president's demand, Secretary Hoover's report). Read for what an item *is* rather than what it says: "What it says is less important than what it is", and its source gives it its authority. Ask who created the event, which leaders lent their names and why, which group custom it is shifting, and who benefits. Apply one standard to both sides. It is a double standard to call your own group's advocacy "a plain forceful statement of truth" and the opposing group's "propaganda": "there are usually proponents and opponents of every propaganda."
