# Campaigns from Propaganda

Every campaign Bernays works through, grouped by the technique it models. Each gives the problem, the moves in order, the result where the book states one, and the principle to carry over. Adapt the moves to the user's case; the principle is the transferable part.

## Changing a custom at its source

**Velvet.** *Problem:* velvet manufacturers faced ruin because velvet had long been out of fashion, and analysis showed a velvet fashion could not be revived from inside America. *Moves:*
1. Hunt for the vital spot: Paris is the home of fashion and Lyons the home of silk, so "the attack had to be made at the source."
2. Organize a velvet fashion service, openly supported by the manufacturers, to contact the Lyons manufactories and the Paris couturiers, learn what they were doing, encourage them to act for velvet, and help exploit their wares.
3. Enlist an intelligent Parisian, who induced Lanvin, Worth, Agnes, Patou and others to use velvet in gowns and hats and arranged for a countess or duchess to wear them.
4. Show the American buyer or woman of fashion the velvet creations in the atelier. "She bought the velvet because she liked it and because it was in fashion."
5. Let the created circumstance reach American fashion editors and reporters, who "reflected it in their news."
6. A department store aiming at style leadership advertised velvet on the couturiers' authority, quoting their cables; hundreds of stores echoed it; the woman traveler appeared before the ship-news photographers in velvet.

*Result:* "Fickle fashion has veered to velvet"; a trickle became a flood, and the industry again kept thousands busy. *Principle:* "substitute purpose for chance" by working through the regular sources of distribution for the custom, so that everyone downstream reports a real, though created, circumstance.

**The music room** (Mozart pianos). *Problem:* a direct appeal competes with every piano maker and, indirectly, with the radio and motorcar; people buy cars because that is the group custom. *Moves:* appeal to the home instinct by developing acceptance of the music room, through an exhibition of period music rooms by well-known decorators; rare tapestries for prestige; a ceremony with key people (a famous violinist, a popular artist, a society leader) to create dramatic interest; projection of "the juxtaposition of these leaders, and the idea which they are dramatizing" through publicity channels; influential architects persuaded to make the music room integral to their plans, with a niche for the piano, which less influential architects imitate. *Result:* the buyer asks to be sold a piano, and the idea comes "to him as his own idea." *Principle:* sell the setting that makes the product necessary, not the product.

**Large hats.** *Problem:* the simple felt hat was crowding out every other kind of hat and hat ornament. *Moves:*
1. Classify hats into six types.
2. Identify the groups that could change hat fashions: the society leader, the style expert, the fashion editor and writer, the artist who might give artistic approval, and beautiful mannequins.
3. Bring them together before an audience of hat buyers. A committee of prominent artists chose the most beautiful girls in New York to wear the most beautiful hat in each classification in tableaux at a fashion fête in a leading hotel; a committee of distinguished American women lent their names on the ground of developing an American industry; a style committee of fashion editors and authorities supported it.
4. Parade before the whole trade, then carry it to consumers through newspapers and store advertising, with manufacturers' broadsides to millinery buyers.

*Result:* one manufacturer who had sold no large trimmed hats before the show sold thousands after it. *Principle:* assemble the custom's authorities around one event aimed at the trade, and the trade carries it to the consumer.

**Silk shoes.** A silk manufacturer seeking a new market suggested to a large shoe manufacturer that women's shoes be covered with silk to match their dresses; the idea was systematically propagandized; a popular actress was persuaded to wear them; the fashion spread, and both firms were ready with supply for the created demand. *Principle:* the man who injects the idea into an industry rules that department of people's lives.

**The London tailor.** An anonymous gentleman tailor in London suggests to British noblemen blue cloth instead of gray, two buttons instead of three; under contract he sends the chosen designs to a large American suit maker, which orders the cloth, advertises the suits as the latest fashion, has them worn by fashionable men in New York, Chicago, Boston and Philadelphia, and so reaches John Smith of Topeka, "recognizing this leadership." *Principle:* trace the chain of imitation to its head and enter there.

## Moving a group through its authorities

**Bacon.** Ask "Who is it that influences the eating habits of the world?" The answer: "The physicians." Suggest to physicians that they say publicly that it is wholesome to eat bacon; large numbers will follow their doctors' advice "as a mathematical certainty." *Principle:* find the authority the public depends on for this decision and work through it.

**Gelatin.** *Moves:* its value in increasing the digestibility and nutritional value of milk was proven at the Mellon Institute of Industrial Research; gelatin was then used and tested by certain hospitals and school systems; the favorable results were projected to other leaders in the field, who followed that group leadership. "The idea carried momentum." *Principle:* research authority, then a pilot with leading institutions, then projection of the results to their peers.

**Service shoes.** *Problem:* a maker of shoes for patrolmen, firemen and letter carriers wanted to sell more of them. *Moves:* make acceptable the idea that men in such work ought to be well shod, which also furthers their efficiency; organize a **foot protection bureau** as part of the business, disseminating scientifically accurate information on foot care (principles built into the shoes). *Result:* civic bodies and police and fire chiefs furthered the ideas and the product, and more shoes sold more easily. *Principle:* an information service that advances the public good the product serves enlists the leaders who care about that good.

**Toothpaste.** *Problem:* one or two large manufacturers claimed advantages no toothpaste can have; the honest competitor must either exaggerate further or lose his market. *Moves:* use propaganda through the dental clinics, schools, women's clubs, medical colleges, dental press and daily press to bring the public "the truth of what a toothpaste can do." *Principle:* when competitors' advertising is exaggerated, answer through other channels than advertising, by establishing the basic truths with the authorities.

## Associating the client with what the public esteems

**Jackson Heights** (real estate). *Moves:* to show the development was socially desirable, a benefit performance of the Jitney Players for Japanese earthquake victims under the auspices of Mrs. Astor; a golf course and planned clubhouse; the post office opening tied to a date important in the annals of the American Postal Service; a competition among interior decorators for the best-furnished apartment, decided by an important committee of judges and publicized to millions. *Principle:* the **associative process**, in which each event attaches the place to society, beauty or national significance.

**Cheney silk at the Louvre.** *Moves:* American silk goes to Paris for a stamp of authority. Cheney Brothers silks, thirty designs inspired by the French ironworker Edgar Brandt (maker of the Verdun memorial doors), in 2,500 yards of printed silks, tinsels and cut velvets in a hundred colors, became the first American art materials shown in the Louvre's Decorative Arts Section, with the Minister of Fine Arts as patron. *Result:* department stores in New York, Chicago and other cities asked for the exhibition, and a quantity-produced product "gained a place in public esteem by being associated with the work of a recognized artist and with a great art museum." *Principle:* tie a commercial product to a recognized artist and institution, and taste-leading stores follow.

**Metropolitan Life.** It "tried to make a majority movement of itself," reaching the public "at every point of its corporate and separate existences": health surveys and expert counsel to communities, health creeds and advice to individuals, and its building made a picturesque landmark "to carry on the associative process." *Result:* broad general acceptance and steadily growing policies. *Principle:* the client's every contact, even its building, is a carrier of association.

**Modernist pianos.** A piano maker had artists design modernist pianos, expecting to sell few. "In order to draw attention to pianos one must have something more than a piano. People at tea parties will not talk about pianos; but they may talk about the new modernist piano." *Principle:* make a talking-point product that carries attention to the line.

## Using group formations to spread an idea

**Ivory soap sculpture.** *Moves:*
1. Start from a fact: a sculptor of national reputation found Ivory soap an excellent medium.
2. Procter and Gamble offered prizes for the best sculpture in white soap, under the auspices of the Art Center in New York, an organization of high standing.
3. School superintendents and teachers encouraged it as an educational aid; contests ran between schools, districts and cities.
4. It suited homes: mothers saved the shavings and failed efforts for laundry, and the work was clean.
5. The best local pieces went to an annual national contest at an important New York gallery, whose prestige and distinguished judges made it a serious art event.

*Result:* about 500 entries in the first national competition, 2,500 in the third, more than 4,000 in the fourth, and the soap became the intimate concern of children as well as housewives. The motives engaged were the aesthetic, the competitive, the gregarious, the snobbish, the exhibitionist and the maternal, all "put in concerted motion by the simple machinery of group leadership and authority." *Principle:* the sponsors lent their names because the contest served an interest they had at heart, the cultivation of the aesthetic impulse in the young.

**The railway and its right of way.** Survey where the railway's interests coincide with its prospective customers'; establish relations with the chambers of commerce along the line; help them secure new plants and industries; disseminate technical information. These are not favors traded for favors: they "actually promote growth on its right of way", and the interests "mutually interact and feed one another." Parallels: a bank's investment service gives customers more money to deposit; a jeweler's insurance department makes buyers feel secure; a baking company's recipe service creates new uses for bread.

## Going indirect

**Luggage.** The attitude of railroads, steamship companies and foreign government-owned railroads governed how luggage was handled. Educate baggage men, in their own interest, to handle it faster with less damage; get steamship companies to relax restrictions and foreign governments to ease baggage costs to further tourism; and the luggage makers profit. "The public relations campaign was directed not to the public, who were the ultimate consumers, but to these other elements." Also, educating the public on what to wear on trips and when sells clothing and luggage together.

**Cosmetics.** A campaign against unhealthy cosmetics might be waged as a fight for a return to the wash-cloth and soap, which health officials would logically take up. "Propaganda, since it goes to basic causes, can very often be most effective through the manner of its introduction."

## Answering rumor with an event

**Hudson Motor** (the failure case). While directors met on a dividend, a false report that only the regular dividend had been declared went out on the ticker; the stock fell, with losses estimated at $500,000 to $1,000,000, until official news of an increased dividend and a 20 per cent stock distribution sent it up more than 6 points. *Principle:* without prompt technical handling, a single rumor at a critical moment is enormously costly.

**Beech-Nut.** Rumor said Bartlett Arkell would sell Beech-Nut to the Postum Cereal Company for $17,000,000 and take the industry from its birthplace. He denied it publicly in terms loyal to his home town, saying he would never sell "to any one at any price." The Mohawk Valley then held "Beech-Nut Day", with more than 3,000 people, a citizens' committee and the backing of the local chambers, in his honor. "A denial would not have carried as much conviction." *Principle:* a community event makes the truth self-evident where a denial only argues.

## Dramatizing an issue so it argues itself

**Low tariff.** The old way is to say on the radio "Vote for me and low tariff, because the high tariff increases the cost of the things you [buy]." The propagandist's way, with radio as one instrument of a plan:
1. Stage a low-tariff exhibition simultaneously in twenty cities, with exhibits illustrating the added cost due to the tariff.
2. Have it ceremoniously inaugurated by prominent men and women interested in a low tariff apart from the candidate's fortunes.
3. Have groups especially affected by the high cost of living agitate for lower schedules.
4. Have prominent men boycott woolens and go to important functions in cotton suits until the wool schedule is reduced.
5. Get social workers' opinion on whether the high cost of wool endangers the health of the poor in winter.
6. Only then speak, to a public already keyed to the subject.

**The Consumer's Committee of Women** fighting the "American valuation" tariff rented an empty store on Fifty-Seventh Street in New York and tagged each item of merchandise with its current price and the price it would cost if the tariff passed. Hundreds of visitors rallied to the cause.

**The NAACP convention** (against lynching, Jim Crowism and civil discrimination in the South). *Moves:*
1. Dramatize the year's campaign in an annual convention that concentrates attention on the problem.
2. Hold it in the South, because "a point of view on a southern [question], emanating from a southern center, would have greater authority", especially when it is at odds with the traditional southern view. Atlanta was chosen.
3. "Surround the conference with people who were stereotypes for ideas that carried weight all over the country": letters and telegrams asked leaders of religious, political, social and educational groups for their view of its purpose. Above all secure southern leaders, even from Atlanta itself; a group of ministers who had come out for interracial amity agreed to cooperate.
4. Program: "Negroes and white men from the South, on the same platform, expressed the same point of view," with a dramatic element spotlighted, a national leader from Massachusetts agreeing with a Baptist preacher from the South.
5. Afterward, mail reports and letters to selected groups, using the event as a weapon for a wider circle.

*Result:* national press coverage; southern editorials took up the subject because southern leaders took part; many southern editors came to see the question as "not only an emotional one, but also a discussable one"; the conference built racial consciousness and solidarity, and the decline in lynching is very probably partly its result.

## Timing and the press

**Masaryk.** Czechoslovakia officially became a free state on Monday, October 28, 1918, instead of on a Sunday, because the world's press would have more space for it on a Monday morning. "I would be making history for the cables if I changed the date." *Principle:* time the event to the medium.

**The Dodge Victory Six broadcast.** A nationwide hook-up of 47 stations and 20,000 miles of wire, costing more than $60,000, with Al Jolson, Will Rogers, Fred and Dorothy Stone and Paul Whiteman ($25,000 in artists' fees), carried a four-minute address by the president of Dodge Brothers to an estimated thirty million Americans: "a sugar-coated sales message." To the objection that a star's fee adds cost but nothing to the product, Bernays replies that every mode of appeal spends money to make itself attractive, as print does with pictures and endorsements.

## Personality misused and well used

**The Prince of Wales** reaped "bales of clippings and little additional glory" from his American visit because he was poorly advised: he became a well-dressed, sport-loving, dancing, perhaps frivolous youth. Only a late trip in the New York subway, a venture into the serious business of daily life, added dignity. Serious studies of American life, like those of Prince Gustave of Sweden, would have presented him as what he constitutionally is, "a symbol of the unity of the British Empire." *Principle:* publicity volume is not the goal. Dramatize the person in the function he represents.

**Coolidge in Indian regalia**, photographed with chiefs at the climax of a "greatly overreported vacation", shows that "a public personality can be made absurd by misuse of the very mechanism which helped create it."

**Coolidge's breakfast for actors** reached not only actors as a group but audiences: everyone who likes amusement and the people who provide it.

## Institutions

**The orphan asylum** losing contributions: the public subconsciously criticizes it for not being organized on the "cottage plan", so the advice is to modify the asylum.

**The jewelry store** whose patronage was shrinking because of its high-priced reputation: feature medium-priced goods, even at a loss, because out of a hundred medium-price customers acquired today a certain percentage will be well-to-do ten years from now.

**The Fifth Avenue bank branch**, opened because it "correctly expresses the kind of appeal which it wishes to make to future depositors": the polite doorman and clean floors matter as much as an able manager, and a scandal involving the president's wife can cancel it all.

**The telephone companies** explain the scientific care taken to choose distinguishable exchange names, so the public appreciates the effort and cooperates by enunciating clearly.

**The Harvard professor** who solved the Venus calendar of the ancient Mayas reached the headlines only because a young publicist, James W. D. Seymour, took the initiative; left alone, his findings would have stayed in technical journals, as unintelligible to the public as Mayan hieroglyphics.

**Columbia's Casa Italiana**, solemnly inaugurated before representatives of the Italian government to emphasize the university's standing in Latin and Romance studies, and **Harvard's Germanic Museum**, ceremoniously opened by Prince Henry of Prussia, show the ceremonial inauguration as high-spotting.

**The Greek vase and the Bronx apartment.** A Bronx housewife cares little for a Greek vase in the Metropolitan Museum, but an artist at a pottery firm can adapt its design to a set of china, priced low by quantity production, that reaches her apartment and develops "unconsciously, through its fine line and color, an appreciation of beauty."
