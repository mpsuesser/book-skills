# Narratives and the PR/FAQ

Amazon's two written forms for thinking and deciding: the *narrative* memo, which replaced slides in meetings, and the *PR/FAQ*, which starts every new idea from the customer. "A narrative is simply a written document that forces clarity of thought." Both are written to the standards in `SKILL.md` step 6.

## Why narratives replace slides

On June 9, 2004, at 6:02 p.m., Bezos emailed his senior team (the S-Team): "No PowerPoint presentations from now on at S-Team." Short narrative memos replaced the slides. Narratives gave life to Prime, AWS, Kindle and Fulfillment by Amazon. "Narrative is to Amazon what an engine is to a Ferrari." Newcomers call it "the weirdest meeting culture you will ever encounter."

- **Bullets hide the reasoning.** Edward Tufte (*The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint*): the bullet style "usually weakens verbal and spatial reasoning, and almost always corrupts statistical analysis." "By leaving out the narrative between the points, the bullet outline ignores and conceals the causal assumptions and analytic structure of the reasoning." Bullets "might be useful now and then… but sentences with subjects and verbs are usually better." Slide templates may lift the worst 10 to 20 percent of speakers at the cost of intellectual damage to the other 80 percent, and they let "speakers to pretend they are giving a real talk, and audiences to pretend that they are listening."
- **Hierarchy buries the critical fact.** Before the 2003 *Columbia* disaster, a piece of foam struck the wing eighty-two seconds after launch. Boeing analyzed it in three reports and twenty-eight slides. One slide stacked six levels of hierarchy (title; very big bullet; dash; diamond; little bullet; parenthesis), carried an optimistic title, and buried the warning in a tiny low-level fragment. The investigating board concluded that "as information gets passed up an organization hierarchy, key explanations and supporting information is filtered out." Tufte: "Serious problems require serious tools." Replace slides with paper handouts that show words, numbers, data and images together.
- **Bezos's reason:** writing a good four-page memo is harder than "writing" a twenty-page deck, because the narrative structure "forces better thought and better understanding of what's more important than what, and how things are related." Slides give "permission to gloss over ideas, flatten out any sense of relative importance, and ignore the interconnectedness of ideas."

**The narrative is a mechanism.** "Good intentions never work, you need good mechanisms to make anything happen." Asking people to try harder on a recurring problem asks for good intentions they already have, so nothing changes. A *mechanism* is a repeatable process or tool that aligns actions and decisions with the Leadership Principles; it is introduced, adopted and audited. Two-pizza teams and single-threaded leaders are mechanisms. So is the narrative.

**Length.** The first attempts were "laughably poor": forty pages, or six pages gamed with tight margins and tiny fonts. The limit became six pages at most, with an appendix allowed: "long enough to express the idea—and not a sentence more. If two pages are all it takes… stick to two." There is no formal template. Narratives force the writer "to think and synthesize more deeply," and the team reviews and critiques each one. "It's a daunting task… and it should be."

## Five strategies for great narratives

**1. Focus on the narrative, not the six pages.** Write in topic sentences, verbs and nouns, not bullet points, and no longer than the idea needs; many are one page. Procter & Gamble's Richard Deupree (who invented the soap opera) asked for a one-page memo: "Boil it down to something I can grasp." "I don't understand complicated problems. I only understand simple ones." "Part of my job is to train people to break down an involved question into a series of simple matters. Then we can all act intelligently." The P&G one-page memo has five elements, and the form works for emails, memos, pitches and even TV commercials:

| Element | What it holds |
|---|---|
| Idea Summary | One sentence: what are you proposing? (your logline) |
| Perspective | A summary of the situation: facts, trends, issues |
| How It Works | The details: how, what, who, when, where |
| Key Benefits | Three benefits, ideally strategic and profitable (the rule of three) |
| Next Steps | What actions, by whom, by when |

**2. Stick to headings and subheadings.** Richard Feynman wrote a six-hundred-page physics book with only two levels: titles and subheadings. On the *Challenger* commission he complained about "little goddamn bullets," then explained the O-ring failure by dropping a sample into ice water. The *Feynman technique*: write the concept on paper in your own words as you would explain it to someone else, in complete sentences with nouns and verbs. "You can recognize truth by its beauty and simplicity."

**3. Don't rush it.** Like the handstand (see `metaphor-and-analogy.md`), a good memo takes a week or more, not a day or two. Clear writing reflects clear thinking, and the biggest mistake is not spending enough time on the writing. A great memo reads with "the clarity of angels singing."

**4. Collaborate to communicate.** Six-pagers go out with no author names, because they are a team effort. In his 2017 letter, Bezos says the difference between a great memo and an average one is "squishy"; readers know it when they see it. You don't have to be a skilled writer yourself. A football coach doesn't need to throw and a film director doesn't need to act, but both must recognize high standards and teach realistic expectations on scope. "*Someone* on the team needs to have the skill, but it doesn't have to be you." Brad Porter: "Precision counts." Preparing the memo forces the team to understand the space, the data and the operating tenets deeply, so an executive can internalize a new area in about thirty minutes of reading.

**5. Hold study hall.** Hand out printed copies at the meeting and not before. Everyone reads silently at the start, the *study hall*. (Microsoft's version uses a shared document with real-time comments and "+1"s.) Budget three minutes a page. A six-pager takes eighteen to twenty minutes, leaving about forty minutes of a one-hour meeting for discussion. In a thirty-minute meeting, a two-pager takes about six minutes, leaving about twenty for challenging the arguments, questioning the tactics, giving feedback and agreeing on next steps. Bezos is usually the last to finish reading. "He assumes each sentence is wrong until he can prove it otherwise," challenging the content of the sentence, not the motive of the writer.

**Drill 13: write the narrative before the slides.** PowerPoint is not a storytelling tool. Write the narrative first, with a theme, titles and subtitles, and fully formed sentences with nouns, verbs and objects. The slides *complement* the story.

Other leaders adopted it. John Mackey at Whole Foods found the six-pager "the start of a fruitful conversation" that balanced gut instinct with rigor. Ronny Kohavi took narratives to Microsoft as a "forcing function"; the silent start surprised people and then spread, the way A/B testing had grown from zero to about twenty thousand experiments a year: "It catches on and scales." Kohavi: "Storytelling is part of your job": translate the pattern you found in the data into a narrative that convinces others to act. Porter: a meeting where everyone has deep context "is magical."

**Drill 14: study the shareholder letters.** Read Bezos's letters for 1997, 2006, 2013, 2014, 2017 and 2020. In each, look for the theme, the clear logical order, the supporting stories and data, and the metaphorical language.

## The PR/FAQ: working backwards

When Bill Carr's team brought SWOT analyses, forecasts and spreadsheets to propose a digital-media business, Bezos asked, "Where are the mock-ups?" He wanted to see the whole customer journey, and he pressed: how is this different from iTunes, what will an ebook cost, what device? "How, exactly, would Amazon's digital offerings be better for customers than anything currently available?" "To Jeff, a half-baked mock-up was evidence of half-baked thinking." He sent the ten executives off to each write a narrative of their best idea, with no spreadsheets or slides. The ideas included an ebook reader, an MP3 player and an "Amazon Puck" (later the Echo Dot). Writing "freed executives from the quantitative demands of Excel and the visual seduction of PowerPoint."

Then came the inversion: "Let's write the press release first." Most companies hand the press release to marketing at the end. *Working backwards* writes it before anything is built, from the customer's point of view, and asks *why* customers will love the product. It answers the reader's *So what? What does it mean to me?* The future press release is a mechanism. An FAQ is attached for internal challenges and technical hurdles; the combination is the *PR/FAQ*, and the FAQ is optional. Prime, Prime Video, Amazon Studios, Amazon Music, AmazonSmile, Marketplace, Echo and Alexa, and Fulfillment by Amazon all started as PR/FAQs. Andy Jassy: "Before we write a line of code, we start with the press release," designed to flush out the benefits and make sure "you're really solving the customer problem."

**Oprah-speak.** Oprah Winfrey called the Kindle her "favorite, favorite thing": think of a book and have it in sixty seconds, with no PC and no wireless contract. *Oprah-speak* means writing as if you were on the couch across from Oprah, describing the product to her and to millions of viewers with broad knowledge. *Geek-speak* is fine inside the building; Oprah-speak is the language of the masses.

### The six elements

A future press release is written, debated, rewritten and debated again, and first drafts are messy. The Kindle release is the model.

1. **Headline.** The trumpet that heralds the arrival, one or two lines. It says *who* is announcing *what*, with the product name when there is one. It need not be a product: "Amazon.com Announces CEO Transition."
2. **Subheading.** The first sentence under the title. It states the most compelling customer benefit or differentiator in concise, everyday language. This is the hook and the logline, thirty words or fewer (the Kindle's was 29).
3. **Summary paragraph.** Open with the location and date. Dating even a hypothetical release forces a conversation about feasibility. Then summarize the product and its benefits. "Put 80 percent of your creative energy into the title, subtitle, and summary paragraph because 80 percent of your readers will stop reading at this point."
4. **Problem paragraph.** Explain the problem the product solves; a quote is optional (the Kindle release used Bezos). The release must raise a problem, "otherwise, there's no reason for the solution."
5. **Solution and benefits paragraphs (three to six).** Go deeper: how the product resolves the customer's problems simply and delightfully, how it works, and how easy it is to start. Keep paragraphs to three or four sentences and call out each benefit with a bold subhead (the Kindle's included *No Monthly Wireless Bills*, *Reads Like Paper* and *Long Battery Life*). Be selective. The whole release fits on a page: "If you've written one and a half pages, it's too long."
6. **Partnership highlights, executive quotes or customer testimonials.** Engaging quotes from spokespeople, partners and customers. Write hypothetical customer quotes even when the idea is "nothing more than a sparkle in your eye." They show precisely why customers will love it. "If the quotes are weak, then it's likely the idea fails to deliver substantial value to a large enough customer base."

**The questions it forces.** How will customers interact with the product? How is it different from anything that exists today? Which features will customers find most appealing? Why will customers love it?

**Example: Amazon Books (2015).** Jennifer Cast was asked to think about small physical stores. The PR/FAQ is "a big commitment that takes time and tenacity": hers took six weeks, more than 120 hours and at least twelve drafts, and won approval in one ninety-minute meeting.

- **Headline:** "Amazon Opens Offline Bookstores with Online Features and Benefits"
- **Subheading:** "Stores Include Amazon's Full Line of Devices and Offer Customers Same Low Prices as Amazon.com."
- **First paragraph:** the opening, the location and the benefits.
- **Second paragraph:** there was no real customer "problem," so she used a hypothetical Bezos quote that highlighted how the store differed.
- **Paragraphs three to six:** details that guided the designers.
- **Quotes:** hypothetical customers praising books displayed face-out with ratings, the tablets and Fire TV Stick to try, and the app.

The Leadership Principle reads *Customer Obsession*, not "service" or "focus," and the PR/FAQ is where that obsession is tested.

**Drill 15: draft a mock press release.** Fill in, in order:

1. Topic
2. Headline (who is announcing what)
3. Subheading (the hook: concise, under thirty words)
4. First paragraph (summary)
5. Second paragraph (the problem)
6. Paragraphs three to six (the details, and how the product solves the problem)
7. Company quotes and customer testimonials, even if the customers don't exist yet

### Running the review

- **The red pen.** The AWS team spent eighteen months writing and refining PR/FAQs with Bezos before writing code. Engineers complained; AWS became the fastest Amazon business to reach $10 billion. Brad Stone describes Bezos's red pen "crossing out anything that does not speak simply and positively to customers." Bezos "didn't believe anyone could make a good decision about a feature or a product without knowing precisely how it would be communicated to the world." His note on one dull draft: "I'm already bored." He pushed for a punchier headline.
- **The Prime launch.** The 2005 letter to customers was drafted months before launch, during development. It runs about two hundred words: "I am very excited to announce Amazon Prime, our first ever membership program, which provides 'all-you-can-eat' express shipping." "It's simple." For a flat annual fee, "unlimited two-day shipping for free on over a million in-stock items"; two-day shipping becomes "an everyday experience rather than an occasional indulgence." It closes with a 1-Click call to action.
- **Pitching with it.** John, a medical-technology executive, went from skeptic to evangelist. Given sixty minutes with his CEO, he built a twenty-minute presentation and gave forty minutes back: "Short presentations are almost always more persuasive than long ones." He extended working backwards by opening with the future TV ad, a two-minute video with actors as patients, then said, "Now that you've seen the vision, let me show you how we'll get there." The hour-long meeting ended in about thirty minutes with his budget approved, and he was later promoted to lead the project as a division president.
- **Zane's three tests** (a product manager with a one-page limit, reviewed quarterly). If you can't explain the problem your idea solves in a sentence or two, you don't understand the problem well enough. If you can't state the customer benefits in a few sentences and show, with quotes, why customers will love it, you don't know your customer well enough. If you can't explain how the product is different, you will struggle to build support internally. With remote work, writing matters more. Product managers act as "mini CEOs," adapting one message for four or five audiences: engineering language for engineers, numbers for the CFO, benefits for sales.

**Backcasting.** NASA planned the moon landing backward from the result: get a rocket off the ground, then reach orbit, walk in space, rendezvous and dock, circle the moon, and land. Ozan Varol: "Instead of letting our resources drive our vision, backcasting lets our vision drive the resources." Working backwards is the same move on paper, and "working backwards is the best way to build the future." Good writers stand out.
