# The Bezos Blueprint

Gallo calls Bezos "the world's greatest salesman" because he "sells dreams, not products." He analyzed all twenty-four shareholder letters Bezos wrote as CEO (1997–2020, 48,062 words) and interviewed former Amazon executives and the CEOs who copied his methods. The *blueprint* is the set of communication tools Bezos built for Amazonians to write, pitch, decide and present. Like an architect's blueprint, it is a model that scales: others can follow it without the designer in the room.

The premises the whole book runs on:

- **Selling is everything.** Professionals spend 40 percent of their working time persuading, influencing, motivating and convincing (Dan Pink), whatever their title. In 1994, before Amazon had a name, Bezos's first job ad listed only one skill as essential for every role: "Top-notch communication skills."
- **Writing is a skill anyone can sharpen.** Between 1997 and 2020, Bezos's average sentence fell from 20 words to 16 and his letters' grade level from 10 to 8, while the letters grew from 1,600 to 4,033 words. "Great communicators are made, not born."
- **The tools changed; the brain did not.** The brain's job is to conserve energy, not to think. Every unfamiliar word, acronym, convoluted sentence and new idea adds to the reader's load, and a reader who carries too much gives up. Kahneman: "do not use complex language where simpler language will do." Cognitive ease pleases people, and pleased people back your idea.
- **Day One** is a mindset, not a strategy: keep the beginner's mind and the start-up's urgency however large you grow. Day Two is "stasis. Followed by irrelevance. Followed by excruciating, painful decline. Followed by death." Bezos ended every letter from 2009 with "It's still Day 1" (from 2016, "It remains Day 1").

**Simplicity** is the theme of every tool: "Simplicity is all about knowing and selecting: knowing your audience and selecting the information your audience needs to know." "Simplicity is an act of selection, not compression." Simple language is not dumbing down: "When you express complex ideas simply, you're not 'dumbing down' the content; you're outsmarting the competition."

## Building any message

Every piece of communication, from a fifteen-second pitch to a six-page memo, goes through these steps. Each step is done when its output is written down.

**1. Know your audience.** "Great communicators don't start with what *they* know; they start with what the *audience* knows." Answer three questions (Drill 2):

- **Who** is your target audience? Warren Buffett drafts each letter to "Dear Doris and Bertie," his sisters: smart, invested, not in the business, imagined as just back from a year away. Name your Doris and Bertie.
- **What** do they need to know that they don't know already? Not everything *you* know.
- **Why** should they care? "Nobody cares about your ideas. They care about how your idea will help them lead a better life."

Every audience, boss, investor, customer or employee, is asking the question Andy Grove put to Clay Christensen: *What does it mean to me?* Assume ten minutes of full attention at most, and fifteen seconds (about thirty-five words) to hold a reader before 45 percent drop out.

**2. Write the logline.** A *logline* (Hollywood's term, from the title and one sentence written on the spine, or log, of a script) is "a concise and compelling sentence that answers the question: What is my story about?" It runs twenty-five to thirty words at most, can be said in fifteen seconds or less, and ten words is better. Examples: "Amazon is Earth's most customer-centric company" (6 words); "Google organizes the world's information and makes it universally accessible" (10). One billionaire investor: "If an entrepreneur cannot express their idea in one sentence, I'm not interested—period."

Find it with the **So what? test** (Drill 6). Accept that you are too close to your idea and suffer from "the curse of knowledge." State the topic, then ask *So what?* three times, writing each answer down; each answer gets closer to the one thing the audience wants to know. Apple's M1 chip: first Apple chip for the Mac → *So what?* first system on a chip → *So what?* sixteen billion transistors, fastest CPU core → *So what?* "a gigantic leap forward for the Mac, delivering more power, faster performance, and longer battery life." The last answer is the logline: "details are not loglines. Loglines deliver the big picture."

Brevity comes from starting with one big idea and selecting the stories, examples and data that support it, never from squeezing everything you know into less time. "The secret to catching a person's attention is not to cut through the noise but to boost the signal."

**3. Put the bottom line up front.** *BLUF* (bottom line up front, a U.S. Army writing technique), taught at Amazon as *BLOT* (bottom line on top): the most crucial thing the reader needs to know, the first thing they see or hear. If they read nothing else, they have the big picture. Amazonians bold it at the top of emails: one or two sentences saying why the reader is on the email and why they should care. Bezos's CEO-transition email opened with its whole news in one sentence: "I'm excited to announce that this Q3 I'll transition to Executive Chair of the Amazon Board and Andy Jassy will become CEO." Then came why, what next, and what Amazon had done. When the message is a call to action, frame it as **Commander's Intent**: it answers who, what, when, where and why, opens and closes the briefing, and begins "The single most important thing we must accomplish is…" It is a sentence with nouns and verbs, never a bullet list. "Brevity clarifies, and clarity inspires."

**4. Build the Message Map.** The *Gallo Method* puts your story on one page: a logline, three supporting messages, and the stories, data or analogies that prove each one. It scales from a fifteen-second pitch to a fifteen-minute presentation. The procedure (Drill 20):

1. Draft the logline: *What is the one most important thing I want my audience to know?* Specific, clear, concise; if it can't fit in a 140-character post, it's too long.
2. Craft three messages that support the logline. None is important enough to *replace* it.
3. Bring each message to life with a story, a data point or an analogy.

The template reads: Logline at the top → three supporting points (Key Point 1, 2, 3) → under each, Story / Data / Analogy. Start writing and wordsmith later, and get colleagues' input. Then memorize the map for a pitch or interview, use it as the outline for slides, share it with the team, and send it to whoever writes your web and marketing copy. UNTUCKit's map: "Shirts designed to be worn untucked," supported by perfect length, fitted size and contoured hemline. The map was on the walls of more than eighty stores. One CEO's "well-positioned to capitalize on market trends affecting the underlying business" became "Our company is healthier and stronger than ever," backed by three messages chosen to tell investors what they want to know, what they need to know, and what they should know but perhaps don't.

**The rule of three** governs step 2: the brain cannot *easily* hold more than three things in short-term memory. Georgetown researchers found that "three charms but four alarms": one product claim fails to convince, two are better, three persuade, and at four or more consumers grow *less* impressed. Investors spend about three minutes on a pitch deck and most of it on three slides (solution, product, team). Bezos speaks in threes: "Put the customer first. Invent. And be patient."; "selection, convenience, and low prices"; "Work hard, have fun, make history."

**6. Revise to Amazon-style precision.** Make the big idea **clear, concise and specific**, to the standards Amazon teaches in its writing classes:

- **Clear:** active voice, so it's plain who is doing what; no jargon; a Flesch-Kincaid grade level of 8 or lower and a Flesch reading ease of 50 or higher (Drill 4); the logline passes the *So what?* test.
- **Concise:** sentences of twenty words or fewer; cut every word the meaning doesn't need. "Third-party sellers are kicking our first party butt. Badly." (8 words) beats a 29-word version that says the same.
- **Specific:** replace vague *weasel words* with facts. "Nearly all customers" → "87 percent of Prime members"; "significantly better" → "an increase of 25 basis points"; "some time ago" → "three months ago." Amazon announcements open with the number, the place and the audience: "Amazon's new one-million-square-foot fulfillment center in Oklahoma City will create 500 jobs."

The model is Bezos's 620-word CEO-transition email: grade level 7.8, 13.1 words per sentence, reading ease 62.4, 6.3 percent passive sentences. It covered twenty-seven years in two minutes of reading. "If you cannot express it in one clear, concise, and specific sentence, no one will pay attention."
