# Delivery, mission, symbols, reading

How the written message becomes a spoken one, and how a leader makes a group carry it. "Great presenters are made, not born."

## The AMP model

About 73 percent of people fear public speaking (*glossophobia*). Avoiding a fear gives it power, and exposure takes the power away. The skill pays: 65 percent of recruiters weigh writing and verbal skills over a candidate's major, 70 percent of professionals call presentations critical to their success, and Warren Buffett says public speaking can raise a person's value by 50 percent.

The Gallo *AMP model* ("amp" as in *to excite and energize*) has three variables:

| Variable | Fixed? | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Ability** | Constant | Natural strengths, the foundation: being comfortable speaking in front of others, deep knowledge of the content, creativity with words, imagery, metaphor and art, a strong resonant voice, ease with humor, good posture. |
| **Message** | Not constant | The content: theme, clarity, word choice, stories, slides, visuals. A strong message has emotionally engaging content (stories, images, videos), a short, clearly expressed theme, three supporting examples, short sentences in the active voice, visually engaging slides, stories that grab attention, and a simple sequence that is easy to follow. |
| **Practice** | Not constant | Rehearse until you have internalized the key message on each slide and can deliver it without notes; until it sounds as conversational as dinner with friends; until you are comfortable with any demonstration (how it works, how long it takes); and until your stories are short, relevant and moving. |

**Run it in order.** First identify the speaker's *Ability* from a video sample, before coaching anything else. Then set the mix:

- A speaker with **more natural strengths** spends about 50 percent on Ability, 30 percent on Message and 20 percent on Practice.
- A speaker with **fewer natural strengths** spends about 20 percent on Ability, 30 percent on Message and 50 percent on Practice.

The two end up equally great presenters. Both spend 30 percent on the message, and the recipes vary from person to person. Build on the strengths and invest in the other two variables. "The key to becoming an authentic and charismatic speaker is not to change who you are"; "*time* is what makes communicators great." "Practice is not something you do because you're bad; practice is something you do because you are good." Rehearsing a presentation at least ten times gives a speaker the confidence to own the room.

**Bezos's evolution.**

- **1998, Lake Forest College.** His strengths were already there:
  - *Creativity:* the catalog "the size of more than forty New York City phone books," and customers trying to "sip from a fire hose."
  - *Humor:* "just head west and call us tomorrow."
  - *Topic knowledge:* web growth of 2,300 percent a year, which "outside petri dishes" doesn't happen.

  His weaknesses were long-windedness and reading from notes, stumbling and losing his place.
- **2003, TED.** He gave the electricity analogy with embedded video, made fun of old inventions (the electric tie press), and used specific numbers for credibility: six hundred ships clogging San Francisco's harbor during the gold rush. In the first sixty seconds, his fillers ("uh," "um") fell from seven in 1998 to one. He relied less on notes and spoke in shorter, tighter sentences.
- **2019, Blue Origin keynote.** He was unhurried, paused after big ideas, and delivered sharp lines: "Earth is the best planet. It is not close." "It's time to go back to the moon. This time to stay." "Big things start small."

Steve Jobs showed the same arc. In his first TV interview in 1978 his eyes darted and he fidgeted, but the strengths were visible: assertive language, humor, a dynamic voice and concise phrasing. By the 2007 iPhone launch, practiced for weeks, he used all of them:

- assertive: "We are going to get rid of all these buttons and just make a giant screen."
- humor: a rotary-dial phone on screen, and "Yuck!" for the stylus
- vocal delivery: pauses, three products repeated faster and faster, "are you getting it?"
- concise: "Today, Apple is going to reinvent the phone."

**Drill 16: video yourself.** Record a practice run of the presentation, pitch or interview on a phone. Rate it yourself and with a trusted friend. Look for:

- **Natural strengths** to play up: creative language, strong writing or slides, posture, vocal tone and variation, creative stories.
- **Too many words:** which sentences can go?
- **Slides** with too much text or too small a font: "If you can't read the text, neither can the audience."
- **Filler words** (*uh, um, ah*) and tag endings (*you know? right?*). Cut more of them each session.
- **The logline:** is it clear, and do you say it the same way every time?

### Slides built from the Message Map

Build slides from the Message Map in `SKILL.md` step 4. Keep every slide's text at 30 points or larger, because bigger text forces fewer words. The 14-slide layout:

1. the logline
2. Key Point 1, followed by three slides of supporting points
3. Key Point 2, followed by three slides of supporting points
4. Key Point 3, followed by three slides of supporting points
5. the logline, repeated

Colin, who ran a sixty-person team at a European financial firm, adopted it. Prep time fell by half, decks went from thirty slides to ten, prep meetings went from three thirty-minute sessions to one, and forty-five-minute client updates became twenty minutes of presenting plus twenty of engagement.

## Make the mission your mantra

The word *customer* appears about five hundred times across Bezos's letters, twenty-five times in the 1997 letter alone. His 1998 mission, to be "the world's most customer-centric company," became a mantra every employee could recite, and Bezos served as its "repeater in chief."

**Overcommunicate by a factor of ten.** John Kotter found that most leaders *under*-communicate their vision by a factor of ten. "Without credible communication, and a lot of it, the hearts and minds of the troops are never captured." Bezos said "customer" sixty-two times in his first speech in 1998. In a seven-minute CNBC interview in 1999 he referred to the mission twenty-one times, about once every twenty-four seconds ("Internet, schminternet"). He told employees to be "terrified, not of our competition, but our customers," and he hired *missionaries* (people who believe in the mission) over mercenaries.

A *mantra* is "a statement or slogan that builds in strength as it's repeated. Overcommunication fuels its impact." It runs on the *mere-exposure effect*: the more often people hear something, the more they like it, internalize it and act on it. The mantra "takes a mental highlighter to the mission." Every handoff from person to person and department to department dilutes a message, and remote work dilutes it more. Clarify the mission and repeat it until you are tired of hearing yourself say it. You'll know it has landed when people on your team use your words and act on them: "You've created missionaries."

**Drill 17: flag the key message.** Start the sentence with "If there's one thing to know, it's this…" (Bezos: "If there's one thing to know about Amazon, it's that it pays 'obsessive attention to the customer, end to end.'"). The audience remembers it and writes it down. Alternatives: "The most important thing you need to know is…"; "If there's one thing you can take away from this presentation, it's this…"; "What I can tell you is this…". "Your audience is looking for road maps."

**Drill 18: condense the mission to five seconds.** Make the mission clear, compelling and repeatable. Use precise words in conversational language that people are comfortable repeating. Condense it until you can say it in five seconds, twelve words or fewer. Amazon's core is four words: "Earth's most customer-centric company." Others:

- Nike: "To bring inspiration and innovation to every athlete in the world."
- Unilever: "To make sustainable living commonplace."
- Tesla: "To accelerate the world's transition to sustainable transport."
- TED: "To spread ideas."
- Twilio: "To fuel the future of communications."

"Make your mission short, make every word count, and repeat it until you're tired of hearing it. Then repeat it again." Jobs and Guy Kawasaki tested a mission by asking whether it fit on a slide in 190-point type.

**Find the purpose under the product.** John Mackey: "Connecting people to purpose is the first and foremost job of a conscious leader." Find the higher purpose by "discerning the intrinsic good that is at the heart of its value proposition." The purpose is how the work makes the community better and lifts customers' lives, which is more than the product itself. Whole Foods: "to nourish people and the planet." Every company needs *purpose champions*, people who keep that higher purpose alive. "A higher purpose is like a living thing: it must be nurtured." Purpose-driven companies report higher productivity and growth, 30 percent more innovation and 40 percent better retention (Deloitte). Hubert Joly led Best Buy around the "noble purpose of enriching lives through technology," acting as chief storyteller with everyday stories of employees, customers and communities. Model the purpose in everything you do, write and say.

- **Medtronic** shows long versus short. Founder Earl Bakken built a battery-powered pacemaker in four weeks after a 1957 power outage. The mission took two years and many rejected drafts, and was finished in 1962. The full version: "To contribute to human welfare by application of biomedical engineering in the research, design, manufacture, and sale of instruments or appliances that alleviate pain, restore health, and extend life." The short one everyone remembers: "To alleviate pain, restore health, and extend life." Since 1974, new employees have received the mission on a medallion at ceremonies around the world, a physical symbol of it. When employees see a direct line from their work to a real benefit for patients, Bakken said, "it makes people feel positive about their efforts."
- **Apple, 1997.** Returning to a company that was 30 percent gems and 70 percent "lousy," Jobs cleared "the bramble and the brush" and stopped talking about "speeds and feeds, MIPS and megahertz." He asked: *Who is Apple? What do we stand for? Where do we fit in the world? What do we want customers to know about us?* His answer: "Our core value is we believe that people with passion can change the world for the better." "If you do the right things on the top line, the bottom line will follow."

Obsessions find you. Michael Moritz says remarkable people are "obsessed," so captivated by an idea that they have no choice. Share the mission and make the journey irresistible.

## Symbols

"Symbols can be very powerful." Bill Carr names four ingredients of Amazon culture: customer obsession, long-term thinking, eagerness to invent, and pride in operational excellence. Beyond written and spoken words, Bezos used a third tool to carry them: symbols. "A symbol is a thing (image, object, or place) that stands for an idea." Symbols are older than language, wired into the ancient brain, and they let ordinary artifacts express profound ideas. They also engage the senses, and people learn best through several senses at once.

- **Visual symbols** are images or objects people can see and touch: coins, flags, chairs, desks.
  - The *empty chair* sits at the meeting table for the customer, "the most important person in the room," and turns the discussion to *What's best for our customers?*
  - The *door desks*, made from doors on legs, are "a deliberate message… we spend money on things that affect our customers, not on things that don't."
- **Auditory symbols** are stirring music or a group cheer. A bell rung for every sale inspired people at first, grew annoying, and was retired; auditory symbols have short lives.
- **Spatial symbols** are places and spaces with meaning, such as the "Day 1" building name that moved with Bezos from office to office.
- The **10,000-year clock** stands for long-term thinking. Solving world hunger looks impossible in five years and possible in a hundred: "We didn't change the challenge. We changed the time horizon. Time horizons matter." Amazon will stick with an initiative for five, six or seven years; Prime Video took a decade.

Meaning motivates. Mackey: a purpose "must be kept at the forefront of people's awareness," and physical symbols carry it into everyday decisions.

## Leaders are readers

"The hard work of writing is best sharpened on the whetstone of reading" (Admiral James Stavridis). Bezos grew up on science fiction (Verne, Asimov, Heinlein), whose ordinary heroes became his "blueprints for an exciting future." The S-Team held all-day book clubs: "Those books really just become frameworks that we can use to talk about the business." Twenty-seven percent of American adults read no books in a year; reading a book and a half a month puts you among the elite. Stavridis reads at least a hundred a year. Read more books, fiction and nonfiction, than others in your field.

**Four reasons to read more books:**

1. **Books are simulators for the mind.** The brain barely distinguishes reading about an experience from living it, so ask, *What would I have done?*
2. **Books offer perspective.** Bezos spent "10 hours living an alternate life" in *The Remains of the Day*. "You can't do that in a blog post."
3. **Books are condensed knowledge.** A writer's years of work take you about four hours to read. "A book is the single most valuable tool to boost your leadership skills."
4. **Readers are better speakers.** They have more arrows in the quiver (stories, insights, examples) and fresh, surprising ways to explain things. Read widely outside your field: "If you just read subject matter that's only relevant to your game or sport, you will sound like everybody else." "Not all readers are riveting, but all riveting leaders are readers."

**Three ways to read with purpose:**

1. **Follow leaders in relevant categories.** A lifetime of reading is about eight hundred books. "Successful leaders know they can't read everything, so they try to read everything other successful leaders do." Identify your categories, identify the leaders you admire, and read their books, blogs, interviews and articles. David Rubenstein: "you have to have a system."
2. **Take notes.** Active readers encode through more channels and remember more.
3. **Share and talk about your favorite books.** Discussion is the real payoff. Amazon's ideas came out of books: *The Mythical Man-Month* (two-pizza teams), *Good to Great* (the flywheel), *Built to Last* and *Creation* (AWS), *The Innovator's Dilemma* (Kindle), *Made in America* (the Leadership Principles), *The Goal* (bottlenecks), and *Memos from the Chairman* (attaching the 1997 letter every year).

## Invent and wander

Five conditions for creative ideas:

1. **Get enough sleep.** Bezos sleeps eight hours, "putters" in the morning, and holds his first important meeting at ten. Leaders are paid to make a small number of high-quality decisions; three good ones a day is above average.
2. **Stay active.** Walking boosts creative output by about 60 percent on divergent-thinking tests (Stanford), and Jobs brainstormed on walks.
3. **Let your passions choose you.** "You don't get to choose them. They pick you. But you have to be alert to them."
4. **Be a learn-it-all.** Learn and Be Curious, with childlike curiosity.
5. **Cultivate a limitless mindset.** Fight *status quo bias*, the preference for the way things are.

Stay distinctive. "The world wants you to be typical—in a thousand ways, it pulls at you. Don't let it happen." It remains Day 1.
