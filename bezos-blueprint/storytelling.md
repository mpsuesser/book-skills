# Storytelling: three acts, beats, origin stories

Stories reach both the head and the heart (John Doerr), and the most direct path to the heart is a story with an arc. People forget the data in a talk; "they will remember the stories you tell." When the *Wall Street Journal* quoted 350 words of Bezos's 2020 congressional testimony, it quoted only the stories.

## The three-act structure

Aristotle said every story has a beginning, a middle and an end. Screenwriter Syd Field identified (he didn't invent) the *three-act structure* as the foundation of all good stories. "Structure doesn't confine creativity; structure unshackles it." A formula turns out the same thing every time and strips the creativity out. A structure frees it. Know the rules before you break them; James Cameron built *The Terminator* in five acts with a coda.

- **Act 1: the setup.** Introduce the characters, launch the central premise, and show the world and the relationships in it. The first minutes matter most, so the opening scene must hook the audience. *Star Wars* reveals its premise in the first ten minutes.
- **Act 2: the challenge.** The hero is tested by villains, obstacles and conflicts. Aaron Sorkin's version is *intention and obstacle*: "somebody wants something, and someone stands in their way." Field's chain: "Without conflict, you have no action; without action, you have no character; without character, you have no story; and without story, you have no screenplay."
- **Act 3: the resolution.** The hero solves the problem, reaches the dream, and *transforms* themselves or the world for the better. They come home with a treasure or newfound wisdom.

Each act is built from scenes.

## The four beats

Not every story that follows the three acts is good. The difference lies in the scenes, or *beats*: the events that drive the story forward and create suspense, tension and excitement. Four beats carry a business story. For each one, here is the question to answer from your own life or business:

- **Catalyst** (the *inciting incident*): the event that disrupts the status quo and starts the adventure, like the spilled orange juice in *Notting Hill*. *What spark lit your passion?* It might be an event, a problem, a mentor, a book or a trip; Howard Schultz found his in a café in Milan. Share it.
- **Debate:** the hero's doubts and soul-searching. Luke Skywalker refuses the call, then changes his mind about thirty-five minutes in. *When did you doubt? What gave you confidence? How did you overcome the critics and naysayers?* Marc Randolph heard "That will never work" over and over.
- **Fun and Games:** the lighter moments, quirky, surprising or funny, that break the tension (the sorting hat and Quidditch in *Harry Potter*). "Find the fun." Sara Blakely made humor a core value at SPANX.
- **All Is Lost:** the hero is as far as possible from the dream, the "dark night of the soul." Climbing out of it is what gives the story its power to inspire. James Dyson built 5,126 failed prototypes; in a good story, failure is welcomed, not feared.

**Drill 11: find your beats.** For a presentation drawn from your life or business, write one moment for each beat: Catalyst, Debate, Fun and Games, All Is Lost.

### A worked example: Bezos's testimony to Congress (2020)

- **Logline:** "I'm Jeff Bezos. I founded Amazon 26 years ago with the long-term mission of making it Earth's most customer-centric company."
- **Act 1:** His mother, Jackie, had him at seventeen. His father, Miguel, came from Cuba alone through Operation Pedro Pan, wearing a jacket his mother had sewn from cleaning cloths. On his grandfather's ranch he learned "You can invent your way to a better place."
- **Debate:** His boss walked him through Central Park and told him the idea was better for somebody who didn't already have a good job. Bezos decided with his heart: at eighty he wanted as few regrets as possible, and most of our regrets are acts of omission.
- **Fun and Games:** He started in a garage. His parents invested knowing there was a 70 percent chance they would lose the money. It took more than fifty meetings to raise $1 million, and investors asked, "What's the internet?" He drove packages to the post office himself, dreaming of a forklift.
- **All Is Lost:** The press wrote "Amazon.toast" and "Amazon.bomb." His letter opened "Ouch." The stock fell from $116 to $6.
- **Act 3:** Eighty percent of Americans view Amazon favorably, and only their primary physicians and the military are trusted more "to do the right thing." In the closing scene, the origin story becomes a metaphor for American entrepreneurship.

The values learned in the ordinary world of Act 1 are what carry the hero through Act 2.

**Keep the fun anecdotes.** Bezos told the same few lighter stories to every audience: the first name, *Cadabra*, which a lawyer heard as "cadaver," and packing boxes on his knees on a cement floor. He said they needed kneepads; the person beside him said they needed packing tables, which doubled productivity. "Humor is disarming. Humor is charming. Humor builds connection and trust."

## Study what makes stories iconic

When Amazon Studios set out in 2017 to find its own hit, Bezos listed twelve elements that iconic shows share:

1. a heroic protagonist who grows and changes
2. a compelling antagonist
3. wish fulfillment (for example, hidden abilities)
4. moral choices
5. diverse world-building
6. urgency to watch the next episode (cliffhangers)
7. high stakes, up to the fate of civilization
8. humor
9. betrayal
10. positive emotions (love, joy, hope)
11. negative emotions (loss, sorrow)
12. violence

His teams had to submit spreadsheets showing how each show handled every element, and where one was missing, explain why. On *Jack Ryan*: "your hero is only as good as the antagonist that he's facing." Two devices carry over to business stories: the *teaser*, a shocking or surprising opening scene, and the *cliffhanger* ending. "Everyone has a story."

## Origin stories

Stories let millions of strangers cooperate (Yuval Noah Harari), and "trade cannot exist without trust." Around the campfire, early humans spent 80 percent of their time sharing stories, and the storytellers became leaders. An origin story is how a founder earns trust, influence, cooperation and culture.

**The Hero's Journey.** Joseph Campbell identified the *monomyth*, the one story behind the world's myths. In entrepreneurial terms:

1. the ordinary world
2. a call to adventure (a problem, challenge or idea)
3. doubts and doubters
4. mentors
5. crossing the threshold (leaving the comfort zone)
6. tests, obstacles, allies and enemies
7. the ordeal: near-death, rock bottom, where the stumble reveals the treasure
8. emerging transformed
9. returning with the *elixir*, a lesson or treasure that benefits others

Bezos's version: an Albuquerque childhood; his grandfather as mentor; the call, web usage growing 2,300 percent a year; his boss as doubter; the road trip to Seattle as threshold; the dot-com crash as near-death; the new ideas (cloud, third-party sellers) as treasure; and as elixir, the "tiniest sip of the elixir we have here in the U.S." "Storytelling is not something we do. Storytellers are who we are."

Campbell described seventeen stages and Christopher Vogler twelve. You can skip or rearrange them. A business story still runs in three acts; the Hero's Journey *overlays* a character arc on top of them.

**Founders who told it well:**

- **Netflix, "Goliath, meet David."** Act 1: a carpool, a $40 late fee on *Apollo 13*, and the question "What if there were no late fees?" Act 2: DVDs by mail; in 2000 the company lost $57 million, and Blockbuster's CEO could barely keep from laughing at an offer to buy Netflix for $50 million. Act 3: streaming and 200 million subscribers. It takes three minutes to tell and leaves out the mess. Marc Randolph: "The whole story is messy, but people's eyes will glaze over if you tell them everything." Tell "emotionally true stories"; the story "can't be a three-hundred-page book. It has to fit into three or four short paragraphs." Repeated often enough, an origin story becomes the brand.
- **Canva.** Melanie Perkins was teaching Photoshop in 2007 when she had the idea. In Act 2 she was rejected by about a hundred investors. She learned to kite-surf to meet funders who did, then went off course and was left stranded, a fun-and-games scene that also shows her grit. She learned she was being rejected because she pitched *how* Canva worked, not *why* it existed: "She had no first act." Once she opened with her frustration over design tools, investors saw the problem before the solution. Canva grew to $40 billion.
- **Airbnb.** Act 1: air mattresses for a design conference, the verdict "Strangers will never stay in each other's homes," and guests who left feeling like locals. Act 2: trust as a design problem, solved with profiles, messaging, two-way reviews and payments. Act 3: four million hosts and 825 million guest arrivals. Brian Chesky's analogy, "the marketplace for space just as eBay was the marketplace for stuff," turned investor Jeff Jordan from "complete skeptic to complete believer in twenty-nine minutes." "Every great founder can really tell a great story." Give the arc ups and downs, tension and relief, and a compelling vision.

**The hundred-word version.** Warby Parker prints its origin story, under a hundred words, on the cleaning cloth that ships with every pair. It still has all three acts. Setting: a young man leaves his glasses on an airplane. Conflict: replacements are expensive, so the founders go on a quest and find allies. Resolution: they start a company that makes buying glasses fun and inexpensive, and gives a pair to someone in need for every pair sold. The extended version lives on the website, but "for most customers, a one-hundred-word story is enough to build trust."

**Drill 12: build your origin story in three acts.**

- **Act 1:** Describe life before the adventure, and the problem or event that sparked the idea.
- **Act 2:** Describe the challenges and obstacles, and build tension: how close did you come to failing?
- **Act 3:** Describe the resolution: how you overcame the obstacles, what you learned, and how it transformed you, your company or the world.

Then share it often. Don't expect people to know your story. "You might get tired of telling it, but others want to hear it."
