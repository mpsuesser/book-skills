# Metaphor and analogy

Bezos is "the master of metaphor and the king of analogies." Both are mental shortcuts: they pack a lot of information into a word or a picture the audience already holds. "The speed with which an idea is adopted is directly proportional to its simplicity."

## Metaphor

A *metaphor* is a comparison between two otherwise unrelated things. Ward Farnsworth's fuller account: a metaphor makes unfamiliar things familiar, invisible things visible and complicated things easier to understand. It can "give life to lifeless things" (Aristotle), amuse by putting a subject in unexpected company, borrow feeling from the thing it is compared to, stick through the beauty of its fit, catch attention by surprise, and do all of it "with wondrous economy." It works the way Buffett's "Margaritaville" does for Jimmy Buffett: language that creates a state of mind, a feeling, in the listener.

**Conceptual metaphor theory** (Lakoff and Johnson, *Metaphors We Live By*, 1980) holds that the brain makes sense of the world by mapping one domain onto another. Every metaphor has a **target domain**, the abstract concept you want to get across, and a **source domain**, the concrete thing you compare it to. Source domains usually fall into three categories:

- **motion:** "I'm on the fast track"; "smooth sailing from here"
- **physical location:** "I'm at a crossroad"
- **spatial orientation:** "My life's looking up"

**Drill 7: spot and sort.** Hunt for comparisons from outside your own subject, in books, articles, speeches and presentations. Sort each one as motion, physical or spatial. Noticing metaphors sparks your own.

### How Bezos's metaphors worked

Metaphors repeated with "striking consistency" become "a blueprint on how to think and act."

- **Day One.** "Day 1" appears twenty-five times in twenty-one letters, and Bezos named his building "Day 1 North." The metaphor stands for risk-taking, speed, curiosity, experimentation, failure and continuous learning. "There is no Day One company… It's a mindset."
- **Two-pizza teams.** "If you can't feed a team with two pizzas, it's too large." In one image it said everything about decentralized decision-making, small autonomous teams loosely connected to each other, and coordination as a drag on speed. It also stood in for the *communication path formula*, N × (N − 1) / 2, where N is team size. Five people have ten channels; ten people have forty-five, which is 4.5 times the effort to keep everyone informed. Bezos took the idea from Fred Brooks's *The Mythical Man-Month*.
- **Single-threaded leaders.** Two-pizza teams helped in product development but not in legal or HR. Amazon found that the best predictor of a team's success was whether it had a leader with the skills, authority and experience to run a team "whose sole focus was to get the job done." The leaders mapped that target onto a source domain they knew well: single-threading, a program executing one command at a time. A *single-threaded leader* (STL) is "a single person, unencumbered by competing responsibilities," who owns a single major initiative. Fulfillment by Amazon sat idle for a year until a vice president, Tom Taylor, was asked to give it 100 percent of his focus.
- **Variants built for other cultures.** Hopper replaced pizzas with *Roman tents*: teams of eight to ten with one accountable leader, after the eight Roman soldiers who could fit in one tent. Twilio uses the *dozen-bagel rule*: if a dozen bagels won't feed the team, it has grown too large. "Pick a metaphor… Better yet, come up with a new one that's unique to your culture and mission."
- **The flywheel** (from Jim Collins's *Good to Great*). A flywheel is a heavy disk that is hard to start but spins faster and faster as it gathers energy, until momentum carries it. Bezos sketched the *virtuous cycle* on a napkin: growth at the center, driven by customer service, selection and low prices. Improve any input and the loop accelerates. Lower prices bring more visitors, more sales and more third-party sellers. That spreads fixed costs, which allows lower prices again. Each Amazon business runs its own flywheel; at AWS, tools attract developers, who attract enterprise customers, whose scale lowers prices. Bezos on retail and AWS: "Superficially, the two could hardly be more different… under the surface, the two are not so different after all." That is metaphor's whole principle: showing that two different things are alike.
- **Door desks** and the **empty chair** (see symbols in `delivery-and-mission.md`).

### Using metaphor

- **Describe unique experiences or events.** Astronaut Chris Hadfield describes a Soyuz launch as a beast "roaring like a dragon," himself as "a little leaf in a hurricane," the ignition as being "in the jaws of an enormous dog." Concrete images carry an experience the audience will never have.
- **Breathe life into arcane topics.** Buffett's *economic moat*: "a business with a wide and long-lasting moat around it, protecting a terrific economic castle with an honest lord in charge of the castle." Economist Diane Swonk spends forty hours a month writing her reports, much of it crafting comparisons: "The economy went into an ice age overnight… It's going to take much longer to thaw the economy than it took to freeze it"; "Covid is the iceberg, and we are trying to get the lifeboats"; "The Fed is running out of rabbits to pull out of a hat." Experts who are also great communicators are rare, and they get an outsized share of attention.

**Drill 8: replace the clichés.** Familiar metaphors have lost their force: *the ball is in your court, bring to the table, think outside the box, a drop in the bucket, a perfect storm, a fly in the ointment*. "If you've heard a figure of speech a thousand times, so has your audience." Reach past the easy one.

**Drill 9: A is B.** The simplest metaphor format is "A is B" ("Time is money"). Pick a complex idea from your field (A). Pick a familiar idea (B). Write "A is B" and describe the comparison in conversational language. Example: a good investment (A) / a castle with a moat (B) → "A good investment is an economic castle with a deep moat around it to deter competitors."

## Analogy

An *analogy* compares two unrelated things to highlight their similarities, with the purpose of transferring knowledge. It can contain a metaphor but is a more elaborate explanation. "Analogies are pervasive in human thought" (Diane Halpern): faced with something new, the brain doesn't ask "What is it?" but "What is it *like*?" Archimedes solved the crown problem by mapping the underlying structure of a known thing (his displaced bathwater) onto an unknown one (the crown). "An analogy makes the abstract concrete." An analogy "almost always starts life as a metaphor, but it needs a storyteller to bring it to life." "The internet is electricity" means nothing until someone explains the parallel.

**Reach far.** Halpern gave 193 volunteers scientific passages with no analogy, a *near-domain* analogy (from the same branch of knowledge: the lymphatic system as blood moving through veins) or a *far-domain* analogy (from a completely different area: the lymphatic system as water flowing through a sponge; electricity as a water hose, with voltage as the pressure, current as the hose's diameter and resistance as sand in the hose). Immediately afterward the groups scored the same. A week later the far-domain readers remembered much more and understood it better. When the similarity is less obvious, readers have to work out the underlying relationship, and the idea leaves a deeper imprint. Other far-domain examples: "the heart is like a system of buckets and pumps"; "the circulatory system is like a railway." Life is like a living organism (dull); life is like a box of chocolates (curious). **Use an analogy far removed from the topic.**

**Choose the analogy that frames the event correctly.** "How we react to events, the decisions we make today, and what we expect from the future depends very much on how we categorize events." In his 2003 TED talk, Bezos took the analogy everyone was using for the dot-com crash, the gold rush. He conceded its fit point by point: both were real, both had huge boom and huge bust, both had hype, people abandoned careers, and both left carnage behind. Then he marked where it "starts to diverge, and I think rather severely": "In a gold rush, when it's over, it's over." He swapped in electricity. The light bulb was the first "killer app"; then came fans, irons, vacuums and washing machines. "We are at the 1908 Hurley washing machine stage with the internet." "With innovation, there isn't a last nugget. Every new thing creates two new questions and two new opportunities." The better analogy clarified his own and his team's thinking and gave him confidence against critics who had written "Amazon.toast" and "Amazon.bomb." A shareholder who bought that day and held until Bezos stepped down gained 15,000 percent.

The pattern for replacing a bad frame: show why the popular analogy seems to fit, mark where it breaks, and offer the more precise one that changes what the audience expects.

More analogies at work:

- **Seeds and oaks.** When staff questioned the move into digital media, Bezos answered: "We need to plant many seeds because we don't know which one of those seeds will grow into a mighty oak." The oak fits Amazon on every point: it lives for a thousand years (long-term thinking), it is huge (selection), it is resilient, and only a few of its millions of acorns ever take root. People "can literally picture it."
- **The handstand.** Most people expect to master a freestanding handstand in two weeks; it takes about six months of daily practice, and those who expect two weeks quit. In the 2017 letter Bezos applied it to six-page memos, which people think can be written in a day or two when "it might take a week or more."
- **The library.** Werner Vogels explained S3's objects, buckets and keys as a library. Books are objects (any data), buckets are sections such as art history or geology, and keys are the card catalogue: each object has exactly one key. He made 100 trillion stored objects graspable as roughly the number of synapses in the human brain.
- **"All-you-can-eat"** shipping named Prime's offer, and Airbnb pitched itself as "the marketplace for space just as eBay was the marketplace for stuff."

**Drill 10: notice analogies.** Count the analogies you meet in conversations, books, articles and videos. Watch especially the popular writers and speakers on complex subjects; they lean on analogy to transfer knowledge. Reach for one whenever you speak on an arcane topic: "Your message doesn't have to change to change how you deliver the message."
