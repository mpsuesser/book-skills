# Simple writing: words, sentences, openings

How Bezos writes complex ideas so that most readers can follow them. The numeric standards (grade 8, reading ease 50 or higher, twenty words per sentence) and the clear/concise/specific revision are in `SKILL.md`, step 6. This file covers the craft behind them.

## Readability

The **Flesch reading ease** score (Rudolf Flesch, 1940s) runs from 1 to 100, and higher is easier: 30 is "very difficult," 70 "easy," 90 or above "very easy." Newspapers and publishers that adopted it saw readership rise 60 percent. The **Flesch-Kincaid grade level** (with J. Peter Kincaid, 1970s) converts the score to a school grade, from words per sentence, syllables per word, and active versus passive sentences.

- For a broad adult audience, aim for **eighth grade**: 80 percent of Americans can read and understand it. Academic papers run at grades 16 to 18; *Harry Potter* at 6 to 8.
- The grade measures how much mental energy the reader spends, not how sophisticated the thinking is. Bezos's 2010 Princeton commencement speech ("Cleverness is a gift; kindness is a choice") scored grade 7: 1,353 words, 88 sentences. "The world's richest man inspired the country's smartest college graduates with words a twelve-year-old could understand."
- The twenty-four letters together: 48,062 words, 2,481 sentences, 18.8 words per sentence, grade 11, 94 percent active sentences. They cover free cash flow, GAAP, pro forma income and machine learning at a level a high-school student can read.
- Bezos improved with practice. Seventy percent of his highest-scoring letters came after 2007. From 2007 on, every letter but two (2010 and 2019) sat at grade 8 or 9.

**Drill 4: score the text.** Run it through a readability checker (Grammarly; in Microsoft Word, check "Show readability statistics" under the spelling and grammar options). Revise until it meets the clear standard in `SKILL.md` step 6. The chapter of the book that teaches this scores 59 and grade 8.

## Select before you write

Simplicity is selection. Speakers end up "in the weeds" when they skip the hard work of pulling the weeds out beforehand.

- Bezos summed up twenty-seven years in a 620-word email by choosing what to leave out. He listed a dozen inventions and ended the list with "and much more."
- Virginia's bid for Amazon's second headquarters ran to nine hundred pages, but Stephen Moret challenged the team to make "the real story fit onto a page": six key messages. "If you have to distill the case you're trying to make into a few points, what would those points be? Make sure those points are clear, bulletproof, and supportable. Don't get lost in a sea of data." Virginia won.
- For a cloud-security start-up's IPO, a deck full of wordy slides, acronyms and weeds, with no stories, became a simple story built on one fact: IT teams used the product to troubleshoot problems in under fifteen minutes.
- Coupang's Bom Kim explained "rocket delivery" with customer scenes, not logistics. Order as late as midnight and wake up to your items "like Christmas morning"; order a tutu at night and have it before ballet. It scored a reading ease of 90 and grade 3, with no passive sentences. He left out the machine learning and "dynamic orchestration." Customers care about the outcome, not how the order gets to the door.
- Indra Nooyi calls the ability to make the complex simple her "hip-pocket skill." "You cannot over-invest in communication skills."

The opposite type is the *sesquipedalian*, a person fond of long, multisyllabic words. One investment-firm founder's complaint about business graduates: their presentations are "comprehensive and highly technical, and utterly incomprehensible and entirely unmemorable."

**Drill 1: principles people can act on.** Amazon's sixteen Leadership Principles fit in about seven hundred words of eighth-grade language. Each one is a name plus a few short sentences that turn it into behavior. Customer Obsession: "Leaders start with the customer and work backwards. They work vigorously to earn and keep customer trust. Although leaders pay attention to competitors, they obsess over customers." Write values, principles and goals the same way: "it's impossible to act on a principle no one can remember or understand."

## Short, ancient words

"Short words are best, and old words when short are best of all" (Churchill). "Great speakers use short words to explain new ideas." In Bezos's 1997 Kindle passage, 92 percent of the words are one or two syllables and 76 percent are one syllable.

After the Norman Conquest of 1066, Latin-based Norman French became the language of the English nobility, while the common people (97 percent of the population) kept Old English. Today about 80 percent of English words are either Germanic (Old and Middle English) or Latinate. The ancient Germanic words are short, often one syllable, informal and conversational. Latinate words are longer, formal and stuffy. Plain English uses the old words: *need* not *require*, *boss* not *superior*, *next to* not *adjacent*.

| Formal | Plain |
|---|---|
| He engaged in a deliberate prevarication. | He told a lie. |
| I perceive something in the distance. | I see something. |
| Let's engage in a conversation. | It's time to talk. |
| You are required to purchase any item you damage. | If you break it, you buy it. |

**The rule:** "Use short words to talk about hard things: a crisis, a complex idea, or a big idea that you want listeners to remember." "As your idea grows in complexity, the length of your words should shrink." For inspiration, mix the two kinds of word. Patrick Henry's "Give me liberty or give me death" pairs Latin *liberty* with Old English *death*. All-Germanic text reads like a children's primer. All-Latinate text is "baffling and bewildering," and "fancy words put people to sleep."

**Drill 3: audit for fancy words.** Take a sample of your presentation script. Mark every fancy, Latin-based word or phrase (an online etymology dictionary will tell you a word's origin). Replace each with a shorter, simpler word. This strips out most of the jargon, and the sentences come out tight, clear and strong.

**Crisis messages.** Health communicators work from *mental noise theory*: in a crisis, stress is high and emotions run hot, so people are less likely to hear information accurately, understand it or remember it. Craft the message so it can be spoken in seven to nine seconds or printed in twenty words, ideally as three items in the shortest words available: "Stay Home. Stop the Spread. Save Lives."; "stop, drop, and roll"; Japan's "Three Cs" (closed spaces, crowded places, close-contact settings). New York's stay-at-home order said the same thing in legalese ("mitigate the propagation… minimize morbidity and mortality"). You have found the most ancient words when no shorter word says the same thing. Churchill's "Brevity" memo told officials to replace "woolly phrases" with single conversational words: "The discipline of setting out the real points concisely will prove an aid to clear thinking."

**Plain-language documents.** GE Aviation's general counsel Shawn Burton led "brilliant contracting." His test: "If someone in high school couldn't pick this up and understand it without any context, it wasn't plain enough." Seven contracts became one, a 142-word sentence shrank to 65 words, and all 33 words that needed a definition (and with them the appendix) disappeared. The first 150 plain-language contracts took 60 percent less time to negotiate. Lincoln worked the same way, breaking a complex case into its simplest elements and talking to jurors as if to friends, in "plain Anglo-Saxon words."

## Aphorisms and hooks

An *aphorism* is a pithy saying that compresses a big idea into a handful of words (Taleb), and each one is a complete unit. Bezos packages strategy as aphorisms so it passes from employee to employee: "Short words are easy to say, easy to read, easy to remember, and easy to repeat." A bland platitude repeats common sense. The aphorisms that last make people see the world in a new way.

Jeff-isms, with why each works:

- "Get big fast." Reading ease 100; it can't be said more simply.
- "You don't choose your passions. Your passions choose you." *Chiasmus*: the same phrase inverted in two successive sentences (JFK's "Ask not what your country can do for you…").
- "You can work long, hard, or smart, but at Amazon.com, you can't choose two out of the three." Every word but *Amazon* is one syllable.
- "In short, what's good for customers is good for shareholders." *In short* tells the reader this is a big principle summarized.
- "Life's too short to hang out with people who aren't resourceful." He chose *hang out* over *associate* or *fraternize*.
- "If you can't feed a team with two pizzas, it's too large." A book's worth of management theory at reading ease 100.
- "Your brand is what others say about you when you're not in the room."
- "It's always Day 1."

Buffett's aphorisms work the same way: "Be fearful when others are greedy and greedy when others are fearful" (*greed*, not *avarice*, plus an inversion); "You never know who's swimming naked until the tide goes out" (tides that *go out*, not tides that *subside*); "bet against America" (not *wager*), "now is no time to start" (not *commence*); "It's not how you sell 'em, it's how you tell 'em" (written the way people talk). "America's best days lie ahead" beats "America is well-positioned to capitalize on future growth opportunities." "If you go bold, keep it short."

**Write for the ear.** A memorable line works like a song's *hook* (an earworm, which researchers call "involuntary musical imagery"). It must be simple and repeatable, and most radio hooks last three to five seconds. Bill Withers: "simple is memorable." Obama's "Yes, we can" was an *epistrophe*, a repetition of words at the end of successive sentences. He used it as a refrain, twelve times in one speech: the ideas filled the verses and the hook was the chorus. "Good communicators are clear and concise; great communicators make music for the ear."

## Seven writing tips

Gallo treats these as *tools and strategies*, not rules. Rules are rigid and every platform has its own style. A post that breaks five grammar rules can still be perfect: "The most effective method of expressing your ideas is the method that works." Learn the rules, but don't let them get in your way. Writing is hard work. Seinfeld writes every day ("It's a game of tonnage") and caps each session ("Give yourself an hour").

1. **Begin sentences with subjects and verbs.** Subject and verb are the locomotive that pulls the railcars. Put them first and let the rest branch to the right: a *right-branching sentence* (Roy Peter Clark). Keep subject and verb close together. "A writer composes a sentence…" works; "A writer who wants to be really good at the craft should compose…" doesn't. If a sentence won't come, start with a subject and a verb. Bezos: "We designed Amazon Prime as an all-you-can-eat free program."
2. **Order words for emphasis.** The period is a *full stop*, and it draws the eye to the words just before it. Put the strongest material first (the locomotive), hide the weaker material in the middle, and save an interesting word for the end (the caboose). "The Queen, my lord, is dead": Shakespeare "stuck the landing." Bezos: "Setting the bar high in our approach to hiring has been, and will continue to be, the single most important element of Amazon's success."
3. **Use the active voice (most of the time).** If the subject performs the action, the sentence is active. If the subject receives it, the sentence is passive ("Amazon was founded by Jeff Bezos"). Passive sentences are wordier, muddle the meaning and sow distrust, because leaders hide in them: "Mistakes were committed by some staffers." The leaders people trust say "I made mistakes. The buck stops with me." Write subject–verb–object ("The boy kicked the ball"). Headlines train the ear: "Fed Raises Rates," "TikTok dances past Google." "Stay active to stand out."
4. **Unleash strong verbs.** "Strong verbs create action, save words, and reveal the players" (Clark). Verbs "are the executives; they should be in charge" (Gary Provost). A weak verb is unspecific, inactive, and leans on an adverb for its meaning: "walked rapidly" → "dashed." Bezos: "We listen to customers, invent on their behalf, personalize the story for each of them, and earn their trust." "Staying in Day 1 requires you to experiment patiently, accept failures, plant seeds, protect saplings, and double down when you see customer delight."
5. **Cut verb qualifiers and "weasel words"** (Amazon's term): *sort of, tend to, kind of, seemed to, could have*. "At Amazon we obsess over the customer" beats "we tend to think that if we're preoccupied with the customer… we could probably be more successful." "Missionaries make better products. They care more" beats "I sort of think that missionaries tend to make better products." Then cut the easy adverbs (*really, extremely, totally*). Stephen King: "The road to hell is paved with adverbs." Le Carré: "Get rid of anything extraneous."
6. **Vary sentence length.** A page of six-word sentences goes stale. "Long sentences create a flow that carries the reader down a stream of understanding, a steady advance. A short sentence slams on the brakes" (Clark). "Fear not the long sentence." Long sentences suit lists and scenes. Bezos's 2000 letter opened "Ouch." and followed with sentences of 16, 18 and 21 words. His 2014 letter set up "four characteristics" of a dreamy business in 9 words, listed them in 28, and landed in 12: "don't just swipe right, get married."
7. **Construct parallel structures.** *Parallel construction* uses the same grammatical pattern for two or more ideas, which gives them equal weight, adds power and cuts words: "increasing selection, speeding delivery, reducing cost structure"; "our low prices, our reliable delivery, and our in-stock position." Parallel lines read and sound equally well. The 1997 letter is a series of commitments that each begin "We will…"

**Drill 5: keep learning from the writing teachers.** Gallo's shelf: *Writing Tools: 55 Essential Strategies for Every Writer* (Roy Peter Clark), *Writing to Persuade* (Trish Hall), *On Writing Well* (William Zinsser), *100 Ways to Improve Your Writing* (Gary Provost), *On Writing* (Stephen King). Be a learn-it-all, not a know-it-all (Satya Nadella).

## First lines

Bezos puts great care into first lines, because they grab attention and set the tone. His 2000 letter, written after Amazon's stock fell more than 80 percent, opened with one word: "Ouch." Four short sentences followed. They grabbed the shareholders' attention, told them what happened, offered hope ("Amazon.com the company is in a stronger position now than at any time in its past"), and gave them a reason to stay. James Patterson rewrites his first pages and first sentence over and over until they hook the reader. An opening doesn't have to pull the reader *all* the way in; it only has to get them invested enough to read the next part. Bezos ends meetings when he loses interest, so his leaders learned to get him invested fast.
