# Creative Selection

Ken Kocienda spent fifteen years at Apple as a software engineer. He helped start the Safari browser and its WebKit toolkit, then added web page editing to WebKit, then wrote the original iPhone keyboard and its autocorrection, and later worked on the iPad. He presented demos to Steve Jobs. The book draws Apple's way of working out of those stories. He insists it is "not a matter of following a checklist": the approach "flowed from the work". It came top down from Jobs's authority and vision, and ground up from the daily efforts of designers and programmers. Apply it at the book's breadth, to any creative or technical product work: software, hardware, design, or anything else made through prototypes and judgment.

## The core frame

- **Creative selection** is the author's name for "this continuing progression of demo ➞ feedback ➞ next demo." Its model is Darwin's artificial selection ("man's power of accumulative selection"). Build variation into demos, keep the strong aspects, discard the weak, and make the next demo from those decisions. Each new demo is "concretely and specifically targeted to be better than the previous one." Round after round moves work "step by step from the spark of an idea to a finished product." It is a slow road of accumulating positive change. Brilliant flashes have little to do with it: the author had only two *Eureka!* moments in fifteen years.
- **The seven essential elements** are the raw material of creative selection:
  1. **Inspiration**: thinking big ideas and imagining what might be possible (Imran Chaudhri seeing that smooth finger tracking would connect people to the iPhone).
  2. **Collaboration**: working together well with other people and seeking to combine your complementary strengths (two colleagues untangling the author's insertion-point code).
  3. **Craft**: applying skill to achieve high-quality results and always striving to do better (making Safari faster with the Page Load Test).
  4. **Diligence**: doing the necessary grunt work and never resorting to shortcuts or half measures (fixing cross-references one by one until the browser built).
  5. **Decisiveness**: making tough choices and refusing to delay or procrastinate (Jobs making the author pick one iPad keyboard on the spot).
  6. **Taste**: developing a refined sense of judgment and finding the balance that produces a pleasing and integrated whole (choosing QWERTY for the iPhone).
  7. **Empathy**: trying to see the world from other people's perspectives and creating work that fits into their lives and adapts to their needs (a tap game to find touch targets comfortable for every level of dexterity).

  The elements combine into **molecules**. Inspiration plus decisiveness makes initial prototypes. Collaboration, craft and taste together make detailed feedback to a teammate. Diligence plus empathy makes software people can use "without pulling their hair out." Each person adds a personal touch, an **octessence**. The elements apply "in *all the things we did, all the time.* Everything counts. No detail is too small."
- **Demos** "served as the primary means to turn ideas into software." A demo is useful only if it is **concrete and specific**. Without an artifact even a simple idea cannot be discussed: two people who each imagine a cute puppy can never settle whose is cuter, but two photos make the argument "child's play." Demos make people react, and those reactions drive the next demo. The sooner creative decisions start, the more time there is to refine them, to backtrack, or to forge ahead.
- **The decider**: "Every Apple demo review had a decider, the person with the sole authority to approve or not and the prerogative to declare what would happen next." Peers decide on their own portions of the work. A team's manager decides at team reviews, and so on up the chain. The result is "a pyramid of demos, reviews, and decisions" with Jobs's final judgment at the top.
- **Working at the intersection** of technology and liberal arts. Apple tried to "make extremely advanced products from a technology point of view" that are also intuitive, easy and fun to use, "so that they really fit the users. The users don't have to come to them, they come to the user." The intersection is judged "only holistically, by evaluating how the product fits the person." It reaches all the senses: the iPhone key click is a recording of a pencil striking the edge of a desk.
- **Design is how it works.** Jobs: "Most people make the mistake of thinking design is what it [a product] looks like." Beauty that is only visual "only goes so far." Form follows function, and "Objects should explain themselves." This credo gives taste "a purpose, a rationale beyond self-indulgence, an empathetic end."
- **The Apple Way**, in the author's summary: "*A small group of people built a work culture based on applying the seven essential elements through an ongoing process of creative selection.*" It is **execution dependent** (a Hollywood term): "the quality of the result is mostly in the quality of the doing." The culture and the people who make it "are one and the same."

## Running creative selection

1. **Set the destination as a specific, well-chosen thing.** State the goal so it turns words into actions, as Jobs's "fast" became Don Melton's rule "never make the browser slower," enforced by the Page Load Test. Excellence means "closing the gap between the accidental and intentional," achieving "not just a *something* or even an *everything* but a *specific and well-chosen thing*." When the ground is new, do as the Purple team did: "we picked a point over the technological horizon" and set out toward it together, unsure of the exact direction. Rules, measurement and the Lombardi parallel: [craft-and-diligence.md](craft-and-diligence.md).
2. **Make the first demo fast.** Follow Richard Williamson's model. Decide what the demo must prove. Set a short schedule, goals and **nongoals**, and choose shortcuts so that "one piece of custom programming" is all that is left to close the circle. Then draw a thick ring around the key details: inside, the **lamppost** gets the highest fidelity. Outside, the **hat shop** interior gets as little attention as possible. The boundary gets extra care, a few props in the window so the audience suspends disbelief. Sort every feature as important, passable or ignorable. How-to: [demos-and-reviews.md](demos-and-reviews.md).
3. **Show it: demonstrate, don't tell.** "We couldn't get away with telling. We were required to show." Show demos to each other constantly, then up the pyramid. Use "the minimum number of words necessary" to direct the viewer's attention. Know the work "inside and out," because questions will rain down.
4. **Review with a decider, and decide.** The decider looks hard and asks whether the work can be simpler. Removing one option often sets off a cascade of further removals: "the best way to answer difficult questions like these was to avoid the need to ask them." Every review ends with a verdict and the next step. Leaders gauge headway with the same questions each time: "Does this demo close the prototype-to-product gap, even a little? Are we seeing enough positive change over last time? Is this technology or app on track?" Running reviews and giving feedback: [demos-and-reviews.md](demos-and-reviews.md).
5. **Make the next demo answer the feedback.** "There was an expectation that new demos would include a response to the feedback from previous demos. This was the one essential demo expectation: progress." Most changes tune a **heuristic** (a subjective value such as an animation's timing, with no single direction of improvement) or how a heuristic and an **algorithm** (an objective procedure whose measure moves one way) combine. The next demo is often minutes away.
6. **Live on it.** Use the in-progress work daily as if it were the real product ("living on"; the industry says dogfooding). Some flaws only show over time and in ordinary life, like typists losing their place mid-word on the iPhone keyboard. Judge whether the product melts away and "leaves a space for what people really care about." Taste, empathy and mental load: [taste-and-the-intersection.md](taste-and-the-intersection.md).
7. **Converge.** Lock features, then spend the last three or four months fixing bugs and polishing. Read the bug curve: spikes after big demos are normal, while a flat line over many days means convergence has stalled. Convergence "isn't magic"; everyone does it, and it makes decent products, not great ones. Throughout, treat milestones as beginnings: a demo that wins is "a successful audition rather than a sold-out performance."

Done when the current demo's review produced a decision and named actions, the next demo visibly answers them, and the work is being lived on.

## Where creative selection bogs down

Success was "as much about what we didn't do as what we did." Name each of these when you see it:

- **Talk without artifacts.** Two-hour coffee breaks and daylong offsites with no examples to ground them: "lengthy discussions about whose imaginary puppy was cuter." A brainstorm that runs past an hour or so, has more than a handful of people, or happens often turns into "a form of sneaky procrastination."
- **Waiting for an epiphany.** Shuffling printed specifications or unchanging paper mock-ups for weeks, hoping to jump straight from concept to complete design. Hoping an approach "would pan out somehow" with no plan, goals, nongoals, schedule or shortcuts.
- **Influence without involvement.** The **Seagull Manager** flies in, squawks, flaps, "drops a big poop on everyone," and flies away.
- **Research cut off from shipping.** Research groups kept apart from the people who ship (Jobs disbanded Apple's Advanced Technology Group in 1997). At Apple the same people came up with the ideas and wrote the shipping code.
- **Never living on it.** Shipping without using the product as an integrated whole first (Eazel's fate).
- **Reviews that end without a decision.** This "interrupts the chain of criticism that provides the logical connection from demo to demo." Conflicting lines of authority, with no final decision everyone recognizes, do the same damage.
- **Oversized teams** on tasks one or two people could handle, causing muddled communication and diluting each person's ability to make a difference.
- **Designing for looks**, fashion or an abstract ideal instead of how the product works.
- **A/B tests for decisions that need taste**, like Google testing 41 shades of blue. This factors out taste: see [taste-and-the-intersection.md](taste-and-the-intersection.md).
- **Bad demos sent upward.** A third-rate demo excused by the hours spent on it, or "one decently fast Thoroughbred mixed in with a bunch of old nags" so the boss can pick the winner.

The author's explanation for how Apple avoided these traps: clarity of purpose. "Concentrating keenly on what to do helped us to block out what *not* to do."
