# Case: the iPhone keyboard, autocorrection and the iPad (2005–2011)

The longest worked example of creative selection in the book: a design nobody knew how to make, found demo by demo. Principles are in [taste-and-the-intersection.md](taste-and-the-intersection.md) and [demos-and-reviews.md](demos-and-reviews.md).

## Setting

Purple was the iPhone's code name. It was a secret hallway of about six to eight programmers at first, about fifteen by the derby, beside the Human Interface (HI) designers. The designers gave the software "a spirit, from concepts and principles to animations and icons," and the programmers supplied "the sinew." The prototype hardware was the **Wallaby**, a phone-sized multitouch screen tethered to a Mac. The platform was chosen by parallel investigation. The iPod's software, a shrunk AppKit, WebKit as the interface layer, and a new touch system were all explored, and AppKit and WebKit were dropped within weeks. Forstall won the internal contest against Tony Fadell's iPod division with skunk works demos of inertial scrolling, the SpringBoard home screen and early Safari running on a pocketable device. The result was a Mac-derived platform with a new touch interface framework, UIKit. Apple "had bet everything on a software keyboard," while the BlackBerry's hardware keyboard ruled the market. Every bug in the keyboard drew comparisons to the Newton, and the keyboard sat at the top of the project's risk slides. "None of us knew how a touchscreen keyboard was *supposed* to work."

## 1. The keyboard derby

- **Problem.** In September 2005, Forstall could not type his own name on a demo keyboard. A day or two later Henri Lamiraux halted all Purple work: "Starting from now, you're all keyboard engineers." Apple had never before stopped a fifteen-person project to focus on one problem.
- **Tried.** Within days there were shrunk laptop layouts, a Morse code keyboard, and a piano keyboard typing chords. The author tried interlocking puzzle-piece keys. Richard Williamson: "Your keyboards are like everybody else's, and they don't work... *The keys need to be bigger.*" The finger covers the target, and there is no tactile feedback. Big keys meant several letters per key, chosen by slides, double taps or long presses. The author's **Blob** keyboard was twelve keys in a 4 × 3 grid: tap for the top letter, slide toward a bottom corner for the others. Typing "bank" took a swipe left, a tap, a tap and a swipe right. Deciding the gesture for every letter overloaded the mind, and the rearranged letters made it worse. The author scrapped all five of his prototypes, keeping three lessons:
  1. "Big keys were easier to tap. Richard was right."
  2. "Rearranging the letters on the keyboard was a bad idea." QWERTY means no hunting around.
  3. "Keys should be tap only."
- **The entry.** Big QWERTY keys with several letters each, tap only, with "the decision of picking the letters" offloaded to the computer: an n-letter word always takes n taps, and a dictionary picks "the most sensible word." He built a rudimentary English word list from Apple's archives. He couldn't fit it into Kim Vorrath's common demo switcher, and chose "a nonconforming demo that functioned properly, rather than the opposite." That got him scheduled last.
- **Judged.** None of the switcher entries gave quick, accurate typing. When Kim said "Well, that's it," the author shouted "No!" Instructions: tap the letters you want and ignore that there are several per key. Forstall typed "Scott is my name" and said, "This is amazing!" Colleagues who tried it afterward were supportive but less impressed: "That's how it often goes with early-stage prototypes." A day or two later the author was made keyboard DRI, without being asked.
- **Takeaway.** Purple collaboration "reduced to a basic idea: We showed demos to each other," and every demo had to be concrete and specific. The derby winner was the keyboard's Black Slab, a beginning.

## 2. From derby winner to QWERTY

- **Cold reactions.** Phil Schiller (two minutes, unconvinced by multiple letters per key) and Tony Fadell (under a minute, barely looked) showed the winner was "a successful audition rather than a sold-out performance." The author pinned a paper cutout of the keyboard's screen area, about 2 by 1.3 inches, beside his desk and accepted he might have to rethink every decision.
- **Living on it exposed three failures.** Names not in the dictionary couldn't be typed ("Teemu"). Neither could deliberate gibberish ("Arrr!" on Talk Like a Pirate Day, September 19). And typists got lost mid-word, the **"Where am I?" problem**. The keyboard suggested only words with exactly as many letters as keys typed, so five keys toward "aluminum" offered **slimy**, and nobody knows the sixth letter of "aluminum." Typists stopped, deleted and retyped. Richard's quick verdicts on experiments: more dictionary words, good; a word hint on the space bar, not so good.
- **The review.** In early 2006, Forstall got lost typing "national" (**Mary**, then **Mario**). The author derailed the meeting at the whiteboard, laying out the four concepts of the derby winner: "1. Big keys with multiple letters per key 2. A QWERTY arrangement 3. Every gesture was a tap 4. A dictionary provided active assistance." Greg Christie cut in: "Aww … come on, Ken! Can't you just put one letter on every key?" His exasperation signaled confidence. He had found the weak link in the chain, the first concept, though probably not how to build the alternative.
- **The fix.** Go back to small single-letter keys, now backed by the QWERTY layout, tap typing and dictionary work. The person typing and the autocorrection code need not see the keyboard the same way: visually F is a small key, but to the software it belongs to the group **FDGRTC**. "The keys actually got bigger from the autocorrection standpoint, even though visually they appeared smaller to the typist." Single letters gave an escape hatch for any word. Key pop-ups (built with Bas Ording) and showing the exact keys tapped "definitively solved the 'Where am I?' problem." The suggestion bar showed the best dictionary word on the left and the exact typing on the right. "Greg Christie was right."
- **Next issue: speed bumps.** With space now entering exactly what you typed, getting dictionary help meant tapping a bubble, and typists couldn't build up speed. Richard and the author noticed a common pattern: type a word, tap the left bubble, again and again. They decided to try "just for kicks" having space accept the top suggestion, despite worries about changing typing "out from under you." It took half an hour of code.
- **The Giggly Demo.** Richard thumb-typed a long sentence fast without looking up, and it came out perfect. The log showed what he had actually hit: "Tge quixk brpwm foz jimprd ivrr rhe kazy …" "Junky key presses produced perfect typing—the opposite of the *garbage in, garbage out* way computers were supposed to work." It was the author's second *Eureka!* moment, and always-on autocorrection became permanent. Months later, Nitin Ganatra typed perfectly with one finger and a thumb while holding a candy bar, a sign the keyboard might "fit neatly and unobtrusively into people's everyday lives."

## 3. Autocorrection: better data and better algorithms

- **The "eff grackles" demo.** Pitching always-on autocorrection to Forstall, the author mentioned the Newton. Forstall channeled the 1993 Doonesbury strip in which a Newton turns "I am writing a test sentence" into "Siam fighting atomic sentry," and ends with "Egg freckles?" He typed "egg freckles" and got "Testing for eff grackles." He asked whether the author could keep the useful corrections and banish the ridiculous ones. The author said he didn't know, and that he would start by finding out what went wrong in this case.
- **Diagnosis: two compounding data errors.** Each dictionary word carries a **usage frequency value**, a measure of its popularity in normal text ("good" ranks above "goof," and "the" is highest). "Egg" was set mistakenly below "eff," and "freckles" was missing, so it became "grackle." The algorithms were fine.
- **The core decision.** At every space bar tap the keyboard makes a ***what you meant versus what you did*** choice: substitute the likeliest word, or keep the exact letters. It has to avoid bizarre results that destroy trust. The author treated better data and better algorithms as separate threads.
- **Better data.** Correct the frequencies of everyday words, especially near-neighbors on QWERTY like "tune" and "time." Check coverage of the most common several thousand words. Add slurs explicitly and mark them never to be offered, so a slightly mistyped "nugget" never yields an epithet ("never provide software assistance for attempts to slur or demean"). Add sports teams, stadiums, cities, product names, chat slang and abbreviations: "less an academic linguistics exercise and more a catalog of contemporary life." "Good dictionaries are built one word at a time." The finished lexicon had many tens of thousands of words, plus touches like "cant" becoming "can't." On January 10, 2007, the day after the announcement, he added "iPhone."
- **Better algorithms.** Version one was the **bike lock tumbler** ("colf" spun to "cold"): "Arrange typed letters in a set of tumblers with their neighboring letters. Spin the tumblers to check every letter combination. Note the dictionary words found by spinning the tumblers. Suggest the found word with the highest usage frequency value." It worked for multi-letter keys and novice typists, but it couldn't settle "vild" (cold, bold or vile?) once small keys raised the errors per word. Apple experts, who couldn't be told why he was asking, mentioned Markov chains, conditional random fields, Bayesian inference and dynamic programming. They inspired him more than they informed him; he had never taken college math. Following Richard's lesson, he skipped what he couldn't solve:
  - **Miss direction.** A tap that misses G to the left most likely meant G or F, not H. The nearest key is the likeliest intended one.
  - **Weight the popped-up key**, since the pop-up is the keyboard's **backchannel**. Scoring each tap separately did no better than frequency alone, as living on it showed, but it was a conceptual step.
  - **Key-tap constellation.** Score whole words as a pattern of touches. Picture a superhumanly accurate typist's perfectly centered pattern for every dictionary word, and find the ideal pattern closest to the actual one.
  - **Pattern skew algorithm.** Measure closeness by summing the "nudges" needed to move each tap onto the ideal dots, a **Manhattan distance** (eleven streets plus four avenues is fifteen nudges). A big sum means a poor match. "Blog" typed slightly off center matches "blog" better than "blot."
- **The final algorithm.** Put each typed key in a tumbler with its neighboring keys; spin the tumblers through every letter combination and note the dictionary words that turn up; calculate each found word's pattern skew; multiply its usage frequency by the reciprocal of its pattern skew; suggest the found word with the greatest product. Months of tuning followed, plus the heuristic cut-off that leaves "oooorr" alone rather than making it "polite." Six weeks before launch the suggestion bar was cut and corrections moved to a bubble under the word. In the keynote, Jobs called the keyboard "phenomenal."

## 4. The iPad keyboard (2009)

- **Problem.** The iPad had a larger screen. Bas Ording's Director demo scaled the Mac layout down: more keys, a number row and traditional punctuation, with sliders for fonts, colors and key sizes, and an animation for every change.
- **Tried.** Holding the iPad over a Mac keyboard, the author saw the top row QWERTYUIOP fit the screen's width: bigger keys, almost laptop size, iPhone-style, with no number row. Bigger keys are easier to tap but force hunting for numbers and punctuation. More keys make them easy to find but shrink every key. He decided to "try out both." He bought a ruler and protractor at Target, measured every keyboard he had into "my own personal database" to build a feel for their relationships, and in a couple of days coded both layouts as real keyboards that worked in any app. He first switched them with the language globe key. Bas suggested a zoom key with an animation that eased in and sped up, so one keyboard "was becoming the other." It looked like a complex morph but took a couple of hours to code. Pre-Steve review loved offering both as "the best of both worlds."
- **Judged.** Jobs studied each layout in silence, taking a solid thirty seconds over every detail, then asked, "We only need one of these, right?" and "Which one do you think we should use?" From days of use, the author chose the bigger keys: "I think I could learn to touch type on it." Verdict: "OK. We'll go with the bigger keys." It shipped that way, minus the zoom key.
- **Takeaway.** Decisiveness, simplicity and the cascade (see demos-and-reviews.md). Most of all, have an opinion grounded in living on the alternatives.

## 5. iPad multitasking gestures (2010–2011)

- **Problem.** The iPad's multitouch system could track up to eleven touches, but the software offered only two-finger gestures, and the bigger screen might allow comfortable five-finger ones. Forstall asked for multitasking gestures for iOS 5, and the author signed up.
- **Tried.** Within weeks there were three: a **side-to-side swipe** between recent apps, a **swipe up** to show a bar of recent app icons, and a **scrunch** (fingers drawn together as if crumpling paper) to return home. Several rounds of demos and polish followed. The side-to-side swipe got an elastic stretch when there was no app further along (after launching from the home screen, the current app is by definition the most recent), inspired by the bounce at the end of a scrolling list.
- **Judged.** Jobs arrived with his own idea for going home, a shooing flick of the wrist. Forstall steered him to the prepared demo. The author presented carefully: screen tipped toward Jobs, palm spread to show the gesture, fingers meeting the glass together, a slow scrunch as the app shrank in step. Jobs tried it, was satisfied, and dropped his own idea. Forstall disliked the elastic stretch, because it took liberties with other developers' apps. Jobs loved it: "This animation … this is Apple." Months later, Henri declined a final review with Jobs: "At this point, I think we should go with the multitasking gestures as they are." Jobs died about three months after that.
- **Takeaway.** Even late in a product's life the same loop ran. Prepare the demo, present the gesture precisely, and let a decider who can surrender his own idea choose what works.
