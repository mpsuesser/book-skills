# Case: Safari and WebKit (2001–2004)

Each story below gives the problem, what was tried, how it was judged, and what the author takes from it. The principles themselves live in [craft-and-diligence.md](craft-and-diligence.md) and [demos-and-reviews.md](demos-and-reviews.md).

## 1. Choosing a browser engine: the crystal ball

- **Problem.** In 2001, Steve Jobs and Scott Forstall wanted Apple to "control its own destiny" on the internet by replacing Microsoft Internet Explorer on the Mac. There were two goals: a browser app, and a web toolkit for third-party developers. The team was two people (Don Melton, veteran of Netscape and Eazel, and the author). A browser has three subsystems: content (HTML), styling (CSS) and scripting (JavaScript).
- **Tried.** They evaluated open source options and commercial licenses with multimillion-dollar price tags. Don favored Mozilla, partly because free code would be an easier sell to management. Mozilla had about 1.5 million lines, which printed at thirty lines a page would fill fifty thousand pages, and it wouldn't build on Mac OS X. Secrecy meant they couldn't ask online or inside Apple. After a week's all-out push the author produced *Building the Lizard: Fifty Steps to Get Mozilla Running on Mac OS X* (one step rebuilt the C library, "a brain transplant"). The result launched and then crashed on every page. Six weeks in, they had nothing, and management was starting to doubt them. Recruiting failed too: nobody would join a project the team couldn't describe.
- **Judged.** On his second day, new hire Richard Williamson asked, "How long have you guys been working on this browser project?" ("Six weeks.") Two days later he demoed Konqueror, a browser from the open source KDE project, surfing the web on a Mac. The demo was so clear that, shown up the chain to Avie Tevanian, "no cajoling was necessary."
- **Takeaway.** Richard's plan, goals, nongoals and shortcuts beat hoping a behemoth "would pan out somehow." His sequence is in demos-and-reviews.md. A quick line count (two hours of work) confirmed the bet: Konqueror's two directories, KHTML and KJS, held just over 120,000 lines, under a tenth of Mozilla.

## 2. Porting Konqueror: to the Black Slab

- **Problem.** Make Linux code work as if it had been written natively for the Mac. Linux and the Mac were "two different countries separated by the same language," with shared C++ but different idioms, especially for graphical interfaces. Apple wanted most code kept proprietary (Mac OS X sold for $129), while Konqueror carried the LGPL.
- **Tried.** The porting strategy: uncut the demo's corners, keep Konqueror as a separate "chapter" used by cross-reference, publish the changes, fix every broken cross-reference, and annotate doubts with FIXMEs. The author explains builds through recipes: an include directive works like "see Hollandaise sauce" in a cookbook, and a compiler error is a terse chef's verdict ("Hollandaise too thick") that names the fault but not the fix. The build took about two months of one-error-at-a-time tedium (undeclared "protocol," then "host," then "port"). After that came an empty white window, and the loop of loading a page, reading the FIXME report and patching the worst gap.
- **Judged.** Loading Yahoo! produced a single black rectangle, the **Black Slab**, the browser's first real output. Within a week it showed all the text, then blue underlined links, then images. The cause of the blank window had been code that went through the motions of drawing without putting pen to page, "a web browser version of air guitar."
- **Takeaway.** The Edison ratio (1:75 at this milestone) and "trial after trial": inspiration pays off only with diligence. One of the author's two *Eureka!* moments came from grinding, not a flash.

## 3. Making it fast: one simple rule

- **Problem.** Jobs's single criterion was speed, much faster than Internet Explorer. That was how people would forget the old browser right away, and it would ready the browser for broadband. By late spring 2002 the browser still crawled. News pages jumbled, shopping carts lost items, and bank logins failed.
- **Tried.** Don's PLT and his rule (no change may make the browser slower), plus Knuth-guided optimization done at the moment slowness was measured, and the trading scheme for essential features. Bookmarks and a streamlined interface were also required, but the team jelled around speed.
- **Judged.** At Macworld on January 7, 2003, Jobs announced Safari loaded pages three times faster than Internet Explorer. His "Why?" slide led with speed. The project had taken eighteen months.
- **Takeaway.** Words connected to actions, like Lombardi's Power Sweep. Naming worked the same way. Jobs's "Thunder" and "Freedom" lost to Forstall's "Safari." The toolkit kept the name WebKit, first scribbled on a whiteboard at the porting meeting. The beta shipped with a data-deleting bug, which got a rush fix, and still exceeded expectations.

## 4. Editing web pages in Mail: the hardest problem

- **Problem.** Rich HTML email (marketing, spam, photo albums) arrived as web pages, and Mail's simple composer scrambled replies to them. Forstall asked the author to make WebKit edit a web page like a word processor document (typing, selecting, deleting, cut, copy, paste) and to swap it in for Mail's composer "without anyone noticing." Jobs cared intensely about email. The author had never written a word processor and said "Um … OK," then signed up: "OK, Scott. I'll do the best I can."
- **Tried.** He started with the insertion point. Rudimentary demos came within a month, but it took nearly a year to edit real inbox pages. He learned the rules by experimenting with Word, the Mail composer and BBEdit. HTML mixes data with **metadata** (markup) the way a spoken cake order ("happy birthday and below that tom") mixes the words to write with directions for placing them, so moving between data and its correct visual rendering was hard. Months of special cases produced heisenbugs.
- **Judged.** A half-hour whiteboard session with Darin Adler and Trey Matteson, arranged through Don, diagnosed it: too much data, not enough visuals, and no single authoritative piece of code. The prescription was to gather the special cases into one class, VisiblePosition: "When software behavior is mysterious, get more organized." Follow-up code reviews with demos showed specific bugs fixed.
- **Takeaway.** "People matter more than programming." The setting for the work (Forstall keeping the author after he was passed over for Safari manager and nearly left for Google) and the solution (help from colleagues, received openly) both turned on the social side. Later, this WebKit editing code handled every editable text field on the iPhone.
