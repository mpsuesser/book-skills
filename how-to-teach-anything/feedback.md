# Feedback

"Feedback is information on how we are doing relative to our goal." The environment gives feedback through plain cause and effect. A teacher's feedback is deliberately designed to support, guide and encourage, and it works like a conversation, in which meaning emerges from the back and forth. When a student knows what effect their actions are having, they can adjust, which improves their performance, their self-evaluation and their awareness.

Feedback is neither advice nor evaluation. Pronouncing something good or bad doesn't help anyone improve, and empty praise (a blanket "great job!") is as useless as a grade with no explanation of how it was reached. The teacher facilitates a natural process: we act, our actions have results, and to learn we notice those results and adjust. Draw attention to the effect of the student's actions and relate it to the goal. Done continuously and consistently, this gives the student ample room to dig into the material and refine their understanding and skill. "When in doubt, favor feedback over teaching (i.e., the integrative approach to pedagogy)." On balance, feedback should lean positive (see the positive-negative balance in [motivation-and-environment.md](motivation-and-environment.md)).

## Qualities of good feedback

Good feedback is an art that depends on how it is given more than on the exact words.

- **Respect.** Students should feel treated with dignity and politeness whoever they are, so feedback is received as feedback and not as a personal attack. A woman in a male-dominated workplace will only take feedback in if she genuinely feels it is about her performance and not about her being female.
- **Timeliness.** Give feedback as close as possible to the event; two weeks after a project it won't stick. Formative feedback comes often and in small doses. A single big evaluation leaves students in the dark in the meantime and lets avoidable errors compound.
- **Be specific.** Tell the student exactly where they stand and why: what they are doing right, what needs improvement, how they compare with others or a fixed standard, and, most importantly, the concrete steps to fix it. Vague feedback causes stress and bad feelings and leaves no idea of what to do next.
- **Use the "sandwich" method.** Compliment, correction, compliment, with sincere compliments: "Your opening was great, and you have excellent breath control. The high notes are still feeling a little unsteady, but you finished strong, particularly that last chorus."
- **Describe rather than evaluate.** "Showing your work here has made it so much easier to follow your process" works better than "Hey, nice job showing your work," because it explains why the work is seen favorably. The student draws their own conclusions and feels more genuine pride, and internal motivation grows. Aim at actions, skills and abilities, not personal attributes, which builds a growth mindset and tolerance for mistakes: "the follow-through on your backstroke is really getting stronger" does more for confidence than "you're a naturally gifted swimmer," which gives the student nothing to work with. Leave out advice ("you should do XYZ") for the same reason.
- **Don't make it personal.** With a sensitive student, give feedback without referring to them directly. Model the incorrect performance and critique yourself, or discuss a hypothetical example. Depending on the student, have them evaluate themselves, or ask for their feedback on your teaching, which makes learning a mutual collaboration rather than a power dynamic. Don't say how pleased or upset you personally are with their performance: "it's not about you!"
- **Mix it up.** Tailor the form to what this student hears best, and appeal to their innate motivators (stress the relative ranking of a performance for a student driven by mastery and winning). Give feedback verbally, in notes, or as small, unobtrusive written corrections; sometimes a smile or a thumbs up does the job. You can also go over a third party's feedback together with the student.

Criticism can be hard to take even when it is friendly, reasonable and transparent, so be kind. Balance sincerity with compassion, don't dump a lot of information at once, and give the student space to process it in their own way. If you never deliver feedback with an air of judgment, the student will soon stop taking it that way.

## Make it actionable and close the loop

Good feedback can be acted on. Ask the student how they plan to use your suggestions, or how they already have. This empowers them and focuses the mind, so they move past any embarrassment or disappointment quickly. Build an opportunity to respond into every piece of feedback. Linking feedback to action lets you see trends, and earns you perhaps the most satisfying feedback of all: "I can see that you took that feedback from before and really ran with it, and because of your hard work you've definitely improved. Well done."

Good feedback teaches students to evaluate and adjust themselves, and how to think about their own progress. It carries concrete, specific detail that anchors the student in action, and an emotional component: the choice of words conveys respect, support and positive regard. Rephrase judgments into observations about effects, or into questions:

- "I can't hear a word you're saying" → "I can hear your voice so much better when you lift your chin and speak up like that. Then your passion in the speech really comes through."
- "This painting is just a mess" → "I don't think your attempt to bring these elements together has really worked this time, and I have a feeling you didn't fully convey your meaning with your use of color here."
- "The reason you keep injuring yourself is because of your grip on the bat" → "What do you think is the effect of holding your bat like that? What do you think would happen if you tried holding it a little higher up?"

## Checking your feedback

Regularly ask:

1. **Does my feedback refer to the goal?** It is most focused when it answers whether the action brings the student closer to or further from their stated goal. This is why clear goals matter; sometimes reminding the student of the bigger aim is correction enough.
2. **Is it specific, concrete and actionable?** Can the student genuinely do something with it? Criticizing a student for not performing beyond their real capability is useless. Keep judgments, assumptions and expectations out, and give plain, neutral facts and precisely what to *do*.
3. **Is it appropriate for this student?** Feedback is communication, and communication fails if it isn't received. Are you speaking in a way this student understands?
4. **Does it carry meaningful information on the task, the process or the performance?** Does it give genuine insight and a learning opportunity?

**Good feedback:**

- is clear, purposeful, meaningful and compatible with prior knowledge;
- is focused on the learning intention and success criteria;
- occurs as students are doing the learning, which is why verbal feedback is much more effective than written;
- provides information on how and why the student has or has not met the criteria;
- provides strategies for improvement.

**John Hattie's three questions.** Feedback is useful when it answers "where am I going?", "how am I going?" and "where to next?", which closes the gap between where the student is and where their learning goals say they should be.

## Feedback to the teacher, and errors

Feedback also runs the other way. In **formative assessment**, students show the teacher what they have learnt. Teachers can also use student assessment data, and ask students for feedback in lesson plenaries, on how well their teaching is working. In explicit instruction, teachers give instructive feedback during the "we do" and "you do" phases and treat errors as useful, correcting them immediately to keep students on track for the learning goals. This **"error training"** raises performance when the teacher has created a safe environment where students are comfortable taking risks. Setting achievement goals and giving ongoing feedback on performance has a powerful, positive impact on learning.
