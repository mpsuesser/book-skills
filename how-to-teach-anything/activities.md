# Activities

Concrete activities for gauging understanding, explaining, asking questions and giving feedback, in a classroom, one-to-one or with a partner. Teaching has to keep coming back to the concrete, and inside the right framework even a simple activity takes on new value. Choose an activity only after you know where the student is and where the lesson is taking them, and judge it by whether it lets this student really understand the material. The better you understand what you're teaching, the better you can pick techniques that actually do their job.

## Group and pair activities

- **Think-pair-share.** Students think about a task alone, then share with a partner, then share the combined result with the larger group. It encourages comparative and collaborative thinking, supports debate and discussion, and surfaces aspects students hadn't considered. It is ideal for smaller groups; with large groups too much time goes on discussing potentially wrong answers, leaving little for teaching.
- **Mini quizzes.** These appraise the learning process, not the content. At the end of the lesson each student writes the main thing they learnt, one thing they still find difficult, and any questions. Use the answers to structure the next lesson; writing them encourages self-reflection.
- **Recruit student teachers.** Break the lesson into chunks and give each small group one chunk to understand. Then shuffle the groups so students must pass their part on and see where the others' parts fit with theirs. Each student is teacher and student at once, and they find the causal connections in the bigger system themselves instead of having you outline it. Or use a student teaching assistant, whose recent experience as a student lets them contextualize material well; have them introduce concepts while you take the complex parts.
- **Assign a project.** Frame the activity as a project the student directs and assembles (a speech, presentation or essay) with your feedback and guidance, and use it to assess their process and pose questions. It works well for real-world tasks with a strong practical element. Have students set the rubric themselves and decide how to measure their performance against the project's outcomes.
- **Essays or debate.** Build the lesson around a single prompt and have students respond with an essay or argument, then present it, submit it, or debate it. Have them grade and rate example pieces before or after writing their own. Or split the class into groups, assign each a position, and have them defend it. This builds critical thinking about the logical structure of arguments and gives a model for organizing their own.
- **Encourage synthesis.** Have students synthesize two different sources (a discussion and a textbook chapter, or two very different books) to arrive at a third, unique perspective. It shows quickly where their understanding stands and where the gaps are, and it makes them think for themselves about consequences and implications. It is especially useful in the social sciences, where three philosophers asked the meaning of life give three answers built on different conceptions of life, and comparing frameworks gives a holistic grasp of the shared concept.
- **Use observation.** Have students watch an interaction, discussion or activity and respond with notes, questions or their own analysis, so that other students' work becomes data to unpack. Silent observation targets attention, which you can then direct into summaries or outlines. Have some students debate while the others observe and evaluate their strategies.

These all let students work interactively and proactively, give you the chance to gauge their deeper understanding, and create an opening for meaningful feedback. Their strength is that students communicate, compare with others, and build understanding through self-explanation.

## Visual activities

With one student, or a topic unsuited to group work, use visual activities. Group work and visuals do the same job: they make the student's internal thinking external, so you can gauge their level, correct them and guide them. Visual information has the added advantage of being intuitive and may be easier to grasp and retain.

- **Concept or relationship maps.** These make connections between concepts explicit. Construction rules and teaching uses are in [mapping-and-explaining.md](mapping-and-explaining.md).
- **Flowcharts and process diagrams.** Use these for cause and effect, anything that unfolds procedurally in time, or decision-making and conditional ("if X then Y") logic. Have students flowchart a scientific experiment, including the processes for differing outcomes. Hand out a simple flowchart of a complex procedure at the start so students can see where they are as the lesson goes, or teach first and have students compile a summarizing flowchart. Give them construction rules, or let them devise their own conventions and symbols.
- **Storyboards.** Simple comics, visuals or diagrams that present sequential information and highlight its most salient points. Because they take time, assign one as a project, or as a group project producing a summary or study aid everyone will use.
- **Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagrams.** These lay out the cause-and-effect relationships behind a specific outcome. The fish's head is the problem or effect, and the bones running to it are causes grouped into categories. For failing an exam, one bone might be "home environment," and asking what caused that gives distractions at home, behind which lie further causes (family visiting unannounced, easy access to electronic devices). It is "a directional mind map that seeks to answer the question of why something has happened": keep asking why of each cause to get past superficial symptoms to the root causes. It works backwards, almost forensically, on a completed situation such as a historical event, a hypothetical, or the student's own performance in self-reflection. Teachers can also use one privately to understand how a student arrived where they are.
- **Venn diagrams.** These quickly show where two or more topics overlap and where they differ, more clearly than prose. Draw one circle per trait or idea and place each item according to which traits it has. The book's exercise sorts animals by three traits, can swim, can breathe air and has legs: jellyfish only swim, earthworms and chimpanzees breathe air but can't swim, and humans, dogs, whales, sea turtles and water moccasins sit in the overlap of swimming and air-breathing, with humans and dogs also in the circle for legs. The result is three intersecting circles showing which traits each animal has. Placing the items takes some practice.
- **KWHL chart.** Four columns: What do I **k**now? What do I **w**ant to know? **H**ow can I find out? What have I **l**earnt? Laid out beforehand, the four sub-goals shape an inquiry and focus a self-directed project, and the last column builds in self-appraisal at the end. The student structures their own learning much as a teacher designs a lesson (the inquiry-based approach). With a student stuck on an accounting or bookkeeping concept, have them map what they know and don't, then find quality sources to fill the gap, apply critical thinking to those sources, and reflect on how well the attempt worked by returning to the first column: what do I know now? More than before?

Visual tools convey a lot of information quickly and concisely, highlight relationships (causal connections, similarities and differences, sequences), and organize the material so you can gauge understanding, give feedback, and correct or expand where needed.

## Combining activities

Techniques combine and adapt freely; "the only limit is your imagination."

- To give in-depth understanding of a complex procedure, have students watch a role play of the procedure going wrong, take notes, and build a fishbone diagram from them.
- To give a rich grasp of current theory, have one student closely read, annotate and summarize a text and another do the same with a different text. Together they build a Venn diagram of the texts' key similarities, then each writes an essay alone expanding on those similarities, synthesizing a new analysis and their own response.
- Have students debate one another or you while building a flowchart of the debate process, then close with a mini quiz with KWHL elements. Can they reflect on their progress and suggest what the next lesson should include?
