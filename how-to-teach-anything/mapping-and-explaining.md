# Mapping and explaining

Tools for building the student's map and explaining new material through what they already know: concept maps, problematizing, the Feynman technique, and analogies.

## Concept maps

A literal mind map isn't required, and the right layout depends on the topic, but any topic can be broken into smaller, simpler subunits that connect meaningfully. To find them, ask:

- Are there categories, or steps in a sequence?
- Can the topic be seen through different lenses, layers or models?
- Which simple concepts must be in place before the bigger idea can be grasped?

Fit the form to the topic. For photosynthesis the student needs the chemical processes, the physiological structures in the cell where they happen, and a way to join the two: a table of which process occurs, in what order, in what part of the cell. The table also serves as the student's summary to study from. A non-academic skill benefits just as much. To teach a wedding bouquet, build a loose mind map together: the general aim first, then a sequence that builds (the "skeleton" of the bouquet, then contrasting foliage types, then smaller supporting flowers in a zigzag pattern, then accent flowers). What makes any map work is that it shows the relationships between subunits, so the student can pull them into a unified whole. A good concept map is relevant, simple, accurate, and draws on existing mental models and knowledge.

**Constructing one.** Nodes are ideas and the links carry information about their relationship. Start with a main concept and give the map hierarchy or structure. Use arrows and boxes, group related concepts clearly, and vary the arrow style for different kinds of relationship (a darker line for a stronger one). Move from the general to the specific and from the known to the unknown.

**Concept map vs mind map.** A concept map is almost always more organized. A mind map suits the start of a lesson, to gauge current knowledge and surface everything known about a topic. Brainstorming is freer and open-ended, good for stimulating discussion: put the main topic in the center and let the student elaborate with your guidance.

**Ways to use one in teaching:**

- Plan a lesson with it, explain with it, or recap a finished lesson.
- Have the student build their own. It shows what they've retained and where they struggle, gives you direction for quizzing, and is a learning experience in itself; teach while correcting and fine-tuning it. Many students have trouble not with the material itself but with how their isolated pieces of information fit together.
- Give a half-finished map for the student to complete as the lesson goes, or a deliberately incorrect one to correct.
- Use it to drive inquiry. The student makes predictions or hypotheses from the map and tests them; for non-practical topics, thought experiments and hypothetical questions do the same job (the inquiry-based approach). Teaching soldering, the map includes what solder is made of and why. The student predicts that changing the composition changes the melting point, tests it on different base materials, and so teaches themselves how composition, melting point and properties relate. Later they can diagnose a poor solder, or predict its composition from its appearance. They have internalized their own mental map and can navigate the topic without a teacher, which is why integrated or contextual learning works: "We don't just know something, we understand it, from the inside out."

## Problematize what students already believe

Once you know what the student already believes, problematizing it is "easily the best way to gain attention, foster curiosity, and motivate students" to learn more about something they find they don't know as well as they thought. Expose the gaps in the belief without telling them they're wrong.

On the first day of a course on nations and nationalism, have students write short pieces on what a nation is. In the next class, ask the authors of the most common answers to elaborate, then point out each answer's problem. If a nation is a group of people living somewhere, could you settle somewhere with a couple of friends and call it a nation? If it is a collective of people with the same ethnicity, why do many nations have several ethnicities in roughly the same proportion? Neither definition is wrong, only incomplete, and showing that nudges students to clarify their existing knowledge and add nuance to it.

## The Feynman technique

Named for Richard Feynman, the Nobel-prize-winning physicist known as the "Great Explainer," who prided himself on explaining the most complex ideas in the simplest terms. It is a tool of **metacognition**, "thinking about thinking," like **elaborative interrogation** (questioning yourself to see the whole picture behind a piece of information). It works on almost any subject and gauges whether you really understand a topic or have glossed over important concepts. Its information is what you *can't* answer, so answer honestly. Teacher and student both use it to find their blind spots.

1. **Choose your concept.** For example, gravity: to understand its basics, to explain it to someone, or to test how well you know it.
2. **Write down an explanation of the concept in plain English.** This is the important step, and the simpler and shorter the explanation, the harder it is. "If you can boil information or a topic down to two sentences in a way that a five-year-old would understand, you probably have a level of mastery over it." Find where the explanation falls apart. The signs are "Well, you know ... it's gravity," or an explanation that is long, rambling and deflective. You may be able to explain the ends (what happens with zero gravity, what causes gravity) while skipping everything in between.
3. **Find your blind spots.** Research whatever you couldn't explain until you can describe it simply ("The force that causes larger objects to attract smaller objects because of their weight and mass"). If you can't summarize it in one sentence, or at least briefly, blind spots remain. This is non-negotiable. Seemingly simple test questions: why is the sky blue? How do television remotes work? How does lightning appear? What are clouds made of? What is digestion? You can answer each on the surface, but then what?
4. **Use an analogy.** An analogy requires deep understanding of the main traits of both things, so it is the true test of understanding (gravity is like stepping into a puddle, with the leaves on the surface drawn to the mass of your foot). It also connects new information to old, so you can piggyback on a working mental model. If you can do steps two and three but not four, you have found the boundary of your knowledge.

The technique is "a rapid way to discover what you know versus what you think you know."

## Analogies

Analogies connect old knowledge with new. They give instant understanding and context: thought focuses on one familiar concept and then slowly differentiates the new one from it. Douglas Hofstadter (Indiana University) holds that analogies are the foundation of all human thought. Identifying likenesses is how we form categories. A dog and a cat look similar, but each is more closely analogous to its own species. Higher-order ideas form the same way: "mammal" groups the criteria of lactating, having hair or fur, and being warm-blooded, which makes a dolphin and a platypus analogous though no one would compare a dolphin to a housecat. Consciously distinguishing elements and making analogies while learning speeds the integration of new knowledge.

**The two-part analogy move.** For "It's like the Uber of X, except A, B, and C": first find a similar, familiar model. Uber gives the mental image of people with their own cars driving or delivering people or things, and getting there requires understanding both things well enough to compare them. Then state how the new thing differs: what makes it something other than a clone of Uber? "That's where the deeper learning synthesis occurs." To learn how a new piece of legislation is created, first find a familiar process it reminds you of, then pick out small details that look similar but come from totally different motivations, and write down what that means for the legislation.

**Four tips for analogies:**

1. **Use multiple analogies for the same topic.** Each type tests understanding differently, so use as many relevant ones as you can. The three types, shown on liberalism:
   - **Antonym**: as hot is to cold, liberalism is to communism or conservatism;
   - **Example/Type Of**: liberalism is a type of political ideology, as the iPhone is a type of smartphone;
   - **Thing/Characteristic**: as volume is a characteristic of speakers, human rights is a characteristic of liberalism.
2. **Use examples to constantly reaffirm learning** (from studies by Daniel Schwartz and John Bransford). Novices learn through their knowledge of an example's content, while experts can skip examples. For ethical systems: should you lie to a friend by saying you're busy when you don't want to talk? What is the fairest way to divide a pie among three people? Examples make dry content realistic.
3. **Remember the purpose of the analogy.** Students say mitochondria is "the powerhouse of the cell" without knowing what that means. Frame the analogy so its purpose shows (a powerhouse provides power, more accurately energy), and list its drawbacks ("powerhouse" suggests merely storing energy, when mitochondria extract, process and release it). Know why the analogy fits, not just the analogy.
4. **Reserve analogies for more difficult concepts.** On easy material they cause "mental confusion and clutter."

Keep a list of the analogies you use with the drawbacks of each, and add visual cues: visual plus text cues improve retention, according to multimedia learning theory. In the academic analogy format (A is to B as C is to D), choose a comparison for the left-hand side that makes the relationship clear without rereading.
