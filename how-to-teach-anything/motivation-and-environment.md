# Motivation and the student environment

The **student environment** is more than the physical surroundings: student, teacher, environment and material stand in close relationship, and an effective teacher keeps all four aligned. Teachers often struggle less with the material than with inspiring students who simply don't care. "There are always two lessons running in parallel": the material itself, and in the background the discipline, stamina and self-regulation the student learns while getting through it. This file covers motivation, gamification, academic buoyancy, productive failure, and freedom from judgment. Feedback is in [feedback.md](feedback.md).

## Understanding motivation

"Unless a student is motivated to learn, they won't learn." Without motivation, neither intelligence nor the value of the material matters.

**Expectancy theory**: people act according to the outcomes they *expect* to follow their actions. Often without knowing it, a person:

1. assesses how likely it is that a given effort will lead to a predictable outcome or change in performance (*how hard do I have to work?*);
2. assesses how directly the effort is linked to a desired outcome (*what will that work get me?*);
3. decides how appealing that outcome is personally, by their needs and values (*how valuable is this outcome to me?*).

A student in PE has no reason to try if they'll be graded well regardless, if the PE grade barely affects the goal they care about (passing the year), or if PE just doesn't matter in their life. Effort, performance and reward must all line up, or motivation won't last. No outcome appeals to everyone; what counts is what this student perceives as valuable, and your own conviction that the topic matters is beside the point. Motivation runs on expectation of the future, so "the reality of the situation is irrelevant—what makes the difference is your student's perceptions." A student who doesn't know what to expect from you or the material can lack motivation even for something in their best interest. Even an already motivated student needs their motivation rekindled after challenges, setbacks or stalls.

**Moves:**

- **Increase the perceived value of the material.** Learn the student's values and principles, then connect the material to them. Someone indifferent to web design will learn some once it serves what they do care about: staying competitive in their industry and saving the time and money of hiring others to fix their business site.
- **Make tasks authentic.** Use hands-on tasks, not "phony hypotheticals," so the student sees the real-world value of the skill. State its usefulness "according to their estimations, not yours!"
- **Build in regular rewards.** Use praise and feedback, stop now and then to acknowledge progress, and encourage the student to feel proud of their achievements. Genuine enthusiasm from you rubs off.
- **Build expectation of a positive outcome.** That expectation is what carries a student through difficult or boring patches. Be crystal clear about the future: what the goals are, why you're aiming for them, and how you will both measure success.
- **Keep the challenge in the sweet spot.** It should be neither too difficult nor too easy, so that overcoming it genuinely feels like an achievement.
- **Be fair.** Inconsistent rules or unexplained favoritism kill motivation. Students need "a direct, predictable and quantifiable relationship between what they put in and what they get out," and your feedback must tell them exactly why they succeeded or failed.

## Gamification

**Gamification** is "the use of video game principles and elements in a non-gaming context." Make learning fun and the student wants more of it without force or self-discipline, as a playing kitten learns the motor skills for hunting while having the time of its life. The game elements:

- **Step-wise progress.** Levels, points, beating competitors. The student knows how progress is defined and what steps it takes. Platforms such as Moodle, Canvas and Piazza can post modules and assignments and show the percentage covered, so a progress number keeps going up.
- **Narrative.** A story, sometimes with characters, moving through a fixed field (a map, a board, a chronology). Present a course as one long story: teach the American Revolution through differing perspectives and theories on how it started and unfolded, how it was perceived then and now, and good anecdotes, not as a list of events. Scaffolding comes naturally as challenge increases, with a chance to **level up** always on the horizon (badges, newly unlocked tools and skills).
- **The player in control of play.** The player doesn't set the rules but makes the decisions and watches the outcomes. With high enough stakes this gives both challenge and achievement. Feedback is immediate, so the player adapts in real time, and the clear link between action and consequence makes each action feel relevant.
- **Collaboration.** Teamwork and strategic communication on a bigger shared problem; social connection makes the topic feel meaningful.

You can gamify along two paths: alter the **structure** through which content is presented, or alter the **content**. For vocabulary, gamifying the structure means sorting words into levels and drilling them with flashcards, where beating the difficult "boss" word moves the student up a level. Gamifying the content means framing teacher and student as opposing players: you "attack" with words, they defend with accurate definitions, and they win "XP" or move an avatar along a board. Nothing about vocabulary is battle-like, and the introduced content makes the existing content easier to engage with.

A game sets the student up as an active agent who tries things and adapts without fear of failure or serious consequences, in "a simpler, safer version of real life" where they explore like a scientist: How does this work? Why? What do I have to do? How can I get from A to B, and what are the rules limiting my actions? A gamified approach satisfies all three expectancy conditions, and the content becomes a "Player Two" that responds to the student. Keep asking how to meet the student where they are and challenge them afresh as they learn.

Not all play leads to learning. Before running a game, ask:

- Have you set up a step-like sequence of tasks that increase in challenge incrementally?
- Have you spelled everything out, or left the student to discover the game mechanisms for themselves (far more interesting)?
- Does the game give immediate feedback, so the student can change their behavior and quickly try again?
- Is it free from "seriousness": is it really safe to try things without penalty?
- Is it very clear how to level up, and is the challenge appropriate to their ability?
- Have you clearly set out the goal, purpose and rules, including what the student can and can't control?

During play, watch for frustration versus engagement. Raise the challenge if they're advancing too fast, and pace and mix things up just as they grow too familiar; then they reach the "flow" of the game. Where your subject resists game mechanics, change the language: quests and missions instead of worksheets and exercises; XP, rankings and hidden levels to unlock instead of grades; guilds instead of group work.

### Extrinsic versus intrinsic motivation

**Intrinsic motivation** is acting from forces within our own perceptions, attitudes or beliefs, doing a thing for its own sake. **Extrinsic motivation** is acting because of an external force such as reward or punishment, to get access to something else. "Gamification is a reward-based system that relies completely on extrinsic motivation." "If you want to merely get the student engaged and paying attention, there's nothing better than gamification." To kindle a love of the subject that outlasts the game gimmicks, it may not be enough; some knowledge is valuable only for its own sake. The ideal is a student genuinely interested in the material, which requires that they feel their success is under their control, that they have the necessary skills, and that the goal is worth attaining.

Students respond to games differently:

- the **"player"** enjoys the competition, the rewards and the desire to win;
- the **"socializer"** values the relational and cooperative side;
- the **"achiever"** hardly cares about the mechanisms and has their eye on the prize;
- the **"philanthropist"** cares about the game's overall meaning and value, win or lose.

Gamification is no substitute for knowing your student's personality. Some respond badly to it: through the **"overjustification" effect**, rewarding a behavior can make it seem less desirable, and such a student may do better without pressure, competition or external incentives. Find out the deeper source of the student's motivation, so a reward system doesn't detract from their natural interest or offer rewards that mean nothing to them. Even enthusiasts tire of games and look for something more meaningful, and like any addiction the user builds tolerance and needs ever bigger rewards. Warning sign: the student starts ticking boxes, rushing an exercise to win its points without doing it with care. "There is no replacement for occasional diligence and effort." Whether to gamify comes down to whether it works in this particular instance.

## Academic buoyancy

**Academic buoyancy** is not giving up in the face of learning challenges. Like intelligence, it is not inborn: it is a set of learnable skills and cultivable habits. Many people "take themselves out of the running by giving up at the first sign of hardship." Researchers from the University of Sydney and the University of Oxford identified **five Cs** that produce it: **composure, confidence, coordination, commitment, and control**. Most obstacles to learning are about mindset rather than content, and belief and perseverance are what separate effective learners: "Their influence is far, far greater than any of the techniques in this book." Missing any of the five sets a learner up for failure; they are "more prerequisites to effective learning than tactics in themselves." Teachers should always encourage them.

- **Composure** is the ability to manage and minimize anxiety. Learning anxiety usually comes from fear of shame and embarrassment: what if people find out I'm learning this and expect me to show it, and I fail? At worst, the worry crowds out attention to the new material. The book calls these fears "entirely baseless." The engine is **catastrophization**: ignoring the realistic consequences and jumping to drastic conclusions, as if the world ends on failure. Beat it by managing **self-talk**. Acknowledge that bad things may happen, but that many of the thoughts are irrational and fictitious, and consider alternative explanations and outcomes. Counter worry with optimism, and a self-berating thought with the reminder that a mistake is a learning opportunity. Every negative thought can be honestly countered with a positive, encouraging, forgiving, accepting alternative, and with persistence the brain comes to accept the retorts as more valid.
- **Confidence**, or **self-efficacy**, is the belief that you can perform a specific task. Without it, people talk themselves down, belittle their progress, and give up early, and giving up is failing too. Two techniques. (1) *Self-talk*: counter the thought that a subject is too hard for you with the assertion that you will keep studying and, with time and effort, succeed. (2) *Goal-setting*: confidence grows from a track record. Set daily or even hourly study goals, meet them over and over, and congratulate yourself each time. Each one met is evidence the confidence is real.
- **Coordination** is the ability to plan and manage time. The trap is **the Planning Fallacy**: people underestimate how long tasks take, then put them off because there seems to be plenty of time. Minimize distractions (phone off, door closed, family told you're busy), start soon after receiving the task, and do the longest, most difficult task first. Leaving it for last breeds false security and unfinished work.
- **Commitment**, or **grit**, is a combination of passion and persistence. A day or a week of study is easy; habits are what fail. Use self-talk, recruit others to encourage you when you're flagging (which strengthens personal responsibility), and keep in mind what you're committing for: What dreams does this information help you attain? What hardships will be removed once you master it?
- **Control** is feeling that we control our fate. First, believe you have the ability to reach the outcome. Innate intelligence "doesn't really affect ninety-nine percent of us in the middle of the bell curve"; hard work makes the result possible, and struggle is part of the process. "Discomfort should be the expectation, not the exception." Second, own the learning process. Ownership brings the personal responsibility that keeps a learner working through setbacks, while being merely told what to do makes study feel futile. Make sure of the goals and tailor everyday work to them, creating your own plan rather than drifting toward other people's expectations.

Academic buoyancy is perhaps better framed as **resilience**, the ability to adapt to stressful situations and "roll with the punches." People who handle minor stresses well also handle major crises better.

- **Susan Kobasa**'s three elements of resilience: (1) looking at difficulties as a challenge; (2) committing to the goal no matter what; (3) limiting efforts and concerns to factors within one's control.
- **Martin Seligman**'s three: (1) seeing negative events as temporary and limited; (2) not letting negative events define oneself or one's perspective; (3) not overly blaming or denigrating oneself for them.

## Productive failure

**Productive failure** (Manu Kapur, National Institute of Education, Singapore) builds on the **learning paradox**: not reaching the desired result can be as valuable as succeeding, or more so, in its neurological rather than emotional impact. The accepted model gives structure and guidance early and keeps supporting students until they get it. Kapur held it is better to let students flounder by themselves without outside help.

In his trial, one group solved problems with full instructional support while a second got no teacher help and had to collaborate. The supported group solved the problems; the unsupported group did not. But the second group dug deeper: they generated ideas about the nature of the problems, speculated on what solutions might look like, and explored roots, methods, and multiple approaches and angles. On the subsequent test the unsupported group significantly outperformed the supported one, and on new problems they used the knowledge they had generated better than "the passive recipients of an instructor's expertise" used theirs. Kapur called this the **"hidden efficacy"** of failure: the miscues, mistakes and fumbling were the important part.

**Kapur's three conditions for productive failure:**

1. Choose problems that "challenge but do not frustrate."
2. Give learners the chance to explain and elaborate their processes.
3. Allow learners to compare and contrast good and bad solutions.

Struggle is a positive condition for learning, but it demands discipline and delayed gratification and runs against instinct. To make failure work:

- **Expect but don't succumb to frustration.** Anticipate frustration and plan how to relieve it, usually by taking a break and getting momentary distance. Pausing lets objectivity seep in and shows the sticking point more clearly. The skill is being comfortable with mental discomfort and confusion.
- **Learning mode is not results mode.** They have different measures of success: in learning, any increase in knowledge is success. Make the learning as important as the result, or more.
- **Know where it applies.** Explicit, static knowledge such as facts and dates doesn't need productive failure. Deep, layered comprehension has to be manipulated and applied, and failure is inherent in that. Failures work like questions, triangulating understanding from what doesn't work and isn't true. Failure is "a blueprint for our next steps," a test run that pinpoints what to fix. If some vegetables in a garden fail, investigate why that soil was wrong and what it needs, or learn placement techniques if plants were too close together.
- **Seek success rather than avoid failure.** Living to avoid failure limits exposure and risk, while seeking success stays focused on the end goal. Failure will be "your occasional companion," so take the approach with more risk and more reward.

## Freedom from judgment

One of the best lessons a teacher can give is a healthy attitude toward risk and failure, and the teacher's own mindset communicates what the parameters and values of learning are. A learning environment **free from judgment** lets students take risks and try new things without feeling stupid. "Judgment is an ego game": it belongs to the **fixed mindset**, which treats knowledge as a way to bolster identity or win arguments. When self-worth depends on success, failure, mistakes, ignorance and delay feel like attacks on identity, and the thought becomes "I'm a failure" instead of "I failed." Only the second leads to self-correction. Clinging to always being right makes us less resilient and less able to learn.

The price of mastery is being a beginner who constantly faces their own ignorance, which makes tolerating failure, uncertainty, ambiguity and complexity a **meta-skill**. A good teacher lets the student experiment, try, fail, adjust and ask questions safely, with no consequences for their self-worth. Disconnect performance from identity, and model nonjudgment, so the student feels safe to make purposeful mistakes.

**Balance positive and negative.** Constant failure demoralizes, and success and pride motivate too. The **Losada Ratio** (Marcial Losada and Barbara Frederickson) claimed an ideal ratio of positive to negative experiences of between about three and eleven to one: around three times as much positive as negative lets people flourish, and above 11:1 (the **"Losada Line"**) the gains are lost. The original paper has been seriously criticized for lacking scientific validity and the concept has been debunked. Drop the specific numbers, but keep the point: each student likely has an ideal balance of challenge and ease, achievement and disappointment, which may shift daily or by subject, and most people work best when the positive outweighs the negative. Work dynamically with the student in front of you.
