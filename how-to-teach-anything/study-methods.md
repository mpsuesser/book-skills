# Study methods

Methods for working with material, which the teacher uses to structure a course or teaches a student to use alone: SQ3R, Bloom's taxonomy, spaced repetition, Cornell notes, and purposeful annotation. Each is the same process seen from the teacher's side or the student's (teacher-training programs have trainees play the student for exactly this reason). Choose a method only once you know the landscape and the route: "A huge mistake is to adopt some trendy or important-sounding technique that does nothing but get in the way of learning." Notes in particular are not automatically necessary.

## The SQ3R method

Francis P. Robinson's method for making reading active. It scales from one book to a whole self-study plan. Five steps: **survey, question, read, recite, review**. "The SQ3R method is no joke": it takes patience and organization, and gets easier each time.

**Survey.** Get the overview before Chapter 1; diving in without it is "going in blind." It is like looking at the whole road map before a trip: when you're confused you'll know you generally need to head southwest. Examine the structure: title, introduction or preface, section and chapter titles, headings and subheadings, pictures and graphics, and conventions such as typefaces, bold or italic text, chapter objectives and study questions. These set expectations and a framework for your reading goals. The parts of John S. Shelton's *Geology Illustrated* ("Materials," "Structure," "Sculpture," "Time," "Case Histories," "Implications") are enough to guess the book's arc. Knowing the significance of a piece is the difference between "a single gear in isolation versus seeing where and how it works in a complex clock."

Beyond a single book, survey all the important concepts of the discipline. If no table of contents exists, build one: you are plotting "a metaphorical 'book' for yourself." Specify exactly what you want to know, because "psychology" is too broad, whereas early psychoanalysis, Freud and Jung, sports psychology or developmental psychology are workable. Note the phrases and concepts that recur across sources, since they are likely essential, and sketch connections and cause-and-effect relationships before studying any of them in detail. For European cinema history, gather the authoritative books, the important films (IMDB), the most-cited directors, the highest-rated films and why, and the national movements. Then organize them into a plan: a chapter on early European film, then films from that era, then a self-assigned film review. At this step you only gather and organize, without touching the resources yet.

**Question.** Turn chapter titles, headings and subheadings into questions, which converts a dry title into a problem to solve. "Foundations of Freud's Analyses of Dreams" becomes "How did Sigmund Freud's work on dream interpretation originate and what were his very first ideas on the subject?" Paraphrase into your own wording as you do it: "Some Effects of Metamorphism on Sedimentary Rocks" becomes "What can happen to bottom-centered rocks through eons of environmental change?" Pencil the questions into the margins; end-of-chapter study questions are ready-made guides. Write down the specific answers you hope to find, turn recurring names into questions (Why was Fellini so influential? What themes did Godard pursue in his filmmaking?) and recurring themes such as French New Wave and neo-realism into targets in your outline, and set up where you'll record the answers: a daily journal, a self-administered quiz, a "knowledge tracker."

**Read.** Now read, looking for answers to your questions, with the anticipation the first two steps have built. "This step is where most people try to start but fail because they lack a foundation and instead have unreasonable expectations." Slow down a lot. Read difficult passages extremely slowly, go back and reread anything unclear, and take one section at a time. Treat videos and courses the same way, using rewind and scrolling. Watch films with a critical eye, listen to director's commentary tracks, and cross-check sources against one another.

**Recite.** This is literal recitation, and it is the biggest difference between reading to learn and reading for entertainment. Ask questions about the material out loud, take copious margin notes, and underline or highlight key points. Always restate them in your own words rather than copying phrases. A geology sentence comparing the slow erosion of hills and mountains to the rapid changes seen in miniature everywhere becomes "Mountains and hills experience the same decay as happens in lowlands and rivers, just more slowly. Similar to baseball players." That puts one piece of information into two distinct phrases, one of them self-made, and adds a personal hook (the author likes baseball). Repeated through a whole book, this multiplies learning capacity. It works across media: for Bergman's *The Seventh Seal*, write questions on its Biblical references, art direction and cinematography, write a summary or video blog, or compare it with other directors. The rephrasing must be meaningful "to you—and no one else."

**Review.** Go back over the most important points and commit them to memory: write more questions on the highlighted parts, answer questions orally, review notes, make flashcards for key concepts and terms ("especially effective"), rewrite the table of contents in your own words, build a mind map, draw a flowchart of a process (glaciation), make a timeline linking eras to their major changes, build a catalog of directors' works, themes and styles, and keep a journal. Note the questions the material left unanswered. Review is the natural continuation of the survey: step back, reevaluate, and make updated, more accurate connections.

## Bloom's taxonomy

Benjamin Bloom's taxonomy (1956, updated 2001) was built to measure the academic performance of college students and became a staple framework for crafting lessons. For teaching and learning it is "a literal step-by-step guideline" for moving understanding forward: six sequential levels, lowest to highest, each depending on mastery of the one before.

1. **Remember**: retrieving, recognizing, and recalling relevant knowledge from long-term memory. Listening, finding information, memorizing, bookmarking, highlighting, drilling by repetition, tabulating into bullet points, outlining key features or quotes, defining main ideas; most exam revision.
2. **Understand**: constructing meaning from oral, written, and graphic messages through interpreting, exemplifying, classifying, summarizing, inferring, comparing, and explaining. Picking information apart to see how it works: categorizing, chunking, inferring and predicting, paraphrasing. Asking for an answer "in their own words" tests understanding rather than memorization. Deep comprehension lets you manipulate a thing however its parts are rearranged or expressed, and relating it to an example or metaphor the listener already knows builds that understanding.
3. **Apply**: carrying out or using a procedure for executing or implementing. Bringing information into the real world: executing, sketching, acting out, articulating, painting, preparing, displaying, reenacting, playing; making a pie chart of data, turning a plan into reality, running an experiment.
4. **Analyze**: breaking material into constituent parts and determining how the parts relate to one another and to an overall structure or purpose through differentiating, organizing, and attributing. Questioning, explaining, organizing, deconstructing, correlating, calculating; drawing a mind map, integrating one set of ideas with another, taking a machine apart, asking "Why is this happening?" Analysis is value-neutral.
5. **Evaluate**: making judgments based on criteria and standards through checking and critiquing. Criticizing, rating, reflecting, reviewing, assessing, validating; weighing information against stated goals. How useful are the results of the experiment? What are the quality and veracity of the claims? How well did you perform?
6. **Create**: putting elements together to form a coherent or functional whole, or reorganizing them into a new pattern or structure, through generating, planning, or producing. Composing, mixing known things into something new, filming, scripting, role-playing, programming, designing systems, adapting material from one form to another, podcasting, blogging. Bloom counted leading as creative.

Reaching **create** means a deep grasp of the subject, and most people never get through all the levels. Skipping levels is the failure mode: someone without adequate understanding of a topic evaluates and passes judgment on it. The challenge is introspection, knowing where you or your student currently falls on the taxonomy, because that shows what is needed to move forward. Use the levels to shape progressive challenge.

The categories overlap, and the point is not to sort cleanly but to work the same material from as many angles as possible, like a toolbox of differently colored glasses. One pass up the levels: highlight to summarize (remember), paraphrase (understand), build your own chart or diagram (apply), break it down, question it and link it to earlier diagrams (analyze), judge whether these methods are helping you retain the material (evaluate), and use that judgment to design a better learning system (create). It is tedious, but "this tough mental work and struggle" is what cements learning.

## Spaced repetition

**Spaced repetition**, or **distributed practice**, fights forgetting directly. Memory has three parts, encoding, storage and retrieval, and spaced repetition works on retrieval. Spread rehearsal over as long a period as possible: one hour a day beats twenty hours in one weekend, and "seeing something twenty times in one day is far less effective for memory than seeing something ten times over the course of seven days." The brain is like a muscle: it needs recovery, sleep is where neural connections form, and an athlete who overtrains in one session is either exhausted for the second half or injured. "Sometimes effort isn't what's required."

Devise the schedule with the student so they understand it and own it. The book's week for a lesson on Spanish history:

| When | Session |
|---|---|
| Mon 10:00 a.m. | Learn the material (five pages of notes) |
| Mon 8:00 p.m. | Review by recalling from memory first, not rereading (about 20 min) |
| Tue 10:00 a.m. | Recall without notes, then check what you missed and mark it (about 15 min) |
| Tue 8:00 p.m. | Review notes (10 min) |
| Wed 4:00 p.m. | Recall independently, then look at notes only to see what's missing (10 min) |
| Thu 6:00 p.m. | Review notes (10 min) |
| Fri 10:00 a.m. | Active recall session (10 min) |

That is seventy-five extra minutes for six additional passes through the lesson, and the student is ready for a test by Friday afternoon. The first couple of exposures show nothing new. After that the learner stops going through the motions, examines the material more deeply, and relates it to other concepts. Cramming gets little into long-term memory and turns into rote memorization that fades quickly.

Measure the number of times the material is revisited after first learning it, not the hours spent: "increase the frequency of reviewing, not necessarily the duration."

**When short on time** (cramming for a test), the material only has to get slightly past working memory and stick for a few hours. Simulate spacing: rather than three hours at night, study one hour three times a day with hours between, or learn it on waking and review at noon, 4:00 p.m. and 9:00 p.m. In every repetition:

- study the notes out of order, so they're seen in different contexts;
- use active recall instead of passive reading;
- intersperse unrelated material (**interleaved practice**);
- focus on the underlying concepts, so you can make educated guesses about what you've forgotten;
- keep reciting up to the last minute; short-term memory holds seven items "on its best day," and one of them might be the one you need.

## Cornell notes

A note-taking method is worth something only if it genuinely helps the student understand and retain. The Cornell method:

1. **Set up the page.** On a handwritten sheet ("writing by hand is key"), draw two columns, the right about twice the width of the left. Label the right "Notes" and the left "Cues." Leave a couple of inches at the bottom and label it "Summary."
2. **Notes.** Take notes only in the right column during the lesson or reading: the bigger concepts with supporting detail, as concisely as possible, plus charts, diagrams and lists. Skip space between points so you can add detail later. Don't organize or highlight yet; capture as much as possible ("Don't discriminate").
3. **Cues.** Afterwards, for each section or concept, filter and analyze the Notes and write the important parts on the left, in organized form: "Turn five sentences of normal notes into one or two sentences with a main point and supporting facts." The page can now be skimmed.
4. **Summary.** At the bottom, reduce everything to a few top-level ideas with only the important supporting facts or exceptions, saying as much as possible in as few words as possible.

Afterwards you skim the Summary and Cues and move on. The page is a self-made study guide that documents the whole process from raw notes to synthesis, so you can go as deep as you need, and its phrasing carries personal meaning: "You are making the information fit your mental scheme, not the other way around." Someone else's notes don't work, because you first have to decode their sense of structure. The method takes the student through four stages of learning: taking notes, editing, analysis, reflection.

Effort is what makes notes work. Peter Brown (*Make It Stick*) cites a study in which students copied some material word for word and paraphrased other material, and they recalled the paraphrased material far better. Handing students ready-made lecture notes handicaps them: "the less effort and involvement a student is able to use to get by, the worse the learning will be."

## Purposeful annotation

Reading is an active back-and-forth between the page and comprehension, and it always sits inside a bigger picture. If texts are central to what you teach, you have to teach a way of reading for your purposes. An effective reader reads with **purpose**: they know why they are reading and what they will do with the information afterwards. Highlighting with no idea why ends in "wait, what did I just read?"

**Before, during, after.** Before reading, set a goal: ask "Why am I reading this?" and tell the student what task follows (a summary, an analysis, a comprehension question). During reading, make meaningful notes and annotations. After reading, do a task that cements and applies the reading. Example: give an article and pique curiosity by saying the author was heavily criticized for the argument, and ask them to guess why. During reading, have them tease out the argument's logical structure and their own response to it. Afterwards, have them summarize and revisit the opening question, then give them an article refuting the first.

**Reading as a conversation with the text.** When confused in conversation we ask the speaker to repeat, rephrase or clarify. Teach the student to respond to a text the same way, with summaries, connections, analyses and arguments.

**Set the stage with your aim:**

- general absorption → a summary or simple outline;
- meaningful engagement → closer analysis of the content;
- understanding rather than memorizing → comprehension questions or paraphrase.

Give one or two clearly defined verbs: analyze, compare, organize, integrate, describe, summarize, explain, develop.

Once the student knows the purpose, let them choose how to annotate: margin questions, highlighting, circling new words and defining them, underlining direct quotes. No method is better than another so long as it logically supports the purpose. Skip annotation rules, color-coded pens and special symbols; a system the student devises alone is likely to work better. Annotations should reflect real engagement, a question or rebuttal that genuinely occurred to them, not a note made because a note seemed expected. Done this way, annotations become breadcrumbs into and out of concepts. Sometimes the only prompt needed is to have the student write their goal in big letters at the top of the page. Finally, ask them to evaluate their system: how has it helped? Has it helped?
