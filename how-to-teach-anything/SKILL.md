# How to Teach Anything

Peter Hollins's method for teaching anyone anything, formally in a classroom or simply when helping a friend: a child's homework, new staff on a machine, a souffle, a piece of software, a university course. Teaching is "in essence a kind of conversation" in which new information is conveyed to a person who doesn't possess it, and the teacher's role is always that of a **facilitator** who sets up the conditions for the natural processes of learning. The techniques are the same from the teacher's side and the student's, so every one of them also serves someone teaching themselves: run the steps below with yourself as the student. Mastering the teacher's role makes you a better learner.

The governing rule comes from the reflective approach: "teaching is practical—it's about what works. There are no topics too difficult or students too stupid, only methods that are unsuitable." Every technique here is a tool to try, appraise and adapt for this student, this topic, this moment. "If it doesn't work, just drop it, or adapt it until it does!"

## The five pedagogical approaches

Everything else in the book refers back to one or more of these. They blend freely.

- **Constructivist approach**: start from what the student already understands and build new knowledge up from it, smaller and simpler concepts first (an instrument: scales, reading music and basic handling before combinations). The teacher lays out "a useful obstacle course" the student learns by moving through. With two or more students, build on what they both know; analogies let a student "construct" the new concept from an old one. Drawback: it can be unstructured, and some students can't make the connections and would rather be told exactly how to think.
- **Integrative approach**: embed new knowledge in a practical, applied way, so dry, abstract knowledge comes "alive in context" (language students role-play ordering food in a restaurant). Students retain what they see the point of; trigonometry is forgotten because it is never applied. Some knowledge suits it more than other knowledge, but the best teachers make even "boring" subjects relevant.
- **Collaborative approach**: share the process of learning through teamwork. Explaining, negotiating, clarifying and arguing bring a topic into sharper focus; students with different strengths support each other; "a kind of self-correction happens in groups." The teacher joins the group or acts as a detached facilitator arranging its conditions. A manager has slightly more experienced staff train new hires on a machine: the trainers reinforce their knowledge and still remember not knowing.
- **Inquiry-based approach**: put questions at the heart of learning, working with "the question, the answer, and the part in between." Four levels, by how much the teacher gives:
  - *confirmation inquiry*: question, method and answer all given, confirming how it's done;
  - *structured inquiry*: question and method given, student finds the answer;
  - *guided inquiry*: question only, student devises the method and the solution;
  - *open inquiry*: nothing given, student devises question, method and answer (the basis of the Montessori method).
  Prompts: "If we solved the old problem in such-and-such a way, how should we solve this new problem?" Or hand a friend three books and ask for the question that best taps all three. It fosters curiosity, but it is hard to prepare, falls flat if students can't answer, and at worst embarrasses students and lowers their confidence, especially those with learning disabilities or who aren't quick thinkers.
- **Reflective approach**: regularly stop, appraise the techniques in use, and adjust. Ask: is what you're doing actually working for this student, and this topic, in this moment? Why or why not? What would work better? It promotes experimentation and costs the teacher the effort of devising new strategies.

## Work within the brain's limits

"The brain isn't a machine": it has natural limits on taking in new information and on focus, and learning goes well when teaching works within them. Volume and intensity must match the brain's capacity, "otherwise your teaching is all for nothing."

**Cognitive load theory** (John Sweller): the brain "can mostly only do one new thing at a time," so prioritize what that thing is. New material is handled in working memory and, once assimilated, stored in long-term memory as **mental schemas**. Learning something new costs far more energy than retrieving something already "banked"; that cost is cognitive load. Working memory holds a maximum of **two or three pieces of new information** at a moment and holds focus for **around twenty seconds**. Moving something into long-term memory is what it means to have learnt it.

Two ways to lighten the load:

- **Alter the content**: chunks, simplification, summaries. A huge pile of stones and a small wheelbarrow: one small load at a time.
- **Sequence it**: break the material down temporally, the beginning first, then the next step, building as you go. A biology teacher starts from one tiny element of an ecosystem and keeps zooming out. A whole is manageable told as a sequence or story with a beginning and an end.

**Overload** looks like a learner working on two levels at once: a chemistry reader meets "optical chirality" and "enantiomers," looks them up, and finds still more unknown terms behind them, using concepts while still trying to understand them. Too much sits in working memory and too little in long-term memory, so no schemas get built. The teacher's fix is an order: "start with this concept first, then move on to this material, and then put it all together with this final piece."

The **information processing model** traces the path: sensing and perceiving (is this worth attention?) → short-term or working memory, which holds it a few seconds and loses it unless it is committed → encoding into a long-term schema with cues for retrieval → retrieval, often triggered by a specific environment. Teach to each stage. Engage the senses, sight and hearing above all (show the product, pass it round, explain it in words and in infographics), since different people learn through different senses. Make the opening interesting, because attention governs the move into short-term memory, along with the amount of information and individual ability. Then use repetition for long-term memory: say the same thing in novel ways, keep it focused, break it into small parts, and tie it to a real-life purpose.

Check the load: does the student have enough stored knowledge to draw on? Are you presenting too many things at once? Is the pacing overwhelming them or gently challenging them? Then:

- Take your time. Be explicit and detailed, give plenty of examples, and anchor each new concept to one the student already has.
- Take a short break every ten or fifteen minutes, or switch activities. Keep the student active with questions and prompts, and show them what matters most.
- Build an hour's lesson as four fifteen-minute chunks, each closed by a simple mind map summarizing its points. Spend lots of time drilling and reviewing.
- Show the student how you are organizing the concepts and how they fit together. "The brain loves connections."

Where scholars disagree: providing partial solutions often works better than handing over ready-made explanations, and audio-visual material lowers load when used sparingly but raises it when overdone.

## Seeing the landscape

The teacher's **advantage of perspective** is that students see only small, unclear sections of the whole while the teacher sees all of it and how it connects; without that advantage it would be "two students blundering around together." Seeing the full **landscape** lets you prioritize, frame tasks and set goals, and it is "the single most significant thing you do as a teacher." See it incompletely or wrongly and you hand the student a faulty vision: you get lost, can't anticipate problems or opportunities, lose their trust, and may teach them something wrong.

Expertise is not enough. A teacher's main skill is conveying knowledge, which includes knowing how to selectively ignore what isn't relevant so the student can focus on what is. The teacher builds a **map**: "a truncated, simplified vision of the landscape," deliberately organized and holding only what this student needs at their current stage. It may omit detail, but it must stay accurate; the student should never find out later that whole sections were wrong. Plot the course before setting off, and know the destination even when the student doesn't.

Teaching exposes the teacher's own map: "Nothing will reveal gaps in your own knowledge like trying to teach someone else!" If you can't explain a topic to a non-expert or a ten-year-old, you don't really understand it. Run the Feynman technique on yourself before you teach.

A map, or **mental model**, is a series of connections: in time (new learning linked to past learning) and across the pieces held now.

**Connecting old knowledge to new.** You never start "from scratch." Building on prior knowledge does two jobs: the student understands and retains the new material, and you find out where they are and where the gaps lie. A map is useless until you know where you stand on it, so ask: What do they already know and understand? How do they envision their path and their goal? What do they consider their most important need, and why? Always start with "what do we already know?"

Sketch a mind map of the topic with the student: the topic in the center, branches for its aspects, links between connected ones. Include what is already learnt as well as what is still to learn, and have the student rate which parts are hardest, easier, and well understood. Trace each difficulty back to the missing prerequisite. In the book's example, a younger brother struggling with magnetism turns out to have shaky electrical charge, and behind that, atomic structure (how an atom's electrons relate to its overall charge). The route is then atomic structure → electrical charge → magnetism issues one by one, from those he partly understands to the hardest. The map also shows both of you that the material is finite and where each piece fits. Students give up when "they don't know what they don't know" and can't connect an exercise to the bigger picture.

Before teaching, run the map checklist:

- Have you broken the new information into genuinely manageable small chunks, or could you separate it further?
- Have you shown the connections and relationships between the chunks, such as their hierarchy?
- Are you being as clear and simple as possible?
- Does the map go somewhere specific, with a fixed endpoint?
- Can you see how each subunit relates to that endpoint?
- Are there gaps: have you assumed anything about what the student already knows?
- Have you included what the student already knows, and linked it to what they don't yet understand?

## Scaffolding

**Scaffolding** is the "process of ramping up mastery from lower levels of capacity": building a complex mental structure out of smaller, simpler units, banking each in long-term memory before adding the next. Its goal is to simplify. The moves, shown on teaching a piece of software:

- **provision of knowledge**: explanations, instructions, what it's for, the basic principles;
- **demonstration of strategies**: run operations while they watch;
- **modeling**: build a model of what you've shared, or show how the strategies fit together;
- **questioning**: have them guess how to do a related operation, and move to a more complex strategy only once they thoroughly understand the first;
- **instructing** as you go: "you click here to do XYZ";
- **feedback and correction** throughout: ask, infer their level from the answer, correct gently, and backtrack to simpler material to check understanding;
- **restructuring the task**: set mini-tasks first, deliberately leave some aspects out to make a point clearer, or show how not to do it (use the program wrongly and let them watch it crash).

Scaffolding can be subtle and spontaneous, like reminding a child to slow down and sound out a hard word. Start from the simplest chunks, then draw attention to the connections between them with questions, prompts and clues. The best lesson plan is one where students are eager to move on to the next step themselves.

**"I do, we do, you do"** (also "show me, help me, let me") is scaffolding as a gradual handover from teacher-centered to student-centered:

- **Teacher-led instruction, "I do"**: the student watches while you work and explain every new concept, skill and piece of information ("I'm using a metal bowl, see?" and why oil residue ruins the egg whites). Orient them to the purpose ("watch closely so you can do the same"), set limitations and goals ("today we are making a perfect souffle"), and draw on previous knowledge, explanations and examples.
- **Teacher and student cooperation, "we do"**: "training wheels." They do the work while you still do part of it, prompting and correcting; your instruction now guides their action. Go one step at a time and lead to each next step with questions ("Do you remember where in the oven it goes and why?"). Have them show their understanding in a limited way first, encourage them, and use mistakes to stop, adjust and reinforce.
- **Student-led practice, "you do"**: the training wheels come off. They perform alone and compare the result against the goals you set together at the start. If the souffle comes out like "a deflated hockey puck," go back and build better scaffolding.

## Running a teaching job

1. **Check your own map.** Explain the topic in plain English in two sentences a five-year-old could follow (the Feynman technique). Done when you can, and you can name the endpoint the student is heading for.
2. **Locate the student.** Ask the three locating questions, sketch what they know and don't, and trace each difficulty back to its missing prerequisite. Done when you can draw a path of small steps from what they know to the endpoint.
3. **Draw the map.** Chunk the material, connect the chunks, and order them within the cognitive load limits. Done when every map-checklist question gets a yes.
4. **Set up motivation.** Make clear what the goals are, why you're aiming for them, and how you'll both measure success. Connect the material to what the student values and pitch the challenge at the sweet spot. → [motivation-and-environment.md](motivation-and-environment.md)
5. **Choose the approach and deliver.** Pick and blend from the five approaches to fit the student and topic, then scaffold through "I do, we do, you do." For explaining, use concept maps, analogies, the Feynman technique and problematizing what students already believe → [mapping-and-explaining.md](mapping-and-explaining.md). To drive understanding deeper, use problem-based learning, Socratic questions and critical thinking → [questioning-and-problem-based-learning.md](questioning-and-problem-based-learning.md). For interactive practice that makes their thinking visible (think-pair-share, mini quizzes, student teachers, debate, flowcharts, fishbone and Venn diagrams, KWHL) → [activities.md](activities.md).
6. **Consolidate.** Revisit the material often, by active recall, spaced over as long a period as possible. When the material is text or the student must study on their own, teach SQ3R, Cornell notes and purposeful annotation, and pace challenge up Bloom's taxonomy → [study-methods.md](study-methods.md).
7. **Give feedback throughout,** small and often, tied to the goal → [feedback.md](feedback.md). When the student struggles, fails or fears failing, apply productive failure, academic buoyancy and a judgment-free atmosphere → [motivation-and-environment.md](motivation-and-environment.md).
8. **Reflect.** Close each session with a mini quiz (the main thing learnt, one thing still difficult, any questions) and ask whether the method is working for this student, this topic, this moment. Done when the next session's plan has been adjusted to the answers.
