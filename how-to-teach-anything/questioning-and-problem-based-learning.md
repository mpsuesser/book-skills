# Questioning and problem-based learning

Depth techniques: problem-based learning, the Socratic method with R.W. Paul's six question types, and critical thinking. All three make the student pull understanding out for themselves instead of receiving it.

## Problem-based learning (PBL)

**Problem-based learning** starts with a problem to solve and forces learning through the process of solving it. "Instead of setting out to learn X, the idea is to set a goal of solving problem Y, and in the process, learning X": pure learning transfer, and an integrative approach. Novice metalworkers carve a complex structure from a solid block with hand tools and come out experts with hand tools; Mr. Miyagi's hard labor turns out to have taught Daniel-san karate. The traditional linear approach (material given, memorized, then shown solving a problem) runs the other way.

Hollins learnt Spanish to keep up with the questions of a classmate he wanted to impress. His flashcards grew from one word on the back to three or four Spanish sentences, and he earned an A+, though he "never did get anywhere with Jessica." He set out to solve X (Jessica) and learnt Y (Spanish).

PBL goes back to John Dewey's *Democracy and Education* (1916) and its premise of learning by doing. Its modern start was in 1960s medical schools, which trained doctors on real patient cases. Faced with a sixty-six-year-old man complaining of recent shortness of breath, the student works out what to ask (medical, family and social histories; how long, at what time of day, after what activities, what makes it better or worse), does a problem-focused exam (blood pressure, heart and lungs, legs checked for edema), decides on lab tests or X-rays, and plans treatment from the results. Research on problem-based medical training finds improved clinical reasoning and problem-solving, more in-depth learning, and better integration of concepts.

PBL makes students take ownership of the approach and the solution: "Instead of simply solving for X, they must come up with the entire equation that leads to X." A real issue with real consequences also raises self-motivation. Choose the problem deliberately, so that solving it teaches what you actually want learnt: to master a new guitar scale, attempt a difficult song built on it.

**The PBL steps:**

1. Define your problem.
2. Determine what you already know.
3. List potential solutions and choose the one most likely to succeed.
4. Break the steps into action items (a timeline often helps).
5. Identify what you still need to know and how you will get that information.

*Meal planning* (solve X, stressful last-minute dinners; learn Y, better cooking). You already know the family must eat, that recipes could start easy and grow more involved, and that you'll need ingredients, a schedule and a strategy for harder techniques. You still need actual recipes and ingredient lists, a calendar, and the specific skills you want to learn. You get them by asking family members for their three favorite meals and from Pinterest, a grocery list or app, a meal-planning calendar or printable, online grocery ordering, and reading, YouTube or a class for techniques. The result is a strategy that repeats week after week while the cooking keeps improving.

*The broken toaster* (solve X, a dead toaster; learn Y, basic electronics). You already know it doesn't work, you're handy, you know a little about wiring, and the model is no longer made. You still need the specific cause, instruction beyond your current skills, tools, supplies, time and a place to work. You find these by disassembling it, consulting a small-appliance "fix-it" manual, and watching YouTube tutorials.

PBL feels like it has too many steps, but it saves time and often money in the long run, since you aren't randomly trying one poorly thought-out solution after another. Retention is better and understanding of the problem deeper. You may have to get creative to design a problem around what you want to learn.

## The Socratic method

"The importance of being a question master cannot be overstated." Information doesn't teach itself, and rereading a passage that isn't landing is the rat pressing the shock lever again: hard work, no progress. Pull understanding out yourself; as a teacher, set up the conditions and prompts that make the student do more than go through the motions.

*Jimbo and Kunal* read the same book on Spanish history. Jimbo reviews and takes notes, and his answers read "like bullet points for a recipe for cornbread": B+. Kunal reads it once or twice and spends the rest of his time probing the whys and motivations of the conquistadors and kings. His answers are essays, and despite forgetting a couple of minor details he makes insightful leaps: A+. Asking the right questions, he can predict what the conquistadors would probably have done without knowing every fact.

Answers matter far less than the questions asked. A question turns a flat fact into "a living, three-dimensional piece of knowledge" with a background and context, and asking one means identifying what you don't know and staying open to the possibility that your whole understanding is wrong. Questions **triangulate understanding**: accept a textbook wholesale and you follow a single path, but question it and the twists, the inaccuracies and the multiple perspectives appear. Questions clarify misunderstandings and reinforce what is already known.

The **Socratic Method** is asking "questions upon questions in an effort to dissect an assertion or statement for greater understanding," uncovering its underlying assumptions and motivations, to enrich both parties. American law schools use it, with students defending a statement against the professor's questions about a case. It is not adversarial by nature, though it may cause defensiveness. Turned on yourself it is a stress test that strips away assumptions: what survives merciless questioning is "deeply comprehended and validated," and every error or gap gets found, corrected and "proofed with a rebuttal."

Say the sky is blue "because it reflects the ocean" (which is wrong), and one follow-up (how do you know it is a reflection of the ocean?) exposes that you have no idea. Innocent, simple questions, honestly answered, unravel what you thought you knew and map your blind spots.

**Running it on a student:** ask so the student has room to express their opinion. Once they've revealed what they think, probe further by asking for clarification, having them elaborate on something they glossed over, or problematizing something they said. The exposed gaps are what they learn from.

### R.W. Paul's six types of Socratic questions

1. **Clarification questions**: why exactly does it matter? Look for the real meaning of what is said, any hidden motivation or significance, and what the speaker hopes to achieve. *What exactly do you mean by that? What is the main issue here? What does it matter to you? What does that have to do with the rest of the discussion? Why would you say that?*
2. **Probing assumptions**: what hidden assumptions might exist? Separate opinion and belief from evidence-based fact; outside a scientific paper there are always inherent assumptions. *Is your blue my blue? Why do you think that? How can you prove or verify that? Where is this coming from exactly? What leads you to believe it?*
3. **Probing rationale, reasons, evidence**: what proven evidence exists? Is the evidence trustworthy and valid, what conclusions are drawn from it and how, and what is missing or glazed over? *What's the evidence, and why is it convincing? How exactly does that work? What is an example of that? What if the information was incorrect or flawed? Can you tell me the reasoning?*
4. **Questioning viewpoints and perspectives**: what other perspectives exist? Assertions nearly always come from a bias, so play devil's advocate and ask why the opposing views aren't preferred and why they don't work. *How else could your evidence be interpreted? Why is that research the best proof? Could the same be said about the opposite claim? What are the potential flaws in this argument? What is the counterargument?* (For the sky: why doesn't the sky color the ocean instead?)
5. **Probing implications and consequences**: what does this mean, what is the significance, and how does it connect to other information? *What does this finding imply? What else does it determine? Who is affected? What are the consequences? How does it connect to the broader topic or narrative?*
6. **Questions about the question**: why is this question important? It is weaker when self-directed. Aimed at someone else, it makes them consider why you asked and what you wanted to evoke. *Why do you think I asked you about this? What do you think I wanted to do when I asked? How might this knowledge help you in other topics? How does this apply to everyday life and to what we discussed earlier?*

The questions can sound like a broken record, but properly answered each goes in a different direction. The book's blue-sky example runs to more than twenty separate probes of one simple assertion, enough to show someone they can only regurgitate a few facts without context. Used on yourself it is a systematic double-check that always ends in a win: you either confirm your mastery or find exactly what is missing.

The same six correct a mistake. A friend claims the Spanish Inquisition was a fairly humane process of light interrogation (sources put the death toll at around one hundred thousand on average). Ask: What exactly is being said, and why does it matter? What is that statement based on? What makes you think it's true, and where is the evidence? Who might hold this perspective, and why, and what is the opposing one? What does it mean for Spanish history as a whole, and are all the textbooks wrong? Why do you think I'm asking? The questions don't change for deepening understanding of any topic, the biology of the brain included.

## Critical thinking

Following the Socratic method is practicing **critical thinking**, which is the inquiry-based approach. By questioning the student you model the internal process they'll run alone long after you stop teaching: "A critical thinker has an internalized teacher in their brain," asking "So what are we doing here? What's this? What does that really mean anyway?" Critical thinking is the *how* of learning rather than the *what*, something we do rather than something we are, and practiced often it makes us experts at learning. Its first step is honesty about the quality of one's own thinking, and its core is taking nothing for granted. Asking a question puts you in the position of not knowing, which opens the way to insight: the attitude behind the scientific method, fed by sincere curiosity.

**Teaching it:**

- Keep yourself out of the picture. Give the student a question rather than a pre-formed opinion, and let them reach their own conclusions. Instruction and explanation have their place, but support the student's process of working out what they think rather than telling them what to think.
- Invite them to challenge any and all assumptions, including those that frame your learning relationship. The aim is not to cram their head with what you believe they need but "conscious, mature open-mindedness."
- This is not letting any poorly considered opinion stand: "every thought, opinion or claim needs to earn its keep." Challenge bias and assumption, keep a neutral perspective, and suspend judgment until there is good reason to prefer one claim.
- Model it. Keep your own ego-based bias in check and revise your beliefs openly when new evidence arrives. Teachers used to unchallenged authority can be stubborn, and engaging your own blind spots earns trust. "Truly intelligent people never fear mistakes, ignorance or being proved wrong."
- Never shut down an earnest inquiry, and always welcome debate. Support students when they spot a fault in their own logic, but don't praise one idea over another. The goal is a sound path, not the "right answer," and you needn't pick a side. "I don't know," or "I don't know yet," is always available, and admitting a messy, unresolved complexity is more mature than forcing an oversimplified conclusion to satisfy the ego.
- Teach tolerance of nuance: holding an idea without forcing a decision or rushing to attach opinions and emotions to it.

This is the **growth mindset**: it takes fear, bias and ego out of the picture and puts data and knowledge at the center by separating facts and logic from personal belief. The sophisticated thinker can say, "Well, for personal reasons I would love it if XYZ were true, but I can't deny the evidence to the contrary, so I guess I must have been wrong on this count." Ideas are not a favorite football team, backed out of loyalty; dropping one is just accumulating more data. Critical thinking is a hallmark of metacognition, standing outside your own mental models and asking neutrally whether they work and whether something better exists. Teaching it takes a teacher who practices it while teaching.
