# Matchmakers

David S. Evans and Richard Schmalensee's account of the **new economics of multisided platforms**, built on Rochet and Tirole's 2000 discovery that payment cards, telecom networks, operating systems and many other businesses share one business model. Matchmakers run under different economic rules from traditional firms, and the textbook formulas for traditional firms give wrong answers for them. The book argues through cases, and this skill keeps each case with the principle it shows.

## The core economics

- A **multisided platform** (also called a **matchmaker**) operates a physical or virtual place that helps two or more different groups find each other and interact. Each group is a **side**. Its raw materials are the customer groups it brings together. Part of what it sells each group is access to the other groups. Its most important inputs are its customers.
- **Figure 1-1, a simple two-sided platform.** Customer A's and Customer B's join the platform and become its members. At any moment, some members of each side are on the platform looking for matches, and a few of those find a partner for a mutually advantageous exchange. Members don't use the platform all the time. For OpenTable the A's are restaurants, the B's are diners, and the platform is the software and databases that match them. For a nightclub the platform is the dance floor and bar, and the space, music, lighting and cocktails do the matching.
- A **single-sided** business buys inputs, perhaps transforms them, and sells to customers. Whole Foods buys granola and resells it, and the shopper never deals with the granola supplier. OpenTable is **two-sided**: it brings diners and restaurants together to interact directly and takes no part in the deals between them.
- **Interdependent demand** is what sets matchmakers apart. Each group's demand depends on the other groups' demand. Merchants want the American Express card only if consumers carry it, and consumers carry it only if merchants take it. Traditional economics ignores this, which is why it calls pricing below cost irrational. For a matchmaker it is often the profit-maximizing choice.
- **Indirect network effect**: the value of the platform to one group depends on how many members of a *different* group take part, for example more restaurants on OpenTable for diners. Usually positive, but it can be negative: more ads lower the value of radio to listeners. **Direct network effect**: the value to a group depends on how many members of the *same* group take part. Positive for telephone users who can reach more people. Negative where same-side members compete or crowd each other, as men do at a singles bar. Platforms then have reason to limit participation.
- **Same people, different roles.** Even when every user looks alike, they play distinct roles: YouTube uploaders and viewers, telephone callers and receivers, M-PESA senders and receivers. Court each role as its own side.
- **It isn't just a numbers game.** The platform has to make the market **thick**: enough participants on each side whom the other side *wants* to interact with. The right restaurants in the right city at the right time count. Many irrelevant ones do not.
- **The one-sided error.** The pre-2000 network-effects theory had a single customer type, and it produced the "grab all the eyeballs you can and figure out how to monetize them later" advice, along with first-mover and winner-take-all dogma. Both are shaky at best. First movers in matchmaker industries mostly died, and Visa expanded nationally sixteen years after the first national card network. The history is in [ignition.md](ignition.md). Platforms can differentiate (Facebook against MySpace) and win while smaller or later. Participants **multihome**, using several platforms at once (most people carry several payment cards), which let Discover enter in the mid-1980s. They **single-home** where they use only one (most people's desktop operating system). Switching is often easy, so network effects reverse: Friendster lost to MySpace, which lost to Facebook.
- **Spotting a matchmaker.** Ask whether the business creates value by connecting two or more types of customers. A strong clue is that it charges some important group nothing (mall shoppers) or gives them a deal almost too good to be true (cheap, thick, glossy magazines).

## The six critical issues

Every matchmaker has to handle these six. Each line states the authors' rule and names the file with the procedures, tests and cases behind it.

1. **Friction.** The opportunity arises when **frictions** (economists' **transaction costs**) keep willing parties from dealing with each other easily and directly. Reducing a substantial friction is "a necessary condition, but not a sufficient condition, for a multisided platform to succeed." The **value pie** has to be big enough to give every side a slice that keeps it on board, fund any subsidies, and leave the platform a good return. → Cases: [cases.md](cases.md) (OpenTable, Alibaba and the US B2B mass extinction, Ariba, M-PESA).
2. **Ignition.** A platform must reach **critical mass** in order to **ignite**, by solving the **chicken-and-egg problem** (also called the **coordination problem**). Otherwise it **implodes**. → [ignition.md](ignition.md): critical mass frontier, zigzag, two-step and commitment strategies, self-supply, marquee customers, "make 'em believe", go narrow/go deep, YouTube, PayPal, Brightcove.
3. **Pricing.** The **pricing structure** has to balance the interdependent demands of all sides, both at launch and for long-run profit. Many platforms run a permanent **subsidy side** and a **money side**. → [pricing.md](pricing.md): price level against structure, the four questions, access and usage fees, the new rule book, Table 2-1, fleet cards, newspaper rebalancing.
4. **Ecosystem.** Platforms sit in wider **ecosystems** of firms, governments, regulation and institutions. They have to play well with these, sometimes change them, and decide how many sides to have and whether to be a matchmaker at all. → [ecosystem.md](ecosystem.md): single-sided, multisided or a mix, vertically integrated and reseller platforms, when to open each side, Symbian, iOS against Android, foundational platforms.
5. **Design.** The platform must **design** its place, whether built from bricks or code, to raise the value of the direct and indirect network effects among participants. → [design.md](design.md): thick markets, large against small, screening and signaling devices, congestion, standards, balancing externalities, search diversion, Aventura Mall.
6. **Governance.** Matchmakers adopt **governance systems** with "laws," "enforcers" and "penalties" that promote positive externalities and deter **behavioral externalities**. → [governance.md](governance.md): the catalogue of bad behavior, gatekeeping, ratings, ejection, the London Stock Exchange, Friendster, MySpace and Facebook.

All six are harder for a **pioneering platform**, which has no model to copy, than for an entrant into a mature matchmaker industry such as malls or magazines, where pricing and design largely follow the competition. Comfortable incumbents land in uncharted waters when a new matchmaker appears. → [disruption.md](disruption.md): turbocharging, creative destruction, death spirals, the retail case, how incumbents survive, and why change runs "slower and faster than you think."

## Vetting a new matchmaker: the six questions

The authors put these to every start-up matchmaker, pioneers above all, and urge founders, investors, employees, partners and would-be participants to ask them too:

1. **Friction:** what is it, how big is it, and who benefits when it is solved?
2. **Design:** does the platform's design reduce that friction and balance the interests of every side, better than other entrants do?
3. **Ignition:** how hard is the ignition problem, and is there a solid plan for reaching critical mass?
4. **Pricing:** can the platform make money at the prices that ignition and growth require?
5. **Ecosystem:** how will it work with others in the broader ecosystem, what risks come with that, and have they been dealt with?
6. **Adaptation:** is the entrepreneur ready to change the design and the ignition strategy quickly as the market reacts?

The biggest mistake platform entrepreneurs make is the "if you build it, they will come" fallacy. Without a significant friction to address, however good the technology, there is no case for investing. → [evaluation.md](evaluation.md): what lies behind each question, the post-launch warning signs and timeline, and the Apple Pay diagnosis worked through with its numbers.

## How to apply it

1. **Map the sides.** List each customer group, or each role one group plays, and what each wants from the others. Mark which way the indirect network effects run between them and whether any direct effects are negative. Done when you can say who needs whom, why, and how much.
2. **Name the issues in play** among the six, and whether the platform is a pioneer or an entrant into a mature industry. Load the file for each issue involved. A new venture, an investment or a launch plan runs through the six questions in [evaluation.md](evaluation.md).
3. **Answer in the authors' terms and at their confidence.** Use their vocabulary (money side, critical mass frontier, zigzag, screening device) and give their rules, tests and case numbers rather than general advice. Where a term needs its exact definition, [glossary.md](glossary.md) carries the book's.
