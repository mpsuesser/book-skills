# Pricing: balancing interdependent demands

## Why matchmaker pricing differs

- **Single-sided pricing** turns on the trade-off between volume and margin. To find the "Goldilocks" price for its 2016 3 series (base price $33,150 in the US), BMW needs only two answers: what it costs to make and ship a car, and how sensitive demand is to price. Price-sensitive demand calls for a low markup and insensitive demand for a high one, and small experiments up and down find the best point. A multiproduct firm also weighs how each price affects its other sales: Mercedes-Benz keeps entry-level prices relatively high so they don't divert buyers from its larger, more profitable cars. A traditional firm almost never prices at or below incremental cost, since selling more would then mean earning less. The exceptions have limits: loss leaders carry explicit or implicit caps per customer (milk can't be stored for long), and introductory prices rise later.
- **A matchmaker must *balance the interests of all sides*** so that every side gets on board, stays on board, and interacts. Raise the price to one group and fewer of that group join. The platform is then worth less to the other group, some of whom drop off, which lowers demand from the first group again, and so on. The right math accounts for these feedback effects and sometimes gives counterintuitive prescriptions. Both prices affect participation on both sides, so set them together.
- **The *Financial Times*.** Raise the reader price and readership falls, so advertisers pay less. Raise the ad price and the paper sells fewer ads, which readers may like, but it also earns less to spend on the content readers want.
- **The four questions** a two-sided platform has to answer where BMW answers one (illustrated with fleet cards):
  1. How price-sensitive is side A (truck fleets)?
  2. How price-sensitive is side B (truck stop chains)?
  3. How sensitive is A's demand to B's participation?
  4. How sensitive is B's demand to A's participation?

  No simple market research or experiment reliably finds the best pair of prices. "There is no substitute for a deep understanding of the firm's customers and for having good judgment."

## Money side and subsidy side

- With interdependent demand in the model, the profit-maximizing price to one side can be below incremental cost, zero, or negative (the platform pays the participant).
- **Subsidy side**: a group charged less than cost, nothing, or a negative price. There can be more than one subsidized group as long as one group works as the money side. **Money side**: the group or groups charged well above cost, providing all or virtually all of the profit. For OpenTable, diners are the subsidy side and restaurants the money side. For search engines, organic search is the subsidy side and search advertising the money side. For credit cards, cardholders are the subsidy side and merchants the money side. Airbnb earns most of its money from a service fee on guests and charges hosts a modest commission that just covers payment processing. *Vogue* sells a thick, glossy magazine for perhaps less than it costs to print and distribute, and makes its money on ads. Google has never charged anyone to search and was worth $527 billion on November 20, 2015, almost entirely from advertising.
- **The newcomer's mistake** is to treat free and negative prices as a gimmick for reaching critical mass. Matchmakers commonly keep them *permanently* because that maximizes profit. OpenTable has never charged diners a penny. "Free" is not a new strategy: malls, newspapers, radio, broadcast TV, yellow pages and credit cards have used it profitably for a long time. It is more common online because the cost of serving one more participant there is close to zero.
- **A subsidy side is possible, not required.** The theory says only that one *may* be profitable. The *Wall Street Journal* charges online readers (who cost almost nothing to serve) as well as advertisers. US nightclubs usually charge both men and women, and online matchmakers generally charge both sexes the same.
- When a platform considers charging its subsidy side, it weighs the revenue gained from that side against the revenue lost from the money side as participation falls. YouTube ran this arithmetic and kept viewing and uploading free.

## Price level and price structure

- **Price level**: how much the platform charges in total, for example the total fee both sides pay per transaction. **Price structure**: how much of that comes from each side relative to the other. What really sets two-sided platforms apart is the importance of these *relative* prices.
- **Figure 6-1.** The vertical axis is P^S, the price to truck stops, and the horizontal axis is P^F, the price to fleets, with P^T = P^S + P^F. Each downward-sloping line holds total price constant, so the farther out the line, the higher the level. Points along a line are different structures. Point 1, on the outer line, has a low P^S and a high P^F. Point 2, on an inner line, has a high P^S and a low P^F. Moving from 1 to 2 lowers the total price and the fleet price, yet truck stops will complain that prices went up.
- **Flat or tilted.** A flat structure treats the sides alike. A **tilted** structure, where one side seems to get the better deal, is what most matchmakers use, though the theory does not say it is always optimal. A tilt can go as far as one side paying 100% of the total, or more than 100% when the other side is paid: if one side gets a $0.50 subsidy and the other pays $2.50, the second side pays $2.50 of a $2.00 total. Restaurants pay more than 100% of OpenTable's fees, because diners pay nothing and earn rewards.
- In practice, choose the structure on each side's *profit margin* given the cost of serving it, not on revenue alone. Sometimes the platform makes more profit by deciding to lose money on one side.

## Access fees and usage fees

Platforms are places that participants have to get into before they can interact, so a platform that can observe both access and usage sets an **access fee** (to join) and a **usage fee** (per interaction) for each side. Together these balance the sides, encourage interaction, and make money.

| Platform | Side | Access fee | Usage fee |
|---|---|---|---|
| OpenTable | Restaurants | $199 a month for the reservation system | $1.00 per seated diner |
| OpenTable | Diners | None | Negative (reward points) |
| American Express | Cardholders | Annual fee | Negative (no transaction fee, plus rewards) |
| American Express | Merchants | None | About 3% of each transaction |
| Chestnut Hill Mall | Shoppers | None (free parking and entry) | None |
| Chestnut Hill Mall | Stores | Monthly rent | Often a percentage of sales |
| YouTube | Uploaders, viewers | None | None |
| YouTube | Advertisers | None | Per view of their ads |

At American Express, "Cardholders value and pay for the option to use the card, while merchants value and pay for card usage in their stores."

## The new rule book

Everything a single-sided firm considers still applies. A matchmaker also has to answer these questions:

1. **How sensitive is each group to price?** For a one-sided business this is the whole question. For a matchmaker it is where the analysis starts. A platform can usually charge more to the less price-sensitive group without losing many members the other side values. Consumers are highly sensitive to prices for web content and avoid sites with even small charges. Advertisers are less sensitive to pay-for-performance prices because a click that leads to a sale earns them a good margin.
2. **Who needs whom, why, and how much?** This decides the structure and whom to subsidize. If the "desperate to meet" group places a very high value on reaching the "be nice to meet" group, the platform may profit by *paying* the "be nice to meet" group to join, so that the "desperate to meet" group will pay a high price for access. That is the simple, if perhaps sexist, explanation for free entry or drinks for women at nightclubs. At the Rosebar nightclub in Buenos Aires, men pay a very high access charge for the VIP room with free drinks, and only very attractive women are admitted, usually invited by the men and paying nothing.
3. **Does one group control whether an interaction takes place?** If so, the platform gets no usage until that group starts an interaction, so it gives that group incentives, possibly subsidies. That is why American Express and OpenTable subsidize usage, and why WEX Fleet One charges truck stops more than fleets. Searchers choose the search engine, and the advertiser can reach them only through the engine they choose, so engines don't charge searchers. Bing even gave "frequent flier" rewards for staying signed in. Engines also share revenue with websites and handset makers to be the default choice.

Applying the answers:

- A platform often charges no access fee to a group whose participation creates positive indirect network effects (merchants at American Express). Access fees often pair with zero or negative usage fees (American Express cardholders): collect a fixed fee, then encourage use.
- **Monitoring costs.** If usage is costly to monitor, or annoying to pay for, skip the usage fee even though an access fee doesn't match each user's value. The *Wall Street Journal* charges for online access, not per story or per minute.
- **Competitive industries** can mostly follow everyone else. A new suburban mall has little choice but free parking with tenants as the money side, and an online media property probably can't charge viewers, while ad rates are set in a competitive market. **Pioneers** have no competitive constraint and no model either. OpenTable and Alibaba were smart or lucky and got pricing right at the start.
- A balance that was right at launch doesn't stay optimal by itself, because market conditions change. Companies usually need to keep the pricing strategy that got them to critical mass, and the balancing goes on after they cross the frontier.

## Case: fleet cards and the balancing act on a moving train

- **The platform.** Long-haul fleets do most US trucking, and fuel is their biggest variable expense after drivers. There are more than 2,500 truck stops near major highways. Many belong to chains, and Love's Travel Stops operates about 300. Drivers buy about 100 million gallons a day, roughly $400 million. Most pay with a **fleet card**, which Comdata invented for long-haul trucking in 1981. FleetCor (which now owns Comdata) and WEX are among the largest providers.
- **What the card does.** With WEX Fleet One, a fleet sets controls (no beer, no overpriced stops) and sees spending in real time. WEX fronts the money, pays the truck stop within about ten days, and bills the fleet, so it carries significant outstanding loans to fleets. Its software sends drivers to the nearest stop with the best fuel price, which matters most to small fleets that can't negotiate with the chains. The card network is the matchmaker between this driver and this truck stop.
- **The structure.** WEX earns roughly three-quarters of its trucking revenue from truck stops. In a typical $2.00 transaction, the stop pays $1.50 and the fleet $0.50, with no late-payment charges. Fleets pay less because they decide whether a transaction happens at all: without a card and permission, a stop gets nothing from that fleet. Large chains pay lower per-transaction fees than small ones. They bring more business directly, and they also make the network more valuable to every fleet, which makes it more valuable to every stop. Anchor stores at malls get lower rent per square foot for the same reason.
- **Rebalancing over 40 years.** Until the 1970s, fleets gave drivers cash and wired more when it ran out. In the early 1970s Comdata introduced a check-like product: fleets paid a fee per check, and truck stops got guaranteed payment free, which made sense because the check had value to fleets only if stops would cash it. With fleet cards, the companies offered to steer traffic to stops that paid higher fees, and the industry moved to a flatter structure with similar shares from each side. In the late 1990s, rising fuel prices and competition made fleets far more price-sensitive, and consolidation gave a few large truck-stop chains bargaining power. Around 2000 the industry moved to a tilted structure: prices fell for fleets and rose for stops, with smaller increases for the large chains. Over four decades the structure went from fleets paying and stops subsidized, to balanced, to stops contributing considerably more. FleetCor and WEX operate profitably on this model.

## Case: newspapers rebalancing toward readers

Print newspapers used content to attract readers, who paid less than the cost of printing and distribution (the subsidy side), and sold readers' attention to advertisers (the money side). Online competition on both sides made both groups more price-sensitive from the early 2000s. Reader revenue was no more than 20% of total newspaper revenue from 1997 through 2007, and more than a third by 2013. Reader revenue per unit of average daily circulation rose 34% from 1997 to 2013, and 17% from 2007 to 2013 alone. The rebalancing stabilized revenue but didn't save many papers. Indirect network effects in reverse made decline inevitable: less ad revenue meant less content and higher reader prices, which meant fewer readers and fewer advertisers.
