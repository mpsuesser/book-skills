# Beyond the castle walls: ecosystems, sides, and whether to be a matchmaker at all

## The ecosystem

- Platforms are seldom "self-sufficient fiefdoms" where all the value is created inside the moat-protected castle walls. An **ecosystem** is all the people, businesses, institutions and other things that, because they interact with one another, affect the value a platform can create. Windows's ecosystem includes PC makers and software developers, and also makers of mouse pads, keyboards, DVDs and surge protectors, plus training and maintenance providers.
- **Mall example.** The site must suit shoppers *and* delivery trucks. If the roads aren't there, the developer may have to work with local governments to build them and may pay part of the cost. Affluent nearby towns, a stop on a major bus route, and distance from competing malls, stand-alone department stores and shopping streets all raise the mall's value. A regional economic decline lowers it.
- **Interdependence cuts both ways.** A successful mall lifts nearby restaurants and stand-alone stores, and a vibrant community lifts the mall. Windows 8's unpopularity hurt every firm in the Windows ecosystem.
- **What pioneers do.** Understand the ecosystem before operating in it. Devise incentives to win its support, or to get participants to act in ways that help the platform and the ecosystem. Otherwise do without some third parties and solve the problem yourself, as OpenTable did by building table management software instead of waiting for others to.

## Single-sided, multisided, or a mix

Every business decides what it does inside its walls and what it leaves to partners. For some the basic choice is whether to be single-sided or a matchmaker, which means ceding some control to others in the ecosystem. Some businesses (payment cards, OpenTable) can only be matchmakers. Others choose, and some reverse the choice later.

- **The trade-off.** A single-sided firm escapes the chicken-and-egg problem but can't use indirect network effects for explosive growth. Once YouTube had enough uploaders and viewers, each drew more of the other, and its only serious problem was server capacity.
- **Start single-sided, then open up.** Palm launched the Palm Pilot with its own suite of apps, avoiding the problem of recruiting developers to an untested device, and became a multisided platform once it succeeded. The iPhone took the same route (below).
- **Single-sided buys control at the cost of capital.** When Zappos switched from a mall for shoe manufacturers to a one-sided online shoe store, it gained a more consistent buyer experience but had to carry inventory and handle shipping itself. Netflix pays producers a fixed fee for streaming rights, often for several years. Producers never deal with subscribers, and Netflix fully controls the customer relationship.
- **Control shapes incentives.** Uber drivers choose when and what to drive (within vehicle standards), and Uber sets the fares. If drivers set their own fares, competition would lower prices in the short run and please riders who got rides, but driving would pay less, drivers would leave, and fewer cars at lower fares could be worse for riders. Uber's model has spread to other services: HourlyNerd links businesses with experts against consulting firms, and Coursera links teachers and students against universities. Weigh the effect on platform value of what you control yourself against what you cede to participants.
- **Amazon started single-sided** in 1994 as a reseller like Walmart, buying books from publishers and selling to readers, then running electronics, toys and the rest the same way. That made sense in the US, which had a well-developed offline retail market, unlike China, where Alibaba could start Taobao as an online mall. Amazon's edge was a "vast selection online," more than 2.5 million titles by 1997, plus convenience. Amazon Marketplace (2000) let third parties choose products and prices under rules against bad behavior.

### A little bit single, a little bit multi

- **Vertically integrated platform (Figure 7-1).** Customer A's (users) on one side. On the other, Customer B's that are complementary products used with the platform. Some come from the platform owner, the rest from unaffiliated third parties, and matches happen with both. Windows is three-sided: more than 16 million apps for users, and competing PC makers who drove down the price of Windows computers. Microsoft also sells its own apps, and Office brought in more revenue than Windows itself in 2013. Vertically integrated platforms can run big businesses that compete with their own participants.
- **Reseller platform (Figure 7-2).** One firm runs both a platform where consumers and third-party sellers deal directly and a store that buys from makers or wholesale distributors and resells to consumers. Amazon had more than two million third-party sellers by 2014, and they supplied over 40% of the items sold on Amazon. Shoppers see offers from Amazon's store and from third parties side by side.
- **Degrees.** Some platforms are just a little bit single: Xbox Studios publishes games for Microsoft's own Xbox. Some single-sided businesses are just a little bit multi: Tencent earns most of its revenue from its own games but lets third-party developers use its system.

## How many sides, and when to open each

- Pioneers rarely start with a master plan. They decide how many sides to have and when to open each as they go, and if they are lucky or good they find the right path as the market responds.
- **Facebook**: two-sided at Harvard in February 2004 (message senders and receivers), extended to other colleges and high schools and to everyone in September 2006. Three-sided from mid-2004 with advertising: $382,000 that year, Apple and Victoria's Secret signing in 2005, almost $11.5 billion in 2014. A fourth side in May 2007 with a developer platform (Zynga's Farmville drove heavy traffic). A fifth and sixth with brand pages for businesses sending and receiving messages. **Figure 7-3** shows the six sides as a hexagon: advertisers, businesses (senders), friends (receivers), businesses (receivers), app developers, friends (senders).
- **Even natural matchmakers choose how many sides to have**, and may supply some things themselves rather than leave them to participants. Apple and Microsoft both rely on independent developers for apps. Apple, except for one brief period, has always made its own hardware and runs a two-sided platform of developers and users. Microsoft runs a three-sided one, with PC manufacturers as the third side.
- **More sides, more complexity.** Each added side can add positive indirect network effects and growth, but complicates the business. Microsoft juggles many PC makers with their own aims, while Apple can tune its hardware and software together.
- **Sometimes a potential side isn't one.** Microsoft asked manufacturers to build the Xbox, as third parties build Windows PCs. None wanted to, because consoles are the *subsidy* side of video game platforms, and Microsoft makes them itself.

## Case: smartphones, from dysfunction to two healthy ecosystems

- **The mess in 2005.** More than 1.5 billion people had mobile phones, and smartphones were growing fast, but their operating systems were rudimentary and most developers had given up on them. Every carrier ran a walled garden: developers needed each carrier's permission, had to negotiate hundreds of contentious deals for global reach, and had to customize for dozens of handsets. Mobile software was "one of the most dysfunctional in all technology." Apple and Google, nine miles apart, attacked these frictions with very different approaches built on the same principles.
- **Symbian, the leader that couldn't clean it up.** It was formed in 1998 as a joint venture of handset makers and a small London software firm, because the handset makers feared paying Microsoft for a dominant mobile OS the way PC makers paid for Windows. It ran on almost 60% of smartphones sold from 2004 to 2008, 5.5 times Microsoft's mobile OS. In 2007 handset makers sold almost 78.7 million Symbian phones, and developers had written about 10,000 apps. In 2006 six of the largest handset makers owned it, including Nokia, which sold half the world's phones. It looked like a successful two-sided business, but frictions paralyzed it:
  - Each owner wanted to differentiate, so Symbian built multiple user interfaces that each maker customized further. Developers had to tailor apps for each handset maker and struggled to get API information under "mixed platform control."
  - Carriers distributed more than 90% of phones, and Symbian couldn't stand up to them. One carrier had 10,000 requirements for Symbian products on its network, so there could be no global releases, only carrier-by-carrier negotiation. Nokia barely sold in the US because the big US carriers wouldn't carry its phones.
  - Symbian couldn't help developers deal with carriers. A developer at the UK carrier Orange couldn't get his own employer to approve an app because it would cannibalize thirty-year-old legacy technology.

  **Lesson:** starting a two-sided platform and getting customers on both sides isn't enough. The platform has to nurture a healthy ecosystem, and Symbian made its ecosystem more dysfunctional. Nokia dropped it for Windows Phone in 2011. Microsoft bought Nokia's handset business in early 2014, yet Windows Phone took only about 3% of smartphone sales in the first half of 2015, with about 385,000 apps.
- **Apple: one side, then two.** Jobs feared that "the device that can eat our lunch is the cell phone." The 2005 ROKR (Motorola hardware, Cingular distribution, iPod inside) flopped. Apple then designed its own phone and outsourced manufacturing, previewed it on January 9, 2007, and shipped it on June 29, 2007 as a **single-sided** business. It ran iOS, adapted from Mac OS and licensed to no other handset maker, with Apple's own apps plus Google Maps and YouTube and no third-party apps, because Jobs didn't want outsiders to "mess it up, infect it with viruses, or pollute its integrity" (web apps through Safari were allowed). Apple signed exclusive deals with one carrier per country. AT&T's ran four years, and carriers gave Apple essentially full control of the handset and couldn't install their own apps. Exclusivity was costly: AT&T had only 26.2% of US connections in Q2 2007, leaving 73.8% of subscribers without the iPhone unless they switched. After four board meetings Apple reversed course. Jobs's open letter to developers came on October 17, 2007, the SDK in March 2008, and the App Store in July 2008. Apps reach users only through the store, under strict review. A year after launch the iPhone was a two-sided platform connecting users and developers.
- **Google: organize the ecosystem.** Larry Page saw users moving from fixed to mobile devices, where Google earned its ad money only on desktops, knew from experience that developing for hundreds of handsets and carriers was a nightmare, and feared Microsoft extending its PC dominance. In spring 2005 Google bought Andy Rubin's Android, an open-source mobile OS any handset maker could install free. After the iPhone preview it scrapped its "Sooner" prototype (no touch screen) for the touch-screen "Dream." The iPhone helped Google: handset makers had no OS to compete with it, and carriers without the iPhone faced years against a rival that had it. On November 5, 2007, Google unveiled not a phone but a framework for organizing the ecosystem, the **Open Handset Alliance** (OHA).
  - **Commitment strategy.** Carriers and handset makers were wary of lock-in, so Android came under a license that let anyone use and modify it free, with no dependence on Google for updates. Free mattered to cost-conscious makers: the OS and other software had been about a fifth of a handset's cost.
  - **The collective action problem.** Open source risked Symbian-style fragmentation. Carriers and makers jointly gain from a standard OS that attracts many apps and users, but each gains individually from making its version slightly different. Google's remedy was a coalition (34 OHA members at launch) that agreed to keep a standard version of Android, enforced through a certification process, so apps written for standard Android would run on every device. "*That*—the organization of the ecosystem—was its big news."
  - A week later came the SDK, a contest for the best apps ($10 million in prizes, deadline April 14, 2008), and Google's own apps such as Maps ported over. Android started out multisided.
  - **Carriers.** Apple's deals with their rivals softened carriers' resistance. Verizon, once hostile to Google, urged it to provide a phone to compete with AT&T's iPhone. Google gave carriers most of the commissions on app distribution, and one by one the walls came down. The HTC Dream (October 22, 2008) did moderately against the 3G iPhone. The real battle began with the Motorola Droid on November 6, 2009.
- **Results.** Developers now write for iOS and Android and reach users worldwide regardless of carrier, carriers no longer block apps, and both firms make sure apps run on most of their handsets. Third-party apps turned out to be what interested users in both phones: in 2015 Americans spent 71% of their smartphone time in apps, and handset makers' other operating systems sold few phones, probably for lack of apps. By 2015 more than 452 million people used iPhones and 2.47 billion used Android phones, with about 1.5 million and 1.6 million apps respectively. Counts aren't the whole story: in late 2015 consumers spent about twice as much time in apps on iOS as on Android, and developers favored iOS because that's where the paying users were.

## Foundational platforms and the turbocharging technologies

- **Foundational multisided platforms** ("platforms-for-platforms") provide core services that other matchmakers build on. **Internet service providers** connect end users with **edge providers** (online businesses that reach users through an ISP, such as Pandora). Fixed ISPs (Comcast, Time Warner Cable in Manhattan, Free in Paris, CNC in Beijing) run wires and cables into buildings, and mobile ISPs (mobile network operators such as T-Mobile) reach devices by radio from cell towers. Until recently ISPs charged users for access to edge providers but not edge providers for access to users. **Operating systems**, the **invisible engines** (also called software platforms), offer services to apps through APIs and run the hardware. Windows and Mac OS do this on PCs, iOS and Android on mobile. Facebook is an invisible engine too, through its APIs for developers.
- Their importance is "impossible to overstate." By giving matchmakers low-cost access to almost everyone, ISPs let them capture enormous positive indirect network effects with little investment. To expand from San Francisco to Brussels, Uber built no distribution facility and hired no dispatchers. It recruited drivers and hired lawyers for regulatory fights. Uber paid only a small fee for the iOS and Android development tools and services (the keyboard, geolocation). People used it 140 million times in 2014 and operated in more than 350 cities in 67 countries by November 2015. Mobile operating systems carry the old players too: Google earned 20% of its ad revenue from mobile apps in 2014, and Facebook 73% in Q1 2015. A nightclub has a capacity limit, while Tinder (launched September 2012) had 10 million daily active users in 24 languages by September 2014.
- **The six turbocharging technologies** reduce the cost, raise the speed and widen the scope of connections between sides. They reinforce and compound one another, with no slowdown in sight:
  1. **More powerful chips.** The first IBM PC chip (1981) had 29,000 transistors and the iPhone 6 chip (2014) two billion, about 300 times faster. More than a billion PCs were in use in 2008, with sales flattening from 2010. More than 1.2 billion smartphones and 200 million tablets sold in 2014.
  2. **The Internet**, a network of networks run by the US government and fully opened to worldwide commercial traffic in 1993. More than 3.25 billion people, 44% of 7.38 billion, had access in November 2015.
  3. **The World Wide Web** (HTML from 1990), with 173 million active websites in November 2015. Many edge providers are matchmakers.
  4. **Broadband communications.** More than 750 million fixed broadband subscriptions by the end of 2014, with an average speed of 4.5 Mbps across 199 countries (three times 2008, enough to stream a movie). Mobile went from 9.6 kbps on GSM before 2000 to 3G from about 2001 and 4G from about 2011, with Verizon advertising 5 to 10 Mbps in July 2015. Mobile broadband subscriptions per 100 people stood at 32 globally, 84 in developed countries and 21 in developing ones, and penetration in 2014 was almost four times 2009.
  5. **Programming languages and operating systems**, from the 1950s through C (Bell Labs, early 1970s) and Java (Sun, early 1990s). More than 11 million professional developers worked in ninety countries in 2014. The division of labor between operating systems and apps unleashed the microchip.
  6. **The Cloud**: 5.5 million servers. A new matchmaker rents storage and computation (from Amazon, for example) instead of buying servers, and rents more as it grows.
- **One world.** Mobile devices are folding the online and offline worlds together. Evans found a ride in Brussels through Uber, and stores use payment apps and beacons to connect with shoppers inside. Matchmakers built on these technologies follow the same economic principles as their older siblings, just with far more power and reach (see [disruption.md](disruption.md)).
