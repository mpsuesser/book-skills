# Glossary: the vocabulary of the new economics of multisided platforms

The authors' definitions, given as they apply to multisided platforms, with their examples.

- **Access fee**: the price charged for getting onto the platform. Example: a card issuer's annual fee buys access to the merchants that accept the card.
- **Anchor tenant** (also **marquee customer**): a significant customer that attracts others to the platform, either of its own type or of another type drawn to it. Department stores and big-box retailers draw shoppers to malls, and they persuade smaller retailers to rent space in the expectation of traffic.
- **Behavioral externality**: actions by platform participants that directly affect other participants, positively or negatively. Unlike network externalities, they depend on participants' behavior, not their number. Example: an app developer who puts malware in an app imposes costs on users.
- **Critical mass**: the minimum sets of participants on the platform's sides needed for it to ignite, meaning to achieve self-sustaining growth. For a two-sided platform it is typically a range of combinations, not a single pair of numbers. A B2B exchange needs enough buyers and sellers to interest each side. With enough, more join. Without enough, it loses the ones it has.
- **Death spiral**: the self-reinforcing loss of participants on either side once a platform loses critical mass, ending in collapse. A dead mall loses shoppers, then retailers, then more shoppers, until no one wants to locate or shop there.
- **Direct network effect**: the effect of adding a participant on others in the *same* group. Positive when the addition lets others reach and interact with more people (telephone users who both make and receive calls). Negative when it hurts others through congestion or competition (another man at a singles bar means more competition and less room to mingle).
- **Ecosystem**: the businesses, institutions and other environmental factors that raise or lower the value a platform can create for its participants. A mall is worth more to retailers with easy road access and with more distance from competing malls, stand-alone department stores and shopping streets.
- **Edge provider**: an online business that delivers services or content to end users through an ISP. Example: Pandora streaming music to listeners on cable broadband or a mobile network.
- **Externality**: a benefit or cost one participant imposes on another without direct monetary compensation. It can come from a network effect or from behavior.
- **Foundational platform**: a "platform for platforms," providing core services to other multisided platforms. ISPs are one kind (Comcast connects users to Google's search engine). Operating systems, or invisible engines, are another (Android lets developers offer apps to users).
- **Friction**: costs or other impediments to mutually advantageous interactions and exchanges. Before online reservations, diners had to find restaurants, look up numbers, call, maybe leave messages and try again, while restaurants staffed phones and kept notebooks.
- **Governance system**: rules prohibiting bad behavior (negative behavioral externalities) by participants, plus the mechanisms that enforce them, including detection and punishment. eBay bars sellers from misleading content and buyers from not paying for what they agreed to buy.
- **Ignition**: when a platform reaches critical mass and begins self-sustaining growth. M-PESA grew very quickly once it had enough would-be senders and receivers and enough agents to sell and redeem e-money.
- **Implosion**: when a platform can't reach or keep critical mass, loses customers, and eventually enters a death spiral. Many mobile-money schemes never got enough subscribers or agents and withered away.
- **Indirect network effect**: the effect of adding one type of participant on *another* type. Positive: an extra restaurant on OpenTable gives diners another option. Negative: more radio ads lower listeners' value.
- **Internet service provider (ISP)**: an entity that connects end users and edge providers through the Internet. Fixed ISPs run wires or cables into premises, where people may set up wireless networks (Comcast, by cable). Mobile ISPs reach devices over the wireless spectrum through cell towers (T-Mobile).
- **Invisible engine** (also **operating system**, **software platform**): software that provides services to apps through application programming interfaces (APIs) and lets users run those apps, generally operating the hardware, including the central processing chip. Windows is one for PCs. Facebook is one too, offering APIs to developers of apps used on Facebook.
- **Matchmaker**: a business that helps two or more different kinds of customers find each other and engage in mutually beneficial interactions. It doesn't mean finding perfect matches, as a village marriage broker would, but good trading partners. A payment card network brings retailers and consumers together on one agreed payment method. Another name for a multisided platform.
- **Money side**: in a two-sided platform, the group that provides all or virtually all of the profit. In a multisided platform, one or more groups may contribute nothing while two or more together provide it. Most newspapers make all their profit from advertisers and subsidize readers with content.
- **Multihoming**: participants using two or more similar platforms, or able to do so easily. Many consumers carry several payment cards and choose one at the till.
- **Multisided platform**: a business operating a physical or virtual place (a platform) that helps two or more different groups find each other and interact. The groups are its **sides**. Facebook is a virtual place for friends to send and receive messages, advertisers to reach users, and developers to provide apps.
- **Pioneering platform**: the first, or one of the first, to identify a friction and build a matchmaker to solve it. Unlike later entrants, it usually has to work out the pricing, chicken-and-egg and design problems itself. OpenTable had no other firm's experience to guide it.
- **Pricing level**: the overall level of prices to both sides. For transaction fees, it is the total paid by both sides per transaction. An exchange's total fee for a trade is the sum of the (usually negative) fee to the liquidity provider and the (usually positive) fee from the liquidity taker.
- **Pricing structure**: how the price or revenue is split among the sides. Fleets pay about 25% and truck stops about 75% of fleet-card fees. One side pays more than 100% when another side's price is negative: restaurants pay more than 100% of OpenTable's fees, since diners pay nothing and earn rewards.
- **Screening device**: a way to limit participation to those on each side whom the other side finds attractive. **Exclusionary vibes** signal that only certain kinds of people should join (lower-income people for a mall, Jewish people for a dating site). **Exclusionary amenities** offer value only to the targeted type (fly-fishing articles in a magazine courting buyers of fly-fishing goods and services).
- **Self-supply**: a platform that connects consumers with providers supplies some of the goods or services itself. It may be a start-up tactic for ignition, or a lasting business because the platform has the expertise and can profit from it.
- **Signaling device**: an indicator that participants belong to the same platform and can profitably interact. Card systems put logos on cards and at merchant terminals.
- **Single-homing**: participants standardizing on, and using, only one platform. Most people have a single wired broadband provider at home.
- **Subsidy side**: a group whose payments don't cover the cost of serving it. There can be more than one subsidized group as long as one group acts as the money side. Most consumers of ad-supported media pay little or nothing for content of significant value.
- **Transaction cost**: economists' term for friction.
- **Turbocharged matchmaker**: a matchmaker that benefits from a significant combination of powerful chips, the Internet, the web, broadband, programming languages and operating systems, and the Cloud. Airbnb benefits from all of them.
- **Two-sided market**: the original name for industries with two-sided platforms. "Two-sidedness is a characteristic of businesses, not always of industries."
- **Two-step strategy**: an ignition strategy that first secures substantial participation by one group, then recruits the other by offering access to the first. Ad-supported media gather eyeballs first and then sell access to them to advertisers.
- **Usage externality**: the benefit a party gets from transacting with another party on the matchmaker, even if it can't reach anyone else. All multisided platforms have usage externalities as well as indirect network externalities.
- **Usage fee**: a fee paid for interacting with another participant. Example: OpenTable's $1 per person reservation fee to restaurants.
- **Zigzag strategy**: an ignition strategy that raises participation on the different sides at the same time. The name pictures the platform pushing one group up, then another, and repeating to set off positive feedback. In practice it may push on all groups at once while varying the effort for each.
