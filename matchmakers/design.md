# Interior design: building the platform to maximize activity and value

Every matchmaker, physical or virtual, decides how to design its place, built from bricks or lines of code, so that participants find each other and make mutually beneficial exchanges, and often builds tools to help them find good matches. The details vary by business, but the principles below almost always apply.

## Make the market thick

- A **thick** market has enough participants on each side who could benefit from dealing with the other side. A **thin** market doesn't. eBay built a thick market for obscure things: sell your grandmother's antique cookbook there and you'll do well, while the old local flea markets were too thin.
- **Stock exchanges and tick size.** Exchanges traditionally kept markets thick through **market makers**, one side of the exchange platform, with ordinary buyers and sellers as the other. Each market maker has to post a **bid** price (to buy up to a stated number of shares) and an **offer** price (to sell) for its stocks. That means bearing risk, for example buying 10,000 IBM shares because someone else just learned bad news. Exchanges compensate them partly through the **tick size**, the increment in which bids and offers are made: one cent on US exchanges now, an eighth of a dollar (12.5 cents) before. The bid must sit below the offer by at least one tick, and market makers keep the spread. A bigger tick makes market making more attractive and trading more costly, so the exchange balances the two in setting it.

## Large or small: getting the right participants

- Participants on both sides differ, and their wants change over time. Each needs enough options to find a good match for what he or she wants *now*.
- **Design large or design small.** Monster admits every employer and every job seeker: it designs large. Specialized recruiters match a limited number of prescreened employers and candidates and build a reputation on quality: they design small. Successful matchmakers make different choices here.
- **Right, not many.** A reservation platform needs the right restaurants in the right city at the right time and can't make up the shortfall with irrelevant ones. A high-end mall needs high-end stores *and* well-off shoppers. Packing it with people who can't shop at expensive stores won't attract those stores, and renting to downscale stores won't attract high-end shoppers.
- **Screening device**: a method of limiting participation to people on each side whom the other side finds attractive. It makes the platform smaller by discouraging the not-quite-right, makes good matches easier for those who pass, and lets the platform charge more because it creates more value. Two kinds:
  - **Exclusionary amenities**: offer something only the right participants want. *Hoist* magazine runs articles for overhead crane operators and sells ads to firms selling crane-related equipment, which is far more efficient for them than an ad in *People*. The glossary's example is fly-fishing articles for a magazine courting fly-fishing advertisers.
  - **Exclusionary vibes**: signal who should join and who shouldn't. Doing this without seeming discriminatory means getting the vibe just right. FarmersOnly.com courts "single country gals" and "local farmers and cowboys" with a name, and a home-page couple in hay under "Single in the Country," that screens out "city folks" who "just don't get it."
- **Limit same-side competition.** Where members of one side dislike competing with each other, each additional rival lowers the platform's value to them. The B2B exchanges drove suppliers away by creating intense price competition. Capping competition on that side makes it easier to recruit the other side and raises total value.
- **Court same-side marquee participants.** Some participants are so attractive that others on the *same* side gain from the traffic they bring. Malls charge low rent to traffic-pulling stores. Nightclubs let the hottest men and women skip the line, since a particularly attractive man is competition for other men but will draw more than one appealing woman.
- **Congestion** is another reason to limit size: crowding makes searching and interacting hard. Physical limits force entry restrictions, as with nightclubs, which need fire-code compliance and room to move. The other fix is to connect people faster. Ride-hailing apps such as Didi Kuaidi let drivers and riders connect quickly and confidently, where people who called taxis often hailed one instead out of doubt. Search engines design results pages carefully because every extra item adds congestion.

## Searching and matching

Getting the right participants isn't enough: the design has to make interactions happen, because that is why participants join and how the platform makes money.

- **Standards.** Organize participants around a standard they all use. Operating systems give developers SDKs. Twitter's 140-character maximum told receivers what to expect and senders what to do.
- **Physical layout.** A successful nightclub's interior makes mingling easy and appealing. Stalls at farmers' markets and bazaars are arranged so merchants get foot traffic and buyers find what they want.
- **Signaling device**: an indicator that participants belong to the same platform and can profitably interact. Card networks use the **card bug** on the card and the **acceptance mark** at the merchant, so a consumer with a Visa-bugged debit card from a US bank knows she can pay at a petrol station in the south of France that shows the Visa mark. Acceptance marks mattered most early on, when few merchants took cards.
- **Algorithms and tools.** At the top end are complex matching algorithms: Google Search matching keywords, websites and ads, computerized securities exchanges, and ad networks matching ads to readers in an instant. In the middle are search tools sophisticated enough to find a crazy cat video, or a Mongolian restaurant in the Chicago area with a table at 9:00 p.m. next Thursday.

## Balancing externalities: matching parties who wouldn't choose to meet

- **Annie and Jack.** Annie, a couch potato, would pay $5 not to see ads for Jack's Used Cars, and Jack would pay $20 to get his ad in front of her. The platform pays Annie $10 to watch and charges Jack $15. Annie comes out $5 ahead, Jack $5 ahead, and the platform makes $5. Everyone is better off.
- **People dislike ads, and they pay to avoid them.** Pandora, HBO and Netflix charge a premium to users who want no ads, and people buy recorders like TiVo to skip them. Radio and television handle the negative indirect network effect two ways: content so good that people endure the ads, or fewer ads. US radio stations compete partly by bragging about how few commercial interruptions they have.
- **Ad-supported media** run on this logic. Instead of paying Annie cash, they pay her in content: magazine features, newspaper news, radio music and talk, search results and TV shows are all subsidies, "bait or bribes," to attract viewers. Successful media earn enough from advertisers to cover the content and make a profit. Two remedies for audiences that dislike ads: make the content good enough that people put up with the ads, or limit the ads (US radio stations boast of few interruptions).
- **Making ads hard to avoid.** *Vogue* buries its table of contents among pages of opening ads and leaves full-page ads unnumbered, so readers struggle to flip to a story. It is still a good deal for the reader, who gets a glossy magazine for a price that probably doesn't cover printing and distribution, never mind the articles. The advertisers reach her, and *Vogue* profits from the match.
- **Search diversion**: designing the platform so a searcher considers things she wasn't looking for. Karen wants shoes as quickly as possible, but she has a dog and doesn't know what she's missing at Maggie's Dog Boutique. Malls are the leading practitioners. E-commerce sites do a version through recommendations based on history and current browsing. Done well, the distraction and longer shopping trip raise sales *and* customer satisfaction.

## Case: Aventura Mall

- **The platform.** An upscale mall north of Miami, called "shopping heaven": more than 300 stores, six of them anchors, 2.7 million square feet on three levels, the largest mall in Florida and the third largest in the US. It opened in April 1983 with 1.2 million square feet, on land Harry Soffer and other investors had bought (more than 785 acres) a quarter century earlier. His son Donald built the town of Aventura, a golf course and condominiums around it. Mall developers draw on decades of learning going back to the Southdale Center in Edina, Minnesota (1956, designed by Victor Gruen), the first modern mall.
- **Layout (Figure 8-1, first level).** A long diagonal concourse runs from JCPenney (with the Gap near its entrance) up to Bloomingdale's. A short branch leads down to Nordstrom. Macy's and Macy's Men's & Home Furniture sit on one side and Sears on the other. Each anchor faces its own parking lot, one of six. A center court has a three-story atrium, with a perimeter hallway on the second level. There are a dozen dining places, a 17-vendor food court and an AMC theater on the third level. Events include a farmers' market in the atrium, a pet adoption event, a chocolate festival, and a Santa's Village with more than a million lights.
- **Store selection as screening and signaling.** The anchor mix and the AMC theater draw different groups of people, each anchor bringing shoppers the others wouldn't. Bloomingdale's and Nordstrom, along with Tiffany & Co. and Cartier, signal a high-end mall: a "Luxury Shopping Destination," an "upscale super-regional shopping mall." One visitor said it was "Definitely not the type of mall where you come if you're trying to find some bargains." Luxury brands locate there because they know the foot traffic suits them, so stores and shoppers both find good matches. Non-anchor stores span 22 directory categories.
- **Layout as search diversion.** The mall isn't designed to minimize walking. Comparing sweaters at Bloomingdale's and Nordstrom means the longest walk, and the perimeter hallway rules out the shortest route. "Take your walking shoes," a visitor warned. More foot traffic past each store raises the odds of a purchase: stores benefit and shoppers don't. Some malls put the up and down escalators at opposite ends.
- **Clustering and the agglomeration effect.** Similar stores are grouped together: luxury stores such as Cole Haan line the corridor to Nordstrom, and more down-market stores such as the Gap the corridor to JCPenney. Tiffany and Cartier face each other. No corridor leads to Sears, which doesn't feed traffic to the luxury stores, and few big brands are near it. Clustering similar stores draws more traffic (the **agglomeration effect**) and also sharpens competition between them. The placements were probably settled with the stores, especially the anchors, which have considerable bargaining power. For Tiffany and Cartier, agglomeration evidently outweighed competition.
- **Upkeep.** A "beautiful and classy place" doesn't happen by accident. The operator makes stores keep up appearances and service, and makes people behave, which needs rules (see [governance.md](governance.md)).
