# Cross-cutting cases: OpenTable, Alibaba and the B2B extinction, M-PESA

The book returns to these three cases across many chapters. Each one carries several principles, so each is kept whole here.

## OpenTable: the near-death of a pioneer

- **Friction.** In 1998 Julie Templeton spent almost four hours phoning to book three San Francisco dinners. Restaurants took reservations in pencil, and tables went empty because they couldn't tell anyone they were free. Both sides spent heavily to find each other, and still tables sat empty while couples stayed home.
- **Building standalone value first.** Chuck Templeton founded OpenTable in October 1998 and found most restaurants had no computer, let alone Internet at the host stand. So the company built a table management system first: reservations, table availability, cancellations, tracking of best customers. When restaurants balked at buying it, OpenTable switched to a small installation fee plus monthly rent. Some better restaurants signed for the software alone, wanting help managing tables and tracking customers more than filling seats. It had about ten by early 1999.
- **Pricing from day one.** The consumer site opened in March 1999, free, with points redeemable for discounts. Restaurants paid $1 per diner seated through the site. Later prices: $199 a month for the system and $1.00 per seated diner. Diners pay nothing and get rewards, so restaurants pay more than 100% of the fees. OpenTable has never charged diners a penny, and "free" was never a temporary gimmick.
- **The eyeballs mistake.** The company raised $36 million in venture capital between 1998 and early 2000, and in May 2000 an experienced CEO took the investors' "grab all the eyeballs" advice. By summer 2001 OpenTable had restaurants in fifty cities but not many in any one. Diners wanted at least a dozen choices for their time, cuisine and part of town, and a table for four in Seattle doesn't help someone in San Francisco. It was burning $1.1 million a month, and investors weighed shutting it down.
- **Go narrow/go deep.** In fall 2001 board member Thomas Layton took over and concentrated on four cities: San Francisco, Chicago, New York and Washington, DC. Enough restaurants drew diners, diners drew more restaurants, and new restaurants especially saw the value of filling empty seats. Most restaurant costs are fixed in the short run, so every extra filled seat earns significant profit. It had ignited by 2004 and then rolled the approach out city by city, going first for the most popular restaurants on Zagat's lists. It went public in 2009 (market cap $626 million on the first day) and was sold to Priceline for $2.6 billion in 2014. By 2015, 32,000 restaurants, mostly fine dining, used its software, 16 million people a month booked through it, and more than 830 million diners had been seated across the US, Canada, Germany, Japan, Mexico and the UK. It took about six years to get off the ground, with patient investors.
- **The paradox it shows.** If diners are valuable enough to *pay* in rewards, why ban some? A diner who fails four times in a year to show up without canceling at least thirty minutes ahead loses the account, however much money her kept reservations made OpenTable. No-shows impose costs on the money side, so governance protects it (see [governance.md](governance.md)).
- **The ancient pattern.** In 1950 Frank McNamara's Diners Club, the first card accepted by many merchants, gave cards free to selected wealthy Upper East Siders. That made it easy to sign fourteen upscale Manhattan restaurants, which paid a 10% commission for the same reason restaurants pay OpenTable $1 a head: extra diners and extra margin. It ignited quickly, city by city. Long before either, the Chinese *meiren* tracked eligible men and women across villages, proposed matches and brokered the deal for a fee. The *Book of Songs* says: "How does one find a wife? Without a matchmaker one does not."

## Alibaba: friction big enough to build an empire

- **The friction in 1998.** Zhang Wei ran Foshan Plastic Sheets (founded 1986, five employees, sales of RMB 500,000, about $60,000). GDP per capita was $826. China had about seven landlines per hundred people (South Korea 44, Ecuador 8) and about two mobile subscriptions per hundred, calls were expensive, roads were dreadful and railroads underdeveloped. The courts didn't understand private business, so contracts and property rights couldn't be relied on, and businesses couldn't trust strangers. SMEs were penned into local markets, dealt with people they knew, and insisted on cash on delivery after inspection. Still, reforms had produced more than 154,000 SMEs by 1998, and GDP grew more than 10.2% a year from 1989 to 1998.
- **Communication platform.** In February 1999 Jack Ma (Ma Yun), after China Yellow Pages and a frustrating spell running a ministry's trade sites, gathered seventeen colleagues in his apartment in Lakeside Garden, Hangzhou. alibaba.com (in English, linking global buyers to mostly Chinese sellers) launched in March 1999 and alibaba.com.cn (in Chinese) later that year. The aim was to make the Internet "a fast and cost-effective tool for ensuring the flow of business information" where telecommunications were poor. Everything was free while it built critical mass. Companies got tools to build their own pages so buyers and sellers could check each other out, and by October 1999 more than 41,000 subscribers had pages, about 55% of them from China. Buy and sell offers sat in categories ("industrial supplies," then "bearings"). One February 2000 snapshot showed 21,976 buy offers, 52,863 sell offers and 275,879 responses. BizMail email and Biz Club chat rooms followed, a zigzag between Chinese suppliers and foreign buyers. By December 2001 it had more than a million members, adding one to two thousand a day, and more than 700 product types. The markets had become thick: someone wanting a thousand badminton rackets "could find at least a dozen Chinese suppliers."
- **Trust.** In an Alibaba survey, 90% of businesspeople trading online put trust ahead of quality and price in choosing partners. International TrustPass launched in August 2001, and a domestic version followed the next year, with authentication, documentation, third-party corroboration and an open Feedback Forum where members rated each other. By June 2007 its TradeManager messaging tool peaked at 540,000 users online, more than 200 forums had over 4.2 million registered users, 20.9 million businesses were in the Chinese community and 3.6 million in the international one, and it was the largest B2B marketplace in the world.
- **Alibaba's five SME challenges** (its own list, the frictions it set out to cut): (1) limited geographic presence, (2) fragmented suppliers and buyers, hard to find and talk to, (3) few communication channels and information sources for marketing, (4) small scale and thin sales and marketing resources, (5) no efficient way to judge trading partners' trustworthiness.
- **Retail frictions.** Real GDP grew 7.6% to 11.0% a year from 1995 to 2004 and consumers wanted to shop, but there were few malls outside the big cities, and local officials slowed permits, so consumers couldn't find shops because retailers couldn't find space. Online, shoppers feared goods would never arrive, arrive damaged or not as described, and they had no chargeback like a US credit card. **Taobao** (first half of 2003) let small merchants and individuals sell directly. It beat eBay, which had bought a Chinese site, by keeping transaction fees at zero while building scale and by selling at fixed prices instead of auctions. **Alipay** escrow followed the next year: the shopper pays at purchase, and funds go to the merchant only after she confirms satisfaction, or after a set time passes without complaint. Before, a buyer sent a money order from a bank or post office, and goods typically shipped fifteen to twenty days after the order. Alibaba then built a fulfillment system, partnering with Chinese transport firms while it managed the process and quality control. Escrow, fulfillment and seller ratings together gave consumers confidence.
- **The platform family (Figure 4-1).** Buyers: Chinese consumers (on Taobao, Tmall and Juhuasuan), global consumers (on AliExpress) and global wholesale buyers (on Alibaba.com). Retail sellers, both small and branded (from the US, EU and South Korea), sell on the retail marketplaces and buy on wholesale Taobao. Chinese wholesalers and manufacturers sell on wholesale Taobao, and global ones on Alibaba.com and AliExpress. Taobao feeds traffic to Tmall. The B2B side is 1688.com (alibaba.com.cn renamed in March 2010, since "1688" sounds like Alibaba and like a phrase for the road to prosperity) and alibaba.com. The B2C side is Tmall (2008, branded and upscale) and Taobao (small merchants), and many of their retailers buy on the B2B side. Services come from logistics partners, Alipay (payments) and Alimama (marketing).
- **Scale.** In 2015 there were more than 367 million active buyers and more than 350 million storefronts. Taobao averaged 100 million unique daily visitors in March 2015. Gross merchandise value was $370 billion in 2014. The September 2014 IPO was the largest ever on the NYSE, and market cap was just under $198 billion in late November 2015 (excluding Alipay and some affiliates). It isn't "the Amazon, or eBay, of China." Nothing like it exists outside China: it is the only major Internet property integrating B2B and B2C marketplaces, with features built for trust and communication problems more acute in China than elsewhere. Large frictions in China don't promise the same pie anywhere else.

### The US B2B mass extinction

- By 2001 the US had more than 1,600 B2B exchanges, most specializing in a commodity. A book of the day called them "The Killer Application." Jupiter estimated in 2000 that exchanges would handle $6.3 trillion (42%) of B2B transactions by 2005, and Goldman Sachs estimated $4.5 trillion. By 2005 virtually all were dead, with no eulogies or postmortems.
- **Cause: no serious problem to solve.** US firms could phone or fax for a pittance and had widespread email by the late 1990s. Sophisticated firms used electronic data interchange, and big buyers such as Walmart ran supplier portals. There were trade shows (McCormick Place in Chicago runs them constantly), lawyers and a working legal system. Exchanges that promised buyers the best price offered sellers the worst one, since "the best price for buyers is the worst price for sellers." The exchanges could have paid suppliers to join, but the value pie was too small to cover buyers' gains, profit and supplier payouts. Like online malls, they were something US sellers didn't need to find buyers. Early buyers left for lack of suppliers, early suppliers left for lack of sales, replacements didn't come, the markets stayed thin, and the feedback never turned positive.
- **Clever survivor: Ariba.** The real US frictions were back-office: phone and mail procurement run on primitive mainframe software, paper invoices, and paper checks that arrived slowly. Ariba began as procurement software for large firms' procurement departments, solving one-sided problems with modern software the way OpenTable's table management did for restaurants. In 1999 it opened ariba.com as a network, moving from bilateral to multilateral connections between buyers and sellers. Its market cap passed $40 billion in September 2000 and fell to $1.1 billion by April 2001. It went back to procurement software, added invoicing and payment tracking for suppliers, and by 2005, a decade after it started, began charging both buyers and suppliers to transact on its network. SAP bought it for $4.3 billion in 2012. Bob Solomon, who ran the network through most of the 2000s: "The B2B exchanges never really knew what they were going to do." Ariba solved pressing problems first for procurement departments and later for suppliers, until there were enough of both to set off positive feedback.
- **Principle.** Alibaba built thick markets in narrow product categories and ignited, while the US exchanges stayed thin and fizzled. Friction is necessary but not sufficient, and even with a big friction, ignition is the hardest problem.

## M-PESA: leapfrogging banks in Kenya

- **The friction.** In 2006 Kenya's GDP per capita was $2,156, about 5% of the US level. There were 558 bank branches for 36.3 million people (164th of 203 countries in the World Bank ranking), and only one in seven people had a bank account, often too costly to use. Families were split: husbands and sons worked in Nairobi or other cities, while wives and parents farmed in rural areas and depended on remittances. People traveled about 200 kilometers (124 miles) on average to deliver or collect cash, or trusted bus and taxi drivers, who might steal it or be robbed.
- **The platform.** Safaricom was run by Telkom Kenya, the landline monopoly, until Vodafone took over management in 2000 with a 40% stake. In 2006 about 7.3 million of roughly 20.7 million adults (15 and older) had mobile subscriptions, about 73% with Safaricom. Vodafone's research found that moving money between people was a major problem. It built a feature-phone system: e-money, account details on the SIM card, transactions sent by SMS. Safaricom ran it as the subsidiary M-PESA (*pesa* is Swahili for money).
- **Two chicken-and-egg puzzles.** People needed somewhere to turn cash into e-money and back. A dedicated network of cash-in/cash-out (**CICO**) agents across rural Kenya was uneconomic for the same reason rural bank branches were: volumes too low to cover fixed costs. So M-PESA had to recruit existing businesses whose other revenue covered fixed costs, and run *two* intersecting two-sided platforms: senders with receivers, and a CICO network of agents doing mostly cash-in or mostly cash-out. Senders won't join without receivers, neither will join without agents, and agents won't join without money moving. Most users both send and receive, playing different roles at different times and caring about different things in each (a part-time student in Nairobi sends grocery money home and sometimes gets money back on special occasions). Agents, paid only per transaction, carry the cost and risk of moving and holding cash.
- **How it works.** A subscriber registers at an agent (a small shop, gas station, bank branch or post office): personal details recorded, SIM replaced, PIN issued. He hands over cash to load e-money. To send, he enters the recipient's number, the amount and his PIN, and the money arrives almost instantly. The recipient withdraws cash at an agent, and both get SMS receipts. Deposits are free. M-PESA charges a fee to send and another to withdraw, and pays agents a commission for each customer signed up and each cash-in or cash-out.
- **Balancing the agent network.** M-PESA used stores already selling Safaricom airtime. That meant designing compensation, selecting and recruiting stores, training and supervising staff, and convincing agents in advance that they'd have enough customers. At launch in March 2007 it had 307 agents, covering all seventy district headquarters. The balance: users want more agents nearby, but too many in one area compete for the same customers and cut each agent's profit, a direct *negative* network externality. Agents who find the business not worth it drop it or neglect it, which annoys users. M-PESA grew the agent count in step with users: about 68 registered users per agent at launch and more than 850 by the end of 2007. It placed agents who could register users and do both cash-in and cash-out throughout the country, including the rural areas where receivers clustered (senders were disproportionately urban).
- **Pricing to make senders recruit receivers.** Sending KShs 35,000 cost KShs 30 to a registered user and KShs 400 to an unregistered one, so senders had a strong reason to get recipients to register.

- **Ignition.** An intense marketing campaign registered users and agents before launch, which solved the initial coordination problem but didn't guarantee success. The campaign focused on one big friction: "Send Money Home." Its award-winning ad shows a young man in a suit sending money from his desk, and shillings landing in the apron of a woman on a farm who could be his mother. Her phone shows KShs 1,000 from John on June 3, 2007.
- **Figures 11-1 to 11-3 (March 2007 to February 2010).** Registered users rose to about 1.5 million in January 2008, 3 million in May 2008, 4.5 million in November 2008, 6.5 million in March 2009, 8 million in August 2009 and 9 million in December 2009. Monthly transaction value rose in step: about $60 million in November 2007, $180 million in May 2008, $290 million in fall 2008, $440 million in March 2009, $550 million in July 2009 and a peak of about $720 million in December 2009. By February 2010 there were 9.7 million users (42% of people over 15) moving $678 million a month, or 20% of GDP at an annual rate. Agents grew from 307 to 25,394 (about 2,500 in March 2008, 7,500 at the end of 2008, 13,500 in March 2009, 22,500 in October 2009). Per agent, transaction volume peaked at about $68,000 a month in July 2008, and users per agent at about 1,000 in May 2008. From mid-2008 agents grew a little faster than users, and both measures fell until early 2009, then settled at about $25,000 and 400 users per agent.
- **Figures 11-4 to 11-6 (to March 2015).** More than 25 million users (25.7 million, 99% of adults over 15) were moving more than $2.5 billion a month. Agents reached about 40,000 in late 2010, 65,000 in November 2012, 110,000 in November 2013 and about 129,000 in March 2015. Annual volume as a share of GDP: 2007, 1%. 2008, 7%. 2009, 17%. 2010, 24%. 2011, 32%. 2012, 37%. 2013, 41%. 2014, 45%. By 2014 more than 84% of Kenyan mobile users could send money, pay bills and pay in stores, and Kenyans (44.9 million people) are more likely to use mobile money, and use it more each month, than people in any other country. Other Kenyan operators copied it, but its success "was far from inevitable."
- **More sides once the network was dense.** Airtime and bill payment (electricity, water, gas, schools) were part of the original design. M-PESA then selectively opened the platform to partners. **M-Shwari** (late 2012, with Commercial Bank of Africa; *shwari* means calm) offered savings from KShs 1 at 2% to 5% interest and 30-day loans of KShs 100–20,000 ($1.15–$230) at 7.5%, and had lent KShs 24 billion ($271 million) by early 2015. A KCB Bank Group program (early 2015) offered loans of KShs 50 to 1 million ($0.565–$11,300) over one to six months at 4% to 12%. **Lipa Na M-PESA** (mid-2013; "pay with") gives merchants a till number that shoppers enter on their phones, and had more than 32,000 merchants by December 2014. It may let Kenya leapfrog payment cards and point-of-sale terminals altogether. By late 2015 M-PESA was **four intersecting two-sided platforms**: (1) sender and receiver, (2) registered user and CICO agent, (3) financial services and registered user, (4) registered merchant and registered user, all meeting at the registered user's phone. Mobile money works as a deposit account reached through agents instead of branches, and makes banks irrelevant for many purposes.
- **Africa.** Mobile money schemes started in 37 of 54 African countries, but only eight cracked the chicken-and-egg problem (Table 11-2): Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana, Kenya, Rwanda, Somaliland (part of Somalia, not recognized as a country), Uganda, Tanzania and Zimbabwe. Others are sure to follow, now that a pioneer has shown how. Where it ignited, mobile money removed the need for branches in rough terrain, cut the cost of moving cash, and cushioned families against shocks: one study found M-PESA users kept their consumption steady through negative income shocks, while non-users' household consumption fell 7%. In developing countries mobile money may slow conventional banking, but it is unlikely to threaten it.
- **Old pattern, new engine.** Western Union's money transfer over the telegraph, run since 1871 through CICO agents that were often third parties, is in most ways M-PESA's model without the mobile phone.
