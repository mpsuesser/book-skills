# Ignition: critical mass and the chicken-and-egg problem

## The problem

- **Single-sided launch.** When Hamdi Ulukaya launched Chobani, the path was hard but the direction was clear: get a retailer to stock the yogurt (three small stores on Long Island) and hope consumers like it. His inputs arrived through ordinary supply chains. Nobody had to persuade the milk to show up.
- **Multisided launch.** The restaurant owner loves the idea and asks how many diners use the site. If the answer is none, or few, the restaurant doesn't bother. Diners love the idea too, find few restaurants, and after one bad experience won't try again and may warn their friends. This is the **coordination problem**, or **chicken-and-egg problem**: neither group joins unless the other does, because the product sold to each group *is* access to the other. Solving it always looks easy in hindsight, and it is one of the hardest problems any business faces.
- **Enough, not just some.** Without enough participants on every side, the matchmaker offers a lousy product. Most people walk out of a nightclub that has only a few potential partners.

## Where "grab all the eyeballs" came from

- **Rohlfs and the telephone.** Jeffrey Rohlfs's pioneering paper on early landline service described the **direct network effect** (also called a positive direct network externality): a telephone is worth more the more people it can reach. Above a critical mass of subscribers, others want to join. Below it, subscribers leave and the network gets less attractive still.
- **The one-sided lesson.** In the 1980s economists studying the VCR standards war (Sony's Betamax against the VHS consortium) concluded that whichever standard got a head start, however small, would win, even if it was the poorer technology. Business writers drew two conclusions: winner-take-all markets and an inevitable **first-mover advantage**. By the mid-1990s "build share quickly and worry about money later" was the accepted wisdom for venture-funded dot-coms.
- **Figure 2-1 (Google Ngram, mentions per 10 million bigrams, 1950–2008).** "Network effect(s)" sits near zero in the 1950s, spikes to about 3.5 in 1962 and about 3 around 1973 after Rohlfs, idles around 1 to 1.5 with a spike to about 5 in 1989, then climbs from about 2 in the early 1990s to about 4 in 1996, about 12 in 1999 and about 25 in 2002, holds around 21 to 22 through 2005, peaks at about 33 in 2006, and ends near 22. "First-mover advantage" is near zero until the early 1980s, reaches about 1.5 by 1987, 3 in 1992, 4 in 1995 and 5 in 1998, peaks near 10 in 2002, dips to about 7.5, rises to about 8.5 in 2005, and ends near 7.
- **The two-sided correction.** Rochet and Tirole saw that "many, if not most markets with network externalities are characterized by the presence of two distinct sides." VHS won because its promoters got *both* consumers and content providers on board, not because content providers responded mechanically to the installed base. In the high-definition DVD war that began in 2005, HD-DVD (Toshiba, Microsoft and others) sold more standalone players than Blu-ray (Sony, Apple and others) and still lost. It conceded after Warner Brothers dropped the format in January 2008. Blu-ray had more content providers: Sony's studio made only Blu-ray discs, and there were unconfirmed reports of Sony payments to Warner Brothers and Fox Searchlight. The Blu-ray group used price and contract terms more effectively to get both sides on board. "A better two-sided strategy made the difference."

## Critical mass

- The term comes from 1940s nuclear physics. Enough fissile material nearby (a sphere of 24.3 pounds, or 11 kilograms, of plutonium-239) sustains a chain reaction, and too little lets the neutrons miss and the reaction fizzle. Business writers use the term loosely to mean the size needed to make money or minimize cost. For matchmakers it has a well-defined meaning close to the physics one.
- **Definition (nightclub).** There is critical mass when men and women both think the club is worth coming to, and at least as many new patrons want to come as old patrons want to stop. Below that point one side or both can't find enough candidates, they stop coming, and the thing fizzles. Glossary form: the minimum sets of participants on the platform's sides needed for the platform to ignite, that is, to grow on its own. For a two-sided platform this is a *range* of combinations, not one pair of numbers.
- **Momentum.** Matchmakers have more time than bombs, but there is nothing as definite as physics here. If early customers think the place is good enough and see newcomers arriving, they keep coming back. Without momentum, the longer a platform stays below critical mass, the more people see it as a dead spot, the harder it is to turn around, and the harder it is to keep investors happy as losses mount.

### Figure 5-2: the critical mass frontier

- The axes are the number of Type A customers (vertical) and Type B customers (horizontal). A downward-sloping, convex **critical mass frontier** runs from many-A-few-B (point 1) through points 2, 3 and 4 to few-A-many-B (point 5). Every point on it is a combination with *just enough* participants for growth. There is no magic number, and many different balances can ignite: YouTube would probably have ignited with a somewhat different mix of uploaders and viewers.
- The **growth zone**, above and to the right of the frontier, has self-reinforcing growth. The **implosion zone**, below and to the left (also aptly called "the dead zone"), is where a platform stuck with too few of one type loses the other type, can't attract newcomers, and heads into a death spiral unless something changes.
- Every platform starts at the origin with zero on both sides and has to push across the frontier. Most die on the trek from the southwest corner. Lopsided success fails too. Many YouTube viewers with few uploaders would have seen everything worth seeing and stopped coming: "Lazy Sunday" alone, however much traffic it drew, couldn't sustain growth. Many uploaders with no audience would have stopped uploading.
- The frontier sits closer to the origin when moderate numbers on each side suffice, or when one side values even a few members of the other. The authors call the hard case in the figure typical in their experience.

## The three ignition strategies

- **Zigzag strategy**: push participation on all sides at the same time, not in lockstep, varying the effort between them. YouTube did this, and its summer 2005 upgrades seem aimed more at viewers than uploaders. Alibaba recruited Chinese suppliers and foreign buyers together at launch.
- **Two-step strategy**: get one group on board, then use access to it to recruit the other. OpenTable signed restaurants first. Chuck Templeton said it needed dozens of restaurants in each area, covering each major cuisine, before diners had enough to choose from. The strategy suits ad-supported platforms, which use content as bait for eyeballs and then sell those eyeballs in the well-developed advertising market. Newspapers, magazines and online publications have to invest in content before advertisers take an interest. Many online attention seekers are three-sided and combine the two: a zigzag to get eyeballs, then a step to advertisers. YouTube showed its first video ad in August 2007, 28 months after launch. Pinterest showed its first in May 2014, about 50 months after launch.
- **Commitment strategy**: essential when one group has to *invest* to take part and needs a guarantee that the other group will show up. Entering consoles, Microsoft had to convince game developers that the Xbox would have enough users. It argued that the Xbox was a great product and committed to selling it at a low price. Google's free, modifiable Android license was a commitment to handset makers and carriers who feared lock-in (see [ecosystem.md](ecosystem.md)).

## Tactics

- **Self-supply**: the platform supplies one side itself instead of waiting. Jawed Karim uploaded "Me at the zoo" and clips of airplanes taking off. Apple put its own apps on the first iPhone, with no App Store. OpenTable built its own table management software because it couldn't wait for others to. Self-supply can also last as a business, where the platform has expertise and profits from it.
- **Marquee customers / anchor tenants**: sign on participants who attract others. Malls sign high-traffic stores early, small retailers follow expecting shoppers, and the anchors and small stores together draw shoppers. Nightclubs fill opening night with "cool" people to create buzz. By New York, its third major city, OpenTable went straight for the most popular restaurants, with salespeople working down the Zagat lists.
- **"Make 'em believe"** (shaping expectations), probably the most common tactic: convince each group that if it joins, the other will be there.
  - The entrepreneur's vision or record may be enough. Max Levchin's reputation won Affirm (a consumer online transactional lending platform, launched February 2013) the capital partners and merchants that then brought consumers on board.
  - Each group may believe the proposition is so good that the other group will believe it too.
  - Some entrepreneurs stretch the truth, telling each side the other is already on board or exaggerating progress.
  - The formal version is the **conditional contract**: each member agrees to join if the platform gets enough members of the other group or groups.
- **Focus narrowly to make the market thick.** Ignition isn't a numbers game: the platform needs enough of the right participants, in the right proportions, in the same place. OpenTable signed leading restaurants in only a few cities. PayPal could have chased every online shopper and merchant. Luke Nosek's insight was that eBay was "the busiest place for person-to-person interactions on the Internet," so the quickest way to grow everywhere was "to first grow on eBay!" PayPal became the standard payment method on eBay, eBay bought it, and by Q2 2015 about 78% of PayPal's volume was off eBay. The two split on July 20, 2015. Without the early focus on eBay, ignition is doubtful.
- **Pricing as an ignition tool.** Alibaba made basic access free for businesses. OpenTable rewarded diners and charged restaurants. Companies usually need to keep the pricing they used to reach critical mass (see [pricing.md](pricing.md)).
- **Build one side's standalone value first.** OpenTable (table management software that better restaurants wanted even without reservations) and Ariba (procurement software) each spent years solving a one-sided problem before running a successful two-sided platform. See [cases.md](cases.md).

## Case: YouTube ignites

- Chad Hurley, Steven Chen and Jawed Karim started on Valentine's Day 2005, helped by digital cameras, cheaper broadband, cheap server storage and Adobe Flash 7. The first site went up April 23, 2005, and no one uploaded or watched. Many start-ups and large Internet companies were racing for the same prize.
- **Driving traffic.** The founders posted their own videos ("Me at the zoo," 18 seconds of Karim in front of elephants), emailed friends and family pleading for uploads, added a dating angle, and offered women $100 on Craigslist to upload ten videos (no one replied). In May Chen was "pretty depressed," with maybe 40 to 60 videos on the site. The June relaunch added related videos, comments, featured videos and an index. Over the summer came the key features: embedding YouTube videos on MySpace and personal blogs, and emailing links, which raised the odds that uploaders and viewers would meet. By September 2005 the site had Watch, Upload and Share buttons, an Invite Friends tab, view counts, comments and five-star ratings, and the Watch button promised "1000s of fast streaming videos." "Lazy Sunday" drew more than five million hits before copyright complaints took it down, and a Mortal Kombat theme video drew more than twenty million. A very long tail of family, funny and silly videos ("My Cat Stinky") mattered in aggregate.
- **Figure 5-1, daily reach** (daily unique visitors per million people): near zero April to September 2005, about 300 by August 2005, about 1,000 by late November, 3,000 by late December, 7,500 by late January 2006, 12,000 by late February, 14,000 by late March, 18,000 by late April, 29,000 to 30,000 by late May, 41,000 by late July, 48,000 to 50,000 by late August, and about 58,000 on September 27, 2006. Uploads rose from a few a day to 30,000 a day by May 2006. The stock of videos went from 40 to 60 in May 2005 to thousands by September 2005 to about 100 million by mid-July 2006. The growth rate jumped between November 2005 and March 2006: that is when YouTube ignited.
- Pushing uploads raised views and pushing views raised uploads. YouTube couldn't focus on one side and expect the other to follow, until momentum from the feedback did most of the work. Both sides were free. The plan was to build traffic, then sell its attention to advertisers, and video advertising took longer to take off than many expected. Google bought YouTube for $1.65 billion in October 2006, when it led all US sites in time spent.

## Fizzles

- In May 2006 five video-sharing sites had about 92% of traffic: YouTube, Google Video, Myspace Video, MSN and Yahoo. Google Video never reached critical mass and closed in 2012. The other three survive as features of portals, not as significant services.
- **Brightcove.** Jeremy Allaire, former CTO of Macromedia (the inventor of Flash), planned a one-stop shop for Internet TV linking content providers, advertisers and consumers. He ran a two-step strategy: first bring publishers on board with technology (encoding, players, a content delivery network), then open the consumer site brightcove.com. Premium publishers would not help build a consumer destination that competed with them for eyeballs and ad revenue, and Brightcove had concentrated on premium content, where that conflict was sharpest, with no user-generated content. With little content and stiff competition it couldn't reach critical mass in time, closed the consumer site in April 2008, and became a one-sided technology provider with some success. The ecosystem conflict sank the second step.
- **eBay in China** couldn't attract enough retailers and buyers to interest either side and abandoned the expansion. Taobao, which charged zero transaction fees and sold at fixed prices rather than by auction, pushed it out.
- **The US B2B exchanges** of 1995 to 2001 fizzled because the friction was too small (see [cases.md](cases.md)).

Securing ignition is "generally a race against time that most lose, though some win in spectacular fashion." For the warning signs and timeline of that race, see [evaluation.md](evaluation.md).
