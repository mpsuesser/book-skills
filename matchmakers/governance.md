# Governance: ruling bad behavior by platform participants

"Good governance is a critical component of operating a successful matchmaker." A platform is a community, like a village, city or country. Its reason for existing is to give participants a good place to meet, so it needs laws, enforcers and penalties, and may police, prosecute and punish its own customers, up to expelling them for good.

## Behavioral externalities

- An **externality** arises when people aren't paid for a benefit they give others, or aren't punished for a harm they do them. On the authors' island, planting a garden the neighbors enjoy is a positive externality, trading coconuts for bananas is no externality at all (she's paid for her effort), and shaking coconuts down noisily enough to wake the neighbors is a negative one.
- **Network externalities** come from the *fact and number* of participants: indirect ones from the other group, direct ones from the same group. **Behavioral externalities** come from participants' *behavior*: actions that directly help or harm others on the platform, such as a developer slipping malware into an app.
- Negative behavioral externalities lower a platform's value. People are less willing to join, and members hesitate to interact, so platforms have strong financial reasons to curb them. Successful platforms nurture the positive behavioral externalities and minimize the negative ones, mainly by discouraging bad behavior.
- **Governance system**: the rules that prohibit negative behavioral externalities, plus the mechanisms that enforce them, including detection and punishment.

## The catalogue of bad behavior

Everything that happens in society can happen on a platform:

- **Fake identities.** A celebrity may want privacy, but some users dislike networks where people aren't real, and disguise enables predation and harassment. Lori Drew used a fake MySpace account posing as a teenage boy to court, then turn on, Megan Meier, and suggested she kill herself. Megan committed suicide.
- **Offensive conduct**: obscene material, hate speech, plain annoyance, people lying about age or weight on dating sites, hooligans trashing an online community the way they trash a mall or nightclub.
- **Fraud and misrepresentation**: counterfeits, taking payment without shipping. Alibaba's escrow answered exactly this.
- **Hidden information and the lemons problem.** Sellers know whether a used car is a lemon and buyers can't tell, so buyers pay less for every car, owners of good cars won't sell, and the share of lemons rises. The remedy is to keep low-quality entrants out, as Apple did with App Store quality control. That takes serious investment in vetting participants and removing bad ones, and any quality threshold necessarily shrinks that side.
- **Opportunism** that may be legal but exploits others. **Front-running**: a broker with a client order big enough to push up BP's price buys BP for its own account first, places the client order, then sells into the rise the client caused, so the client pays more. All exchanges have rules and detection staff against it. **Drip pricing**: an online merchant adds a surcharge for paying by card after the buyer has entered card details, betting the buyer is too far along to cancel. Card networks historically banned surcharges, and drip pricing appeared in the UK and Australia once regulators required networks to allow them.
- **Bodily harm.** Sexual predators have used Craigslist to meet victims. BlaBlaCar (20 million members in nineteen countries outside the US) puts strangers in cars together, so it verifies identities, requires photos, has drivers and passengers rate each other after every trip, and offers a "ladies only" option for women who want to travel only with women.

## What platforms can do that governments can't, and vice versa

- Platforms lack warrants, interrogation and jail. What they have is speed: the City of Los Angeles took years to jail Lindsay Lohan for more than a day despite repeated arrests, while Facebook simply disabled her account (late 2008, for posting under an assumed name).
- Where the law is silent or ineffective, the platform is the only recourse (Missouri had no law against what Lori Drew did), and weak law creates an opportunity for platforms to create value by devising and enforcing rules.
- **The London Stock Exchange.** In the early 1700s English courts treated options and futures as gambling and wouldn't enforce them, and Sir John Barnard's Act (1734) made them illegal, though there was little prosecution. Trade went on through the century, which couldn't have happened had fraud been rampant. In Ranald Michie's history it was left to market participants "to create a code of conduct that enforced the conditions necessary for trade." At Jonathan's Coffee House, brokers who reneged were at first thrown out bodily. Once the room grew too crowded to watch the door, their names went up on a highly visible blackboard. After several models, the London Stock Exchange opened on March 3, 1801, to members paying a significant fee, with rules monitored and judged by a committee with full-time staff and "enforced by the threat of expulsion from the market." Most rules built trust between members about payment and delivery. Exchanges have kept such rules ever since, against members who fail their obligations or manipulate prices.
- Platforms make rules even where courts are available, and some write them in great detail. BlaBlaCar's "Terms and Conditions" are punishable by suspension or termination. eBay's "Rules & policies" exist "to create a safe and fair environment for all eBay members". Everyone is barred from profanity, misrepresenting identity, and closing deals outside eBay. Buyers must pay for what they commit to buy, may not bid without intending to buy, may retract bids only in specific circumstances, and may not bid up a price for a seller. Sellers sign a contract: deliver what they sell, post no false or misleading content, don't manipulate prices, and don't sell pornography, firearms or counterfeits.

## Enforcement tools

- **Guard the gate.** Screen participants before they join. Apple's App Store, effectively the only route to iPhone users, rejects an app that isn't "useful, unique or provide some form of lasting entertainment," or that is "plain creepy." It names the externality: "We have lots of serious developers who don't want their quality Apps to be surrounded by amateur hour." Cheating (tricking review, stealing user data, copying others' work, manipulating ratings) means removal and expulsion from the developer program, and Apple pulls apps later if they break the rules. One app submitter found the top rejection reason was "your content is boring" and the second "your design/layout needs a facelift."
- **Ratings.** Five-star rating systems are common and seem effective. Uber and BlaBlaCar passengers and drivers rate each other, and either can decline a low-rated counterpart, which gives everyone an incentive to follow the rules, serve well and behave.
- **The bouncer.** Ejection is the ultimate threat behind every rule, and the bouncer may be software rather than brawn. Nightclub bouncers throw out people who grab women, pull knives or throw bar stools, and keep undesirables out. OpenTable's "no show" policy requires diners to cancel at least thirty minutes ahead, and its terms say it monitors cancellations and closes the account after more than four no-shows in twelve months. That holds even for diners who are valuable enough that OpenTable pays them rewards.
- **Sanctions short of exile.** Google bans "link farms" (charging sites for links) and "doorway" pages that trick crawlers into counting keywords a site doesn't really have. In 2011 it found JCPenney planting keywords on thousands of sites that linked back to jcpenney.com, and manually demoted the company for about ninety days. JCPenney fell from the first result for "Samsonite carry on luggage" to the seventh page, which in practice took it off the search engine. Web businesses call this "Google Jail."

## Case: Friendsters, fakesters and fraudsters

The right rules aren't always obvious, and the back-to-back fall of the two largest US social networks shows the cost of getting them wrong.

- **Friendster** launched in March 2002 and had more than three million users by November 2003. Jonathan Abrams found dating sites "too anonymous and creepy" ("with JDate, a guy is almost bound to be twenty pounds heavier or twenty years older than he is in his photo") and aimed at accountability by linking friends of friends. He hadn't anticipated **fakesters**, invented personas for celebrities, fictional characters, objects, places, animals and more. They made serious networking hard, and some, such as Tila Tequila with her provocative photos, drew enough traffic to overload Friendster's limited servers. Friendster banished them, the fakesters organized to reinsert their profiles and took revenge with fraudsters posing as real people, and growth slowed sharply.
- **MySpace**, launched in August 2003, welcomed the fakesters, and Tila Tequila moved over. It overtook Friendster and became the largest social network of the mid-2000s, but its "Everything goes!" policy, with no push for reliable information, drew minors lying about their age, the predators who sought them, and pages of nudity, obscenity and crude content. It earned the label "vortex of perversion." Many users liked the risqué tone, but advertisers, its main source of revenue, wouldn't put their brands beside sordid content. MySpace fell back on ad networks, whose seedy ads deepened the reputation.
- **Facebook**, started in February 2004 by and for college students, adopted strict rules. It required real identities and at first limited access to ".edu" addresses, starting with harvard.edu, then selected groups such as businesses with identifiable email. When it opened to everyone in September 2006 it had 500 regional networks tied to known organizations. By the time it had to start deleting violators like Lohan, virtually everyone on it was real, and it had set the **vibe** of a network for real people keeping up with real friends. Its terms ban bullying, intimidation and harassment, and content that is hateful, threatening or pornographic, incites violence, or contains nudity or graphic violence. User Operations (*Newsweek*'s "porn cops") reviews everything users flag through "report this" links, keeps a blacklist of forbidden profile names (Batman, for one), enforces a "real-name culture," and even acts on a reported "jerk." Around late 2008 about 150 employees, almost a fifth of the workforce, policed content. Facebook had 1.55 billion monthly active users as of September 30, 2015.
- **Verdict.** Friendster had the right idea but didn't put enough effort into designing a system for making and enforcing rules. MySpace thought rules didn't matter and was wrong: users were happy but advertisers weren't, and that strangled the money side. Facebook still sees bad behavior, as every community does, but built rules and an enforcement division to keep the place as nice as possible.
