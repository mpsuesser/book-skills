# Fizzle or sizzle: vetting a new matchmaker

Starting a mall or a magazine is fairly cut and dried, because so many have been started that the road map is known. A **pioneering platform**, the first or one of the first to spot a friction and build a matchmaker for it, has no one to copy on the chicken-and-egg problem, the pricing balance or the externality problems. There is no step-by-step guide, but there is a checklist. It serves the founder, and anyone the founder must persuade: family, co-founders, employees whose jobs ride on the venture, the participants asked to invest in joining, and, for a pioneer inside a big company, senior management and the board (Microsoft staff had to persuade Bill Gates to back the Xbox). Anyone on the receiving end of a pioneer's pitch should ask tough questions, because most multisided platforms don't take off.

## The six questions, with what lies behind each

1. **What's the friction, how big is it, and who benefits from solving it?** This is the first and most important task. The bigger the friction, the more value the platform can create, the easier it is to get participants on board, and the better its chance of making money. Knowing which participants gain most from removing the friction guides both ignition strategy and pricing. Work out what friction the platform addresses, how much of it the platform can eliminate, how much value that creates, and whether that is enough to ignite a sustainable, profitable business. Some frictions are too small. Americans pay by card in a few seconds, so shaving perhaps a second off with a phone excites few people, and several well-financed mobile-payment launches failed. There are two kinds of opportunity:
   - **A clear existing friction drastically reduced.** People already made and took reservations before OpenTable. The question is whether the friction is big enough to earn revenue that covers launching and running the platform.
   - **A new way to interact** that nobody saw because it was impossible. Hardly anyone shared videos before YouTube, so YouTube couldn't know how much value it would create. It turned out people badly wanted to share videos once they could.

   The biggest mistake is the **"if you build it, they will come" fallacy**. They won't, unless the platform solves a big problem for participants and the entrepreneur cracks the chicken-and-egg problem. "Without a significant friction to address, no matter how great a platform's technology, there is no case for investing."
2. **Does the platform design reduce this friction, balance the interests of participants on all sides, and do it better than other entrants?** The answer is usually no. Examine how the design actually reduces the friction. Start-ups and giants alike saw the online-video friction at the same moment. Few survived, and the survivors are puny next to YouTube. They saw the friction but lacked a great way to reduce it: bad designs, the wrong pricing structure, or a failure to work with the ecosystem.
3. **How hard is the ignition problem, and does the entrepreneur have a solid plan for achieving critical mass?** A design that *would* create value if people joined still has to reach critical mass. Until the right participants join, the platform is a dud, and a dud's reputation is hard to repair, especially with impatient investors. Novices underestimate this. The entrepreneur needs a realistic view and specific tactics for getting enough of the right participants, in the right proportions, before the money runs out or the platform's reputation is trashed. See [ignition.md](ignition.md).
4. **Do the prices necessary for ignition and growth enable the platform to make money?** This ties back to the friction. Enough value means a pie big enough to recruit participants, fund subsidies for the most desirable (marquee) participants, and make money. If one side needs heavy subsidies, the entrepreneur should be able to explain why the other side will pay enough to cover them and a profit, and why those high prices won't stunt the platform's growth. This is easier for ad-supported platforms, where a developed market sells access to viewers, and the founder needs only a convincing story about how ad revenue will cover the content that attracts them. See [pricing.md](pricing.md).
5. **How is the matchmaker going to work with others in the broader ecosystem, does it face related risks, and has it dealt with them?** Look outside the platform's walls for parties the platform and its participants depend on, and for any who could block ignition or growth. Apple and Google both knew their smartphone platforms depended on mobile carriers and planned for them. Brightcove abandoned its multisided video platform partly because it hadn't factored in how the media ecosystem would hamper ignition. See [ecosystem.md](ecosystem.md).
6. **Is the entrepreneur ready to modify her design and ignition strategy quickly in response to market reactions?** This matters more for matchmakers, which must balance on several dimensions at once. Plans that made sense at the start need adjusting once there is evidence of how participants actually behave. When restaurants balked at buying OpenTable's system, Chuck Templeton quickly switched to a small installation fee plus monthly rent, and restaurants signed up. Like a skateboard rider, the pioneer adjusts constantly to the terrain.

## Warning signs after launch

- **The countdown.** After launch, the platform either reaches critical mass before time runs out and ignites, or it implodes. There's no standard time limit, but in the authors' experience platforms often make it in the first couple of years or never do. Participants stop coming when the platform doesn't deliver, and then new participants, returning participants and investors all become hard to win.
- **Dead in the water at the outset** (fairly easy to diagnose, though the cure may not be obvious):
  - The platform can't attract participants to one side or both, not even the early adopters who will try almost anything. YouTube's quiet launch led the founders to redesign the site.
  - Marquee players essential to critical mass won't join. OpenTable's investors should have slept better once the most popular restaurants in a city signed on, and worried greatly if they wouldn't.
- **Once activity starts** (harder: is growth fast enough to beat the clock?):
  - Participants on both sides, and activity on the platform, aren't accelerating.
  - Early adopters are defecting because they've decided there isn't enough value to stay.
  - Deals with marquee players aren't closing.
  - No momentum is building.
- **The inflection point.** After a year or two, most matchmakers should see a sharp acceleration that feels like taking off. A platform still struggling for critical mass by then risks an imminent fizzle. After almost three years and heavy cash burn, OpenTable's investors radically and successfully changed its ignition strategy, from "grabbing all the eyeballs" (sign lots of restaurants) to "go narrow/go deep" (build thick markets in a few cities first). More often, a platform that hasn't ignited after a couple of years implodes as participants leave, or loses its funding as investors doubt it will ever work. eBay's China expansion is an example.

## Questions for understanding any matchmaker

Investors shouldn't put money in without answers, or at least confidence that good answers will emerge. An entrepreneur shouldn't start without plausible answers to all of them, and needs the right answers early, even though the best ones only come after launch, from how participants respond:

- Who's participating in the platform, and how does it create value for them?
- How is it designed to promote interactions among them?
- How does it use prices to balance participation, and does it have a subsidy side?
- Does it have rules and standards, and how do they affect its ability to create value?
- How does it encourage every part of the broader ecosystem that affects its ability to deliver value?
- How did, or will, it solve the chicken-and-egg puzzle and ignite?

## Worked case: Apple Pay, one year in

**The launch.** On September 9, 2014, Tim Cook announced an "entirely new category of service" against an image of a fat wallet ("Our vision is to replace this"). He said Americans "scramble" for their cards about 200 million times a day in an antiquated mag-stripe process. Apple turned the service on on October 20, 2014. At the start of that day no consumer had Apple Pay, and most merchants lacked terminals that could take it: a textbook chicken-and-egg start. Apple knew only that earlier US mobile-payment efforts, including Google's and a joint venture of three of the four largest carriers, were struggling.

**Organizing the ecosystem (well done).** The US card system in 2014: the average adult had 2.4 cards, used more than 200 million times a day for $4 trillion a year. There were three groups of businesses. First, four networks: MasterCard and Visa work through banks, while American Express and Discover issue cards and mostly serve consumers and merchants directly. Second, more than ten thousand banks, savings banks and credit unions, which issue nearly all debit cards, with the larger ones issuing credit cards too. Third, merchant service firms such as First Data and Vantiv, which supply terminals and processing. All the major networks except Discover were on board at launch. Users loaded cards by photographing them, banks paid Apple 0.15% of transaction value (15 cents per $100), and banks and networks promoted the service. They backed it for three reasons: they expected convenient mobile payment to move people from cash to cards, they feared rival mobile schemes that bypassed the card networks, and banks, seen as boring and blamed for the financial crisis, liked being associated with Apple's cool brand.

**The numbers.**

- **Consumer side.** Apple Pay needed an iPhone 6, whose near-field communication (NFC) chip lets a phone pay with a wave. About 29 million Americans had an iPhone 6 or 6 Plus by the end of 2014, and more than 70 million by September 2015: about 15%, then 35%, of Americans aged 18 to 65.
- **Merchant side at launch.** The top 100 retailers and restaurants account for 36.5% of retail sales, and only 10 of them had terminals that took Apple Pay. Those 10 made 10.7% of top-100 sales, which is 3.9% of all retail sales. Smaller merchants, making the other 63.5% of sales, were less likely to have NFC, and mom-and-pops least of all. A payments executive in early fall 2014 said: "The truth is, today, no one uses NFC." A reporter found that Best Buy, Walmart, Dollar Tree, the Post Office, Safeway, Taco Bell and others didn't take it.
- **Strategy.** Apple went **"go broad/go shallow"**: national coverage from day one, but in any given area few consumers (new iPhone required) and few merchants (new terminal required) could use it. That made many thin markets and no thick ones, the opposite of OpenTable's go narrow/go deep.
- **Black Friday, November 28, 2014.** Only 9.1% of iPhone 6 users had installed Apple Pay, and only 50.5% of those used it at a terminal that accepted it. So in a typical transaction at an accepting terminal, about one iPhone 6 user in twenty (4.6%) used it. Lack of signage wasn't the cause, since accepting terminals usually showed the Apple Pay logo. Those who tried it loved it: 52.6% found it amazing and easy (of whom 42.1% would use it "every chance I get" and 35.1% "when I remember that I can"), 14% found it confusing, and 1.8% found it difficult.
- **October 2015.** Only 16.6% of iPhone 6 owners had ever tried Apple Pay, and the share using it when they could fell to 30.7%. Out of 100 users, about 17 had tried it and about 5 used it when they could. Because more people owned the phone, users with Apple Pay probably rose from about 2.7 million to 11.7 million, and the chance that an American adult used it at an accepting terminal went from 0.7% to 1.8%.
- **Merchant side a year on.** Card networks had shifted fraud liability to merchants who lacked chip-and-pin (EMV) terminals by October 1, 2015, and EMV terminals can generally take contactless payments once NFC is switched on. But installing them and redesigning systems is costly, dipping a card is slower than swiping, many merchants judged the cost of fraud lower and didn't upgrade, and many who did upgrade left NFC off. Apple may have bet that consumer demand would tip merchants. It didn't: 23 of the top 100 had NFC, making 19.8% of top-100 sales, which is 7.2% (0.365 × 0.198) of all retail and restaurant sales. Probably about 10% of retail sales passed through Apple Pay-capable terminals, and Apple Pay was about 1.8% of value at those terminals, so about 0.18% (18 out of 10,000) of card transactions at retail stores after a year.

**The diagnosis, from Cook's own two videos.**

1. **Friction (question 1).** The "before" video staged a woman digging for her card, a clerk asking for ID, and a second swipe. Most Americans don't live that: they know where their cards are, are seldom asked for ID (especially with a PIN debit card), and small purchases need no PIN or signature. Shaving perhaps a second off paying isn't enough to excite people. Apple Pay doesn't remove a significant friction for most card users or merchants.
2. **Design against ignition (questions 2 and 3).** The "after" video showed why the chicken-and-egg problem was hard. The shopper needed not just a smartphone, not just an iPhone, but a *new* iPhone 6, so about 65% of American adults still couldn't use Apple Pay almost a year after launch. The store needed new terminals, so Apple Pay wasn't usable at 77% of the hundred largest retailers, and at an even higher share of smaller ones. Choosing NFC meant both sides needed new technology. Too few devoted users emerged to push merchants: one large retailer said in June 2015 it hadn't adopted Apple Pay "because not even a 'small percentage' of its customers have asked for it." The positive feedback between sides never got going.

**The contrast: Starbucks (2011).** Its app showed a QR code holding the customer's payment credentials, worked on all major smartphones, and was read by scanners that virtually every Starbucks already had. More than 2% of Starbucks transactions went through it by the end of its first year, and 16% by 2014.

**Options and verdict.** Apple Pay could still ignite as merchants gradually install NFC-capable terminals (they still have to switch NFC on) and iPhone owners replace old phones, until feedback effects kick in. Apple could also change strategy: add features that address bigger frictions than paying at the counter, give retailers incentives to make NFC available, or move to QR codes or another approach more merchants can accept. Samsung Pay (on Samsung 6 phones as of September 2015) works at old mag-stripe terminals. Unlike most pioneers, Apple has the money and reputation to survive a slow ignition, and OpenTable took about six years with patient investors. But a rival could ignite fast. The authors' verdict: as investors, "we wouldn't necessarily write it off, but we would be quite concerned," and other mobile-payment entrepreneurs shouldn't give up. "No multisided platform is guaranteed real success, even with a big bank account and persistence, if it isn't solving a big enough problem." The flip side is M-PESA, which removed a huge friction and ignited fast (see [cases.md](cases.md)).
