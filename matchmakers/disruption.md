# Creation, destruction, and transformation

## Turbocharged matchmakers and the gale of creative destruction

- A **turbocharged matchmaker** benefits from a significant combination of the six technologies: powerful chips, the Internet, the web, broadband, programming languages and operating systems, and the Cloud (details in [ecosystem.md](ecosystem.md)). These raise the power and reach of the indirect network effects that drive every matchmaker. Matchmakers already grow fast once they solve ignition, and the technologies give them an ever-growing source of dynamism. Airbnb benefits from all six.
- Joseph Schumpeter (1942) described the "perennial gale of creative destruction." Turbocharged matchmakers are now behind that gale worldwide. On November 20, 2015, Apple had the highest market cap in the world ($672 billion) and Google the second highest, Uber was the most valuable unicorn at $51 billion, and seven of the ten most valuable unicorns were matchmakers.
- **Value without destruction.** OpenTable made white-tablecloth restaurants more efficient (less labor for reservations, fewer empty tables) without destroying any businesses, beyond a few reservation takers and fewer pencils and notebooks.
- **Destruction.**
  - *Newspapers.* Ad-supported sites such as Yahoo drew readers away, so advertisers spent less. Craigslist (which spends nothing on content) and Monster took the profitable classifieds. US print newspaper ad revenue fell from $47.4 billion in 2005 to $20.2 billion in 2013, a 57% drop. Less revenue meant less content and higher reader prices, which meant fewer readers and fewer advertisers: a death spiral for many. Average daily circulation fell 31% from 1995 to 2014 (58.2 million to 40.4 million), the number of dailies fell by 280 (17%) from 1990 to 2014, and newsroom employment fell 42%.
  - *Travel agents.* Expedia and other two-sided matchmakers run from the Cloud offer cheaper, more efficient alternatives. Travel agents per hundred thousand Americans fell 55%, from 44 in 2000 to 20 in 2014.
  - *Taxis.* Uber, Lyft and Didi Kuaidi created a great deal of value for riders and drivers. Taxi drivers protest worldwide, and New York City medallion prices fell 23% from 2013 to 2015. Without a successful fight, the traditional taxi business will likely go into terminal decline.
  - *Video rental, typewriters, cameras.* Video rental is covered below. Word-processing software on PC operating systems, plus cheap printers, obliterated typewriters. Camera phones on smart mobile operating systems cut demand for cameras.
- Pioneering turbocharged matchmakers even displace traditional matchmakers, as online media did to newspapers.

## Why a platform threat is so dangerous, and the options

- Threats come from new matchmakers *and* from the businesses they power. The most potent threat comes from competing against the **subsidized side** of a multisided platform: online media give content to viewers free, while physical newspapers charge readers as well as advertisers.
- An incumbent facing a platform threat is hit at once with "an improved way of doing things, a radically different pricing model, indirect network effects they can't duplicate, and sometimes a global reach" it couldn't obtain or match on its own.
- **Choices**, like a householder in a hurricane's path: build to withstand the wind, board up the windows, leave until the storm passes, sell and move, or stay and live for the moment. Sometimes the best a business can do, unappealing as it is, is to **milk its assets and prepare for an inevitable exit**. The Yellow Pages publishers lacked the assets and skills to take on search engines.
- **A successful pivot: American Express.** In 1950 AmEx was best known for Travelers Cheques (introduced around the turn of the century): accepted by most US merchants, exchangeable for foreign currency abroad, and guaranteed against loss or theft. Diners Club's charge card, more convenient for consumers and merchants, grew explosively among the same travel and entertainment merchants. AmEx launched its own charge card in 1958 and succeeded because it knew the payments business, already had consumers and merchants, and had travel offices in major cities abroad.
- **New platform ideas as opportunities for incumbents.** In the 1950s and early 1960s many US banks started card schemes and couldn't reach critical mass, partly because regulation kept them from operating nationally or even statewide. They couldn't compete with American Express, which wasn't a bank. In 1966 the MasterCard and Visa national networks gave any bank that issued cards access to every merchant taking the brand, and any bank that signed merchants access to every cardholder. The networks ignited, and even small banks could run profitable card programs.
- There is no simple survival guide. Responses differ by category (jewelry, electronics, groceries), by size, and between single-sided stores and two-sided malls.

## Case: retail ("Gone Missing")

- **The symptom.** In December 2014, Boston's high-end Newbury Street, Copley Place and Saks weren't crowded. RetailNext's sensors showed visits down 8% from 2013 to 2014. The US had 894,000 retail stores selling more than $3.6 trillion in 2014. More than 125 chains announced over 6,400 closures in 2014: Sears closed 339 stores including its Chicago flagship, Delia's closed all of its roughly 100 stores, and a bankrupt RadioShack, whose 5,000 stores had been within minutes of 90% of Americans, was closing almost 2,000. Luxury malls like Aventura still drew crowds while many down-market ones emptied. Best Buy rented out excess space and cut new stores by 20%.
- **The case of the missing shopper.** Shoppers didn't all go online: physical stores still had 93.6% of retail sales in 2014, down only 2 points from 95.5% in 2010. Nor did they buy less: per-capita spending in physical stores rose 2.2%, from $13,299 to $13,595, between 2013 and 2014. What changed is that they visit fewer stores, five per mall trip in 2007 against three in 2014, because they no longer need to walk around to see products and compare prices. They search and compare online (eBay, Amazon, retailers' sites, Google, Yelp, cnet.com, consumerreports.org, Facebook and Pinterest reviews), and on the trip itself they use apps (AroundMe for where to shop, store notifications, PriceGrabber for prices, GPS and maps), so there are fewer window shoppers and fewer drop-ins by passers-by. Adult smartphone ownership rose from 35% in 2011 to 68% in 2015, which put search and discovery anywhere, including mid-trip.
- **Canary in the coal mine.** The quiet aisles signal that "traditional brick-and-mortar stores may not be in danger of extinction, but the businesses that run them are." Stores were designed for browsing, discovery, purchase and carrying goods home, and that set their size, layout, marketing and prices. Those practices make less sense when a shopper just wants to pay and pick up efficiently. Warehouse clubs and supercenters have gained steadily since about 2000.
- **The three waves of retail disruption**, which go well beyond a "sexy matchmaker kills boring single-sided business" story:
  1. **Fixed broadband providers**, the foundational platforms linking households and content providers, which enabled matchmakers such as Google and eBay and also single-sided online stores such as Walmart's, which are apps on those platforms.
  2. **Mobile broadband and smartphones** with sophisticated operating systems, giving access almost anywhere and anytime, and a foundation for discovery apps, many of them multisided (Groupon's app connects advertisers and consumers).
  3. **The integration of online and offline**, just beginning: GPS-aware apps that work in physical space, and stores installing wireless devices that track shoppers as they enter and move around.

  Behind every change in how people shop, and every retailer response, "you will find a multisided platform or an app running on one."
- **Extinction: video rental.** There were 18,739 US video rental stores in 2005, and 83% were gone by 2013. Blockbuster closed its last 300 US stores in early 2014 without even trying to pivot. Netflix's mail-order DVDs (more than 55,000 titles by 2005, with search and recommendations stores couldn't match) cut into sales, and streaming was the fatal blow: instant, no returns, running on browsers, mobile apps, Internet TVs and Roku. In 2000 Blockbuster's executives "just about laughed us out of their office" when Netflix proposed a partnership (Barry McCarthy, Netflix's CFO at the time). In 2001 Reed Hastings told *Billboard* he expected a robust streaming catalog within a decade. Netflix used its mail business to bide its time and work out the technical and business problems while broadband improved, launched streaming in 2007, and had more than 44 million streaming subscribers by 2014. Blockbuster's scale and knowledge of its customers might have carried it across. Like many incumbents, it may have lacked the technical and business skills, or been too wedded to what had made it successful. Small stores couldn't pivot to capital-intensive national streaming. Some diversified into coffee or party supplies, and some carried on (an independent owner told *Time* that people were tired of having everything fed to them at home). His odds of survival don't look good.
- **Death spiral: malls.** Owings Mills Mall near Baltimore lost its draw, Boscov's, so fewer shoppers came, which made it less attractive to smaller stores and then to shoppers, and losing Lord & Taylor and Sears repeated the cycle. By the Sunday before Christmas 2014 the *New York Times* found few visitors peering through locked gates at the vacant spaces of former tenants such as H&M, Wet Seal and Kay Jewelers. "It's depressing," one shopper said. About 1,500 enclosed malls were built from 1956 to 2006, and none opened from 2007 to 2013. Positive feedback magnifies small changes in both directions, so a fall in foot traffic or one departing store can start a downward spiral. The result is common enough to have a name, "a dead mall," and a virtual cemetery, deadmalls.com. In 2014 about one mall in five had vacancy of 10% or more (a signal of trouble), and more than 3% had vacancy of 40% or more (a sign of impending death). Once a mall is in a death spiral there isn't much hope.
- **Reinvention: omnichannel.** Stores won't go the way of the typewriter. In A.T. Kearney's 2014 survey of 2,500 shoppers, many still preferred stores. Even among millennials, the most online group, about 40% preferred stores for looking for things, more than 70% for trying them and more than 60% for buying them. Retailers are integrating physical and online channels ("omnichannel" retailing, which the authors suspect will go back to being called just "retailing").
  - *Macy's* (150 years old, fifteenth-largest US retailer in 2014) shows in-store inventory online: eight sweaters in her size and color five blocks away, to try on there or buy online. In early 2015 CEO Terry Lundgren merged the separate store and online silos into "one unified merchandising and marketing organization."
  - *Burberry*, cash-strapped in 2006, focused on millennials in their twenties, especially in emerging economies where wealthier people are younger, and reached them through digital media. Under CEO Angela Ahrendts (2006–2014) stores came to resemble the online environment and the website the stores. Associates carried iPads with "the full global collection," runway shows streamed to stores, and giant screens brought the online world inside.
  - *Westfield Group*, one of the largest mall operators, set up Westfield Labs in San Francisco. It is making malls destinations for entertainment, leisure and food, and trying pickup centers for online orders.
  - *Apple's* stores dispensed with cash registers early: associates take payment by swiping a card on an iPhone running an iOS app.
  - *Online-born retailers moving into stores*: Warby Parker (founded 2010) had twenty stores and five showrooms by late November 2015, combining "the snappy ease of online ordering with the fun and serendipity of real-shopping." Bonobos had seventeen "guideshops" in 2015 where customers try things on and order for shipping, gaining the store's try-on benefit without its inventory.

  Past success in physical retail is no guarantee of success in omnichannel. Competition may come from online-native entrepreneurs with no brick-and-mortar experience.

## "Slower and faster than you think": what history teaches

**The five messages** (they sound contradictory, but aren't):

1. Matchmakers have been around for millennia, and some belonged to the sharing economy of their day.
2. Much of what today's market darlings do is old: they use technology to improve on what matchmakers have long done.
3. What *is* pioneering is that modern information and communications technologies have turbocharged the multisided platform business model.
4. History suggests today's sharing-economy matchmakers won't be the last to make waves.
5. Turbocharged matchmakers will transform industries gradually, over decades, but in fast spurts as innovative new matchmakers rapidly emerge and displace incumbents. "Things will be both slower, and faster, than you might think."

**Old models under new engines.**

- *The sharing economy* (no news headlines used the phrase before February 2010, a hundred did in November 2015) is matchmakers pairing spare capacity (a room, a car, a lawnmower) with people who can use it. OpenTable matched empty tables in 1999, Craigslist rooms a decade before Airbnb, and newspaper classifieds rooms centuries before that. Boston Marathon runners once struggled for rooms ("I just don't know where I'm going to sleep"). More than a thousand Airbnb places were listed for the 2016 race by November 30, 2015.
- *The Athenian emporion* (by 300 BCE), a city-state-run place by the docks, matched lenders with traders. Lenders made loans repayable with interest on a voyage's safe return and forgiven on disaster, so one deal was both loan and insurance. It also matched traders with shipowners who carried several traders at once, an early sharing economy of ships' capacity.
- *Renaissance fairs* (early 1400s), city-state-sponsored mini-versions of Alibaba's 1688, offered tax concessions, suspended import bans and settled disputes. A seven-day fair in Salerno in the mid-1400s recorded 102 transactions, including two Frenchmen from Naples selling 5,000 hair combs to a German merchant. Repeated dealing built enough trust for credit, such as a debt payable on demand at any of sixteen fairs the next year.
- *Newspaper classifieds* (before 1800): "[b]y 1800 most English and American newspapers were not only supported by advertising but were the primary medium carrying it" (*Advertising Age*). The ads were unstructured at first and later sorted into categories so readers and advertisers could connect.
- *The telegraph* (1832), when a message or money had moved about a hundred miles a day on horseback. Western Union consolidated the industry by the mid-1860s and by 1880 had 9,077 US offices handling 29.2 million messages over 234,000 miles of cable. Department stores used it for inventory control. Its money-transfer "app" turned telegraph offices into CICO agents, and Western Union's model since 1871 is M-PESA's minus the phone. WeChat and WhatsApp look like small advances beside it.
- eBay resembles malls and Renaissance fairs, though it curates through ratings rather than by choosing stores. YouTube resembles the TV networks, except that it gets its content free while CBS pays for it. eHarmony, like the village matchmaker and unlike a nightclub, tries to make sure its matches suit each other.

**The end-of-history illusion.** Don't assume all the good stuff has already happened. Better or different matchmakers will come. The telegraph mattered more to the global economy than anything the Internet has yet spawned, and the Athenian emporion probably did too in its part of the world. Predictions of sudden death run early. *The Economist* foresaw "The Cashless Society" on July 9, 1966, yet half a century later Americans use cash for about 26% of transactions (about 10% of spending on things they could pay for by card). E-commerce didn't kill stores. More than 40 million newspapers were still printed daily in the US two decades after Yahoo's 1994 founding. The electricity parallel: Edison's bulb came in 1879 and the first central station in 1881, but in 1900 only 8% of urban US households had electric light and electric motors powered about 5% of factories. Twenty years later about half did. By the end of the 1920s, half of US factories were designed around distributed electric motors. Smartphones may speed things up, but turbocharged platforms will transform industries over decades rather than years.

**But some things happen fast.** M-PESA leapfrogged banking in Kenya. BlaBlaCar may grow the same way in developing countries where poor infrastructure leaves ride sharing little competition for long-distance travel. Video rental died quickly. When a turbocharged platform ignites it can grow explosively, and those in its path take little comfort from economy-wide change arriving slowly and in waves.
