# Magic Words

Words that seem interchangeable are not. Swapping one word for a near-synonym changes what the listener infers: who is in charge, how sure the speaker is, whether they listened, how they feel, whether they belong. Those inferences drive action. In the Copy Machine study, adding "because" to a request to cut in line raised compliance by over 50 percent, and a meaningless reason ("because I have to make copies") worked as well as a real one. The word did the work, not the reason. Saying you "recommend" rather than "like" something makes people 32 percent more likely to take the suggestion.

**Magic words** are the right words used at the right time. They work by leveraging the science of human behavior, so each one comes with a mechanism, and the mechanism tells you when the same word backfires. Berger groups them into six types: words that (1) activate identity and agency, (2) convey confidence, (3) ask the right questions, (4) leverage concreteness, (5) employ emotion, and (6) harness similarity (and difference).

Language plays a **dual role**: it influences the people who hear it, and it reveals the people who produce it. The six types cover influence. For reading what words reveal about a writer, a group or a society, and the text-analysis methods behind the book, read [what-language-reveals.md](what-language-reveals.md).

## Applying the book

1. **Name the outcome** the words must produce, in the listener's terms: comply, believe and follow, feel heard, open up, tell the truth, keep reading, judge the speaker competent or visionary, accept them as one of the group. For self-directed language (resisting temptation, calming nerves, getting unstuck) the listener is the user. Done when you can state one outcome per audience.
2. **Pick the moves** from the goal map below. Most texts need moves from several types.
3. **Audit the text word by word.** Content usually stays fixed; the choice is in nouns vs verbs, pronouns, tense, hedges, fillers, question form, concreteness, emotional register and style words. Done when every sentence has been checked against the chosen moves.
4. **Run each move's flip condition.** Every move in this book has a context where the opposite wins (doubt beats certainty with people who are dug in, abstract beats concrete for vision, "can't" beats "don't" for refusals, difference beats similarity where novelty is valued). Done when each change is confirmed for this context.
5. **Deliver the rewrite with each change named** in Berger's terms (for example, "turned the verb into an identity", "negative-assumption question"), so the user learns the move along with the wording.

## 1. Activate identity and agency

Words signal who's in charge, who's to blame, and what it means to engage in a particular action.

**Turn actions into identities.** Nouns are category labels, and category labels imply permanence: not what someone did, but who someone is. "Rebecca goes running" describes an action; "Fred is a runner" describes an identity. "Losing is bad. Being a loser is even worse." Because everyone wants to see themselves positively, framing an action as **an opportunity to claim a desired identity** gets people to take it, and framing a bad act as claiming an undesired identity stops them.
- "Can you help clean up the blocks?" → "Can you be a helper and clean up the blocks?" Asking preschoolers to be a "helper" raised helping by almost a third.
- Talking about "being a voter" rather than voting raised turnout by over 15 percent.
- "Don't cheat" → "Don't be a cheater" more than halved cheating. "Please don't be a litterbug." "Don't be a liar."
- "Want people to listen? Ask them to be a listener. Want them to lead? Ask them to be a leader."
- On a résumé, "hard worker" rather than "hard working"; describe colleagues as "innovators" rather than "innovative".
- For the user's own habits: "I'm a runner" rather than "I run" makes the behavior feel stable.

Flip: identity language can push people away when they doubt they fit the category, for example after setbacks or when stereotypes say they don't belong. Telling children a game involved "being scientists" rather than "doing science" reduced girls' interest.

**Change can'ts to don'ts.** "I can't" implies an external, temporary constraint (my doctor, my spouse); "I don't" implies an entrenched attitude with an internal locus of control, which makes people feel empowered. When tempted, people who practiced "I don't eat chocolate cake" picked a candy bar half as often as those who practiced "I can't" (around 75 percent of the "can't" group chose the candy). To apply: write down why you don't do the thing, choosing reasons that put you in control, and put the "I don't" statement where temptation strikes (a sticky note on the fridge or computer, a calendar invite timed for when resolve will be tested).

Flip: to turn down someone else's request, "can't" is the better word, because it puts the refusal on an external constraint and distances you from the no. Experts suggest finding a "no buddy" for this; "can't" does the same job in language. "I can't serve on the task force because my boss asked me to mentor a new hire." When the asker controls the constraint, naming it lets them decide which matters more, or move the roadblock.

**Turn shoulds into coulds.** "What should I do?" invokes the moral compass, which is right for right-versus-wrong questions (lie, cheat, steal). In **right-versus-right dilemmas** (tradeoffs between two moral imperatives) and in creative problems, should-thinking traps people weighing two imperfect options. "What could I do?" encourages divergent thinking: stepping back, considering multiple objectives, alternatives and outcomes, and relaxing assumptions about fixed functions. People who asked what they could do produced solutions that were higher quality and three times as creative. When seeking advice, ask people what they think you *could* do, not what you *should* do.

**Talk to yourself.** Self-talk, the inner voice, usually runs in the first person ("Why am I so upset?"), which keeps us inside the situation. Taking an outsider's perspective, using your own name, "you", "he" or "she" ("Why does Jane feel this way?", "Jane, you can do this. You've given a ton of speeches before."), distances us from it. Speakers who did this before a stressful five-minute-prep speech were more confident, less nervous and performed better, and saw the speech as a challenge they could meet rather than a threat. Distanced self-talk also helped people choose healthier food and focus on facts during a health scare. "You can do it!" feels more natural than "I can do it!"

**Know when to use "you" (pick your pronouns).**
- "You" draws attention and increases relevance; it acts like a stop sign. Use it where attention must be won: "5 tips you can use to save money" beats "5 tips to save money" in a crowded social feed.
- "You" also suggests responsibility, blame and work. Where attention is already there (a help page someone opened to solve a problem), it hurts: "If the printer isn't working" beats "If you can't get the printer to work"; "Space can be freed up by..." puts less work on the reader than "You can free up space by...".
- Focus on the action rather than the actor to avoid accusation: "Has the dog had dinner?" rather than "Did you feed the dog?"; "I wanted to talk, but now didn't seem like the best time" rather than "I wanted to talk but you were busy."
- "I" takes ownership. Dropping it states opinion as fact ("Dinner isn't yummy"); keeping it marks an opinion ("I don't think this is right"). "I found X" claims credit but makes the finding seem subjective; "The results show X" seems objective. Choose by how you want credit or blame assigned and how subjective or objective the claim should seem.

## 2. Convey confidence

Words convey not only facts and opinions but how certain the speaker is of them, and certainty shapes credibility. **Speaking with power** (from William O'Barr's courtroom studies) means speaking like the judges, lawyers and expert witnesses did: less formal language ("please", "yes, sir"), fewer filler words and hesitations, fewer hedges, and fewer statements turned into questions ("He was in the room, wasn't he?"). The same testimony delivered powerfully made a witness seem more credible, competent and trustworthy, and jurors awarded thousands of dollars more. When two financial advisers were equally accurate, almost three-quarters of people chose the one who made more extreme, more confident predictions. People prefer confidence even when it outstrips ability, because when someone speaks with certainty, we think they're right. Powerful speech is learned, not innate.

**Ditch the hedges.** Hedges express ambiguity, caution or indecisiveness: *in my opinion, kind of, may/might, could, I think, sort of, around, seems/seemed, it seems to me, probably, I believe, about, maybe, generally, I guess, a bit, appears, I suppose*, plus *arguably, presumably, usually*, "according to him". Hedged advice is followed less (a mechanic who isn't sure it's time to fix the engine loses the repair and maybe the customer). Most hedging is unconscious: "I think she's a great hire" makes the subjectivity unnecessarily explicit, since it is already your opinion. Hedge only on purpose. When you do want to signal uncertainty, use the right hedges: put the hedge first ("I think this is the best", which owns the opinion) rather than after ("This is the best, I think", which backs away), and personalize it ("It seems to me like this will work" persuades more than "It seems like this will work").

**Use definites** in place of hedges: *definitely, guaranteed, unambiguous, clearly, irrefutable, unquestionable, obviously, absolutely, essential, undeniable, everyone, every time*. They suggest that what is said isn't just an opinion but a truth about the world.

**Don't hesitate.** Hesitations ("uh", "um", "er", and fillers like "like", "you know", "I mean", "okay", "so") hurt even more than hedges: they make speakers seem less intelligent, less informed, less qualified and lower status. **Style trumped status**: a "student teaching assistant" who didn't hesitate was rated above a "professor" who said "um" five to seven times. Tag questions ("It's cold, isn't it?") likewise make a speaker seem unsure. Fillers come from starting to talk before knowing what to say, so: plan what to say, script answers to likely questions, and pause instead of filling. Pauses also give listeners time to process and to respond with "Yeah", "Uh-huh" or "Okay", which increases liking. A sales executive (Lindsey Samuels) whose slides were strong read her call transcript, circled every "uh", "um" and "er", cut them, and converted almost a third more prospects the next month. An occasional um is fine; it can signal thinking or that you're not done.

**Turn pasts into presents.** Past tense says something was true at a particular time, for one person: subjective and transient. Present tense says it is true now and will stay true: general, enduring, close to an objective, universal truth. Across more than a million online reviews (books, music, electronics, restaurants), present tense made opinions more helpful and persuasive. "I love that book", not "I loved that book"; the restaurant "makes" delicious tacos, the printer "does" a good job, the treatment "has" a 90 percent success rate, the diet "helps" people lose weight. When presenting results, talk about what you *find* rather than what you *found*.

**Know when to express doubt.** Persuasion has two stages: first people decide how receptive to be, then whether to update. On contentious topics, an **anti-persuasion radar** goes up, and direct, certain messages can push people further the other way. Expressing doubt about your own view (you have thought carefully but aren't completely convinced you're right) makes you less threatening, validates the other side and acknowledges nuance. "Uncertainty signals an openness to other perspectives." That raised persuasion, especially among people with strong prior beliefs. When an audience knows something is uncertain, pretending otherwise looks overconfident: acknowledging study limitations made scientists and journalists more trustworthy. Turning statements into questions invites feedback. Tentative words ("results suggest" rather than "demonstrate") are right when the goal is to stay within what is known.

## 3. Ask the right questions

Questions collect information, but they also shape how the asker is seen, steer the conversation and build bonds. They **reveal as well as solicit**: a question tells the other side what the asker knows, assumes, and how assertive they will be.

**Ask for advice.** People fear it makes them look incompetent; in fact advice-seekers are seen as *more* competent. Being asked strokes the advice-giver's ego ("Of course my opinions are valuable, so this person is smart for asking"). It works like flattery but is less overt, so it avoids flattery's suspicion of ulterior motives. Boundary: asking about things the person knows nothing about, or things you should be able to solve yourself, can backfire.

**Follow up.** Asking more questions makes people more likable and gets more second dates, but question types differ in how much they signal caring and interest:
- *Introductory* ("How are you?"): automatic, so it shows little.
- *Mirror* ("A Reuben sandwich, how about you?"): returns the favor with little effort.
- *Topic switch / non sequitur* ("I'm taking a week off to go to the mountains" → "What's your favorite movie?"): signals you weren't listening; worse than no question.
- *Follow-up*: asks the person to elaborate on what they just said (foodie → what food; worried about the project → why; can't wait for the weekend → what's planned). Follow-ups work because they signal responsiveness: you listened, understood and want to know more.

**Deflect difficulty.** For an unfair or prying direct question (prior salary, top budget, why you left, whether you have other offers, when you'll have kids) there are three responses. Answering honestly earns trust but gets exploited. Declining protects the information but costs trust (negotiators who declined were judged twice as likely to be hiding something) and keeps attention on the missing answer. **Deflecting**, answering with a related question of your own, beat both: a better deal than honesty and more trust than declining. Questions are like spotlights: a related question moves the spotlight off you while showing interest. Examples: asked your maximum price → "Is there a number you had in mind?"; asked your last salary → "Can you share a bit more detail about the salary range for this position?"; asked when you plan to have kids → "Do you have any kids?". Deflection also softens unwelcome feedback: asked whether a presentation went well → "How do you think it went?" It must stay close to the topic; an unrelated question just looks evasive.

**Avoid assumptions.** When the other side has an incentive to hide negatives (a seller, a landlord, a candidate, a patient), the phrasing decides what you learn. In a used-iPod sale where the device had frozen twice:
- General question ("What can you tell me about the iPod?"): 8 percent of sellers disclosed the problem.
- **Positive-assumption question** ("The iPod doesn't have any problems, does it?"): around 60 percent disclosed, so buyers still overpaid almost 40 percent of the time. The question presumes no problems and signals an asker who is unaware or won't push.
- **Negative-assumption question** ("What problems does it have?"): sellers were 50 percent more likely to admit problems. It signals the asker knows issues may exist and is assertive enough to pursue them. Askers paid no likability penalty.

The more aversion there is to revealing something, the more this matters. "You don't smoke, right?" makes "No" the path of least resistance. "What questions do you have?" draws out more confusion than "You don't have any questions, do you?" Ask a landlord "Have residents ever complained about the neighbors?" rather than "How are the neighbors?"

**Start safe, then build.** Deep self-disclosure creates closeness, but it is a catch-22: deep self-disclosure requires social connection, and social connection requires prior disclosure. A deep question from a stranger gets an excuse or a surface answer. The fix is sustained, escalating and reciprocal self-disclosure: start with safe, easy questions that still give a small window into who someone is (a **microrevelation**), have both people answer each one, and build toward more probing ones. "Many people are willing to go second, but few are willing to be first", so structure the exchange so that both take turns. Arthur and Elaine Aron's 36-question procedure (often called **Fast Friends**) does this in about 45 minutes and works even between people with different values or politics. For the full protocol and all 36 questions, read [fast-friends-questions.md](fast-friends-questions.md).

## 4. Leverage concreteness

**Linguistic concreteness** is how specific, tangible and imaginable words are: things you can see, touch and picture (a table, jeans, "those lime green Nikes") versus intangible concepts (democracy, "a digital transformation", "something").

**Make people feel heard.** Feeling heard takes three things: the other person attended, understood, and *demonstrated* that they listened. The third is what concrete language supplies, since only someone who understood the specifics can echo them. "I'll go search for that T-shirt in gray" beats "I'll go look for that". Customer-service agents who used more concrete language ("your money back" rather than "a refund" or "something"; "at your door" rather than "there") had more satisfied customers, and customers spent 30 percent more in the following weeks after concrete email replies. Stock phrases ("I'd be happy to help with that") fit every situation and therefore prove nothing. To a partner's bad day, answer "I can't believe the vice president showed up forty-five minutes late" rather than "That must have been tough". The concrete detail must be relevant to what they said; irrelevant specifics lower satisfaction.

**Make the abstract concrete.** The **curse of knowledge**: "the more we know, the more we assume others know, and thus the more we end up communicating in ways that are tough to understand. And abstractness is the cause." Experts drift into acronyms, lingo and abstraction: solving problems becomes "ideation", why someone should buy becomes "identifying a value proposition", employees become "human capital", exercising more becomes "lifestyle modifications". Concrete language is easier to understand and remember ("rusty engine" sticks better than "available knowledge"), makes help pages more helpful ("How to split and move the keyboard"), holds attention, and even makes apologies work (prisoners who gave more concrete explanations of their transgressions were more likely to get parole). Swaps: pants → jeans; refund → money back; furniture → table; that → T-shirt; really → mouthwateringly; nicely → warmly; go → walk; solve → fix; device → phone. Prefer verbs that describe actions (walk, talk, help, improve) over adjectives (honest, aggressive, helpful).

**Know when it's better to be abstract.** Concrete language describes the tangible here and now; abstract language gets at the bigger picture. When the listener is judging *potential* rather than trying to understand, abstraction wins. In a year of funding applications to a venture firm, more abstract pitches made investors see more growth potential and scalability, and were more likely to pass the first round. Uber as "a smartphone app that makes it easier to get a taxi" is concrete; the cofounder's "a transportation solution that is convenient, reliable, and readily accessible to everyone" makes the market seem far larger. Abstract language also signals power and leadership: describing ignoring someone as "showing dislike" rather than "not saying hello", or a product as "nutritious" rather than "containing lots of vitamins", made speakers seem more fit to manage or lead. (Founders who pitch only the business as it exists today, concretely, are at a disadvantage: "I see men pitch unicorns and women pitch businesses.")

**Focus on the how or the why.** To become more concrete, ask *how*: how the product meets the need, how the initiative will be carried out (feasibility). To become more abstract, ask *why*: why it meets the need, why it matters (desirability).

## 5. Employ emotion

**Highlight the hurdles (the pratfall effect).** Mistakes are neither good nor bad; context decides. A highly competent person who makes a small blunder becomes more likable, because flawless people are hard to relate to and a slip humanizes them. An incompetent person's blunder only confirms the bad impression. So once competence is established (a second interview, a manager with a track record), revealing past shortcomings and failures makes people like and relate to us more, and gives others hope. Keep the slip relatively minor; a mistake central to the job counts against you.

**Build a roller coaster.** Endless positivity is boring ("a great life insurance commercial"). Stories need tension: across tens of thousands of movies, those that repeatedly moved from the lowest emotional lows to the highest highs and back again were liked more. Low points make high points more powerful: "Victories are savored more when they're snatched from the jaws of defeat." A founder who sold a company fast for $100 million is less interesting than one who was turned down by 279 retailers before the 280th said yes. When telling your own story or interviewing someone (as Guy Raz does on *How I Built This*), dig into the failures, money lost, dead ends and rejections, not just the highlight reel.

**Mix up moments.** People adapt to steady states (**hedonic adaptation**: lottery winners end up no happier; the tenth chocolate chip is less pleasurable than the first). Interruptions reset adaptation: shows are more enjoyable when broken up by commercials. In stories, **volatility**, moment-to-moment unpredictability about whether things will get better or worse, makes the ride more stimulating. A bumpy trajectory beats a smooth one with the same peaks.

**Consider the context: positivity versus emotionality.** Words differ not only in how positive they are but in their **emotionality**: "the degree to which they express an attitude based on feelings or emotional reactions." "Beautiful" and "best" are equally positive (both 8.4 out of 9) but differ in emotionality; "amazing" is more emotional than "perfect", "enjoyable" more than "worthwhile". High-emotionality words include *loved, enjoyable, nostalgic, hate, distressing, unhappy, terrible*; low-emotionality words include *impeccable, spotless, talented, healthy, tireless, overpriced, overcooked, clueless*. Which to use depends on the domain:
- **Hedonic** things are consumed for pleasure (music, movies, novels, vacations, flowers). Emotional language persuades: "This is an amazing place, and it was enjoyable to eat there" beat "This is a perfect place, and it was worthwhile to eat there" (65 percent chose the first restaurant). A "heartwarming" movie, an "inspiring" destination.
- **Utilitarian** things are bought to do a job (glue, gasoline, toasters, razors, software). Emotional language backfires, because it violates expectations and can lower trust. Use positive but less emotional words: a dictation app is "brilliant", not "awesome"; also "flawless", "perfect".
- One product can mix both: shoe cushioning or fuel use is utilitarian; shoe color or car design is hedonic.
- Pitching yourself: résumés and job applications are read with a utilitarian eye, so use less emotional positives (unless the company prides itself on culture or being "part of the family"). Dating profiles are hedonic, so emotionality helps.

**Connect, then solve.** In problem-solving conversations (service calls, meetings, sales), open with warmer, more emotional language to build rapport ("How can I help you?" rather than "How can I solve your issue?"), then shift to more cognitive language in the middle to solve the problem. Agents who did both had more satisfied customers who bought more. "Don't just solve. And don't just connect. Connect, then solve."

**Activate uncertainty.** **Attracting** attention is not **holding** it. Clickbait and presentation gimmicks grab attention, then disappoint. Across tens of thousands of online articles read by almost a million people, emotional language kept people reading, but emotions differed by their certainty:

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Certain | happiness, pride, excitement | anger, disgust |
| Uncertain | surprise, hope | anxiety, surprise |

Uncertain emotions (anxiety, surprise) kept readers reading, because they wanted to resolve what they didn't know; certain ones (disgust) drove them away. People were 30 percent more likely to finish an article that made them anxious than one that made them sad. (Sadness can go either way: certain when a dog dies, uncertain when it is sick.) Making people feel proud or happy may make them *less* likely to listen to whatever comes next. Open a curiosity gap instead: leave the outcome up in the air. Facts alone lull audiences, so use emotional language to make them care. "Style compensates for topic": even a dry subject can hold attention with the right words.

## 6. Harness similarity (and difference)

Every text has **content** (what is said) and **linguistic style** (how it is said), carried largely by small, nearly invisible style words: pronouns ("we", "I"), articles ("a", "the"), prepositions ("in", "to"). Communities develop shared lingo and style ("pivoting", "omnichannel"; on a beer site, "S" for smell and more fruity words), and **linguistic similarity**, how closely someone's style matches others', predicts how they fare.

**Signal similarity** when fitting in, familiarity and safety are valued, as in most workplaces. Across five years and more than 10 million emails at one firm, employees whose linguistic style matched their coworkers' were three times more likely to be promoted, got better reviews and bigger bonuses; dissimilar employees were four times more likely to be fired. **Adaptability mattered more than initial fit**: most new hires converged within a year; those later fired never adapted; those who later quit had converged early, then drifted away. "Fit isn't something we have to be born with, we just have to be willing to adapt over time." Pay attention to how colleagues use words and adopt some of their mannerisms. Similar language also predicts second dates, friendships, couples still together three months later, and better negotiations, because matching requires listening and signals the same tribe.

**Drive difference** when creativity, innovation or stimulation is valued. People have a drive for novelty; distinctive phrasing is more memorable ("May the Force be with you"). Songs whose lyrics were more atypical for their genre were more popular, even controlling for artist fame and airplay, and the same song did better on the chart where it was more atypical. "Old Town Road" mixed country tropes (horses, banjo) with Porsches, a Gucci hat and 808s: "Too rap to be a country tune and too country to be a rap song." Atypical movies do better too, and *Hamilton* was a hit partly because its style diverged from what theatergoers were used to. Pop music reverses it, because pop is a domain where familiarity is valued. The strongest mix keeps familiar markers so the audience can place the work, with different content: "Similar enough to evoke the warm glow of familiarity, but different enough to feel exciting and novel."

The test: "Similarity feels familiar and safe but can also be boring. Difference can be exciting and stimulating but can also be risky." Ask which the context values.

**Plot the right progression.** Similarity also applies between parts of the same piece. A story's **speed** is how far it jumps semantically between consecutive parts (wedding scene to more of the wedding is slow; wedding to alien invasion is fast).
- *To entertain*: faster stories are liked more overall, but not at the start. Begin slowly, so the audience can take in characters and setting (folktales repeat: three little pigs; jokes use the **rule of three**, or comic triple), then pick up speed; by the end, faster is better.
- *To inform*: speed hurts (academic papers that moved fast did worse). The book's summary: "slowing down but covering more ground". Returning to key concepts repeatedly, with deepening layers of complexity or in different applications (**circuitousness**), helped papers and increases learning.
- **Volume**, how much ground a piece covers overall, helped movies but hurt TV shows, which people watch as a quick diversion.

## Praise the process, not the person

From the epilogue: praising ability ("You must be smart at these problems") implies performance comes from a stable trait. After a later failure, children praised this way solved fewer problems, enjoyed them less and gave up sooner, because failure now meant they weren't smart. Praising the process ("You must have worked hard at these problems"; "Great job, you must have worked really hard!"; "You really studied, and your improvement shows it") kept them motivated and interested in learning. A setback then reads as a misstep and a reason to work harder. Telling someone they're smart, good at math or a great presenter is ability praise.
