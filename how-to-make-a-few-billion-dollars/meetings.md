# Run electric meetings

Jacobs's method for internal meetings, operating reviews, board meetings and public appearances. Boring meetings (rehearsed speeches, passive listeners, chairs that might as well hold "human-shaped cardboard cutouts") are a symptom of **"big company-itis"**: a meeting culture stifled by bureaucracy that **prioritizes predictability over effective communication**. Good meetings are **productively unpredictable**. They have dialogue instead of corporate pageantry, and everyone leaves knowing they contributed and what happens next. Even modest one-off meetings are consequential.

**The three ingredients for electric meetings: the right people, a crowdsourced agenda, and an atmosphere where everyone feels safe to respectfully disagree.**

Recreate the small seminar where nobody can sit and observe, nine students in fast 90-minute discussions (Jacobs's Bennington College), not the 150-seat lecture hall of passive listening. Learning style matters, and lively discussion is the best way people learn in business.

## The right people: keep it small, but large enough

- **Size: about 15 to 20.** At 45 to 50, there's posturing. At 15 to 20 there's less of it, and discussion is more sincere.
- **Invite a mix.** Senior leaders should see what's going on across the company, not just in their own jurisdictions. That gives them context, so they decide with the broader organization in mind rather than in a silo, and they help solve problems wherever those sit. The top few dozen people should know everything going on, good and bad.
- For the most important meetings, bring the **core C-suite plus a handful of subject-matter experts or rising stars**: people who add value and benefit from the exposure.

## The crowdsourced agenda: the MOR process

**MORs and QORs**, the monthly and quarterly operating reviews, are the most important meetings on the calendar. They are "the backbone of how I run my businesses."

- An MOR comes every 30 days or so. It covers the prior month's results and defines the coming month's opportunities.
- A QOR comes every third month and reviews the quarter as a whole before earnings are reported publicly.
- Each unit, whether a core service operation or a corporate function, gets its own review with unit and corporate leadership plus invitees.

**The steps:**

1. **Build the deck.** The unit leader and financial planning and analysis produce it, timed to go out in advance. It covers:
   - concise financial and operational KPIs;
   - customer and employee satisfaction scores;
   - comparisons to the prior month or quarter and to the same period last year;
   - projections, with their assumptions.

   Keep less content per page, so the what, when and why of each takeaway is immediately clear. **Resist the urge to flood communications with nonessential information.**
2. **Everyone reads it cover to cover beforehand**, at their own pace and in their own learning style. Thirty minutes of reading replaces a couple of hours of live presentation to a bored audience, and no meeting time is spent flipping slides.
3. **Each attendee submits their top one or two takeaways and top one or two questions.** Questions must be incisive and cut straight to the results. Examples:
   - Why is revenue with a top customer trending down, and what can we do to course-correct it?
   - Why didn't IT launch the tool it said would go live last week?
   - How can we improve forecasting for seasonal demand?

   Questions usually zero in on problems, since solving problems creates shareholder value. They can also ask how to leverage a success: how did an account manager turn around a customer relationship in the dumps, and can we replicate it?
4. **Survey and rank.** The team puts every takeaway and question into a digital survey in random order. Each attendee scores each item from 1 ("This isn't worth discussing") to 10 ("Discussing this should be the highest priority").
5. **Run the meeting from the ranking.** Open by reading the takeaways that scored 7 or higher. Then take the questions from the highest-ranked down, as far as time allows.

The process guarantees that everyone arrives having read the deck, thought about the takeaways, formulated questions and ranked the group's submissions, so the meeting starts in productive conversation.

## Avoid the spin: moderation

- **An assigned moderator runs the tempo in real time**: pausing on a topic for follow-ups, or moving on, depending on how productive the discussion is. The quality of moderation is critical.
- **Pick moderators unexpectedly.** The unit leader is often a sensible choice. Alternatives:
  - A **"surprise moderator"** from outside the unit, with a neutral view and no compulsion to put a positive spin on things.
  - An attendee senior enough to be invited but not so experienced as to be the obvious choice, who gains hands-on experience shepherding strong personalities.
- **Spontaneity defeats the curated narrative.** No one should arrive with a scripted story. Senior management needs an unobstructed view to meet its fiduciary duty, and people sense when a narrative doesn't ring true. Put it all on the table, good and bad, and especially the bad, because what's going wrong can often be turned into a moneymaking opportunity.
- **Operating reviews double as succession planning.** They build bench strength by exposing executives to the whole company, and they let leadership watch rising stars in action. A record of consistently adding value in reviews can tip a promotion. Someone who runs an amazing MOR early in their career has shown they can lead financially savvy executives who value their time.

## Explore conflicting opinions

- **Conflicting opinions are the purpose of the meeting.** Unanimous agreement that everything is copacetic is a warning sign. There are almost always alternative ways to read the same facts, and if those don't emerge in plainspoken discussion, something may be wrong.
- Constructive dialogue between two people who differ lifts the debate from speaking style to **the gist: what is the value, what is the risk, what is the domino effect?**
- **Push every attendee to speak up.** Someone who only listens "is taking without giving." The moderator goes around the room several times, putting the same question to every participant. On video, participants can type answers into the chat.
- **Take narrow questions offline** when only a few subject-matter experts can address them. Keep the group on questions that need its collective wisdom, especially the big, hairy problems.
- **The exit test.** By the end, everyone should know how the unit performed against plan, where the opportunities for improvement are, and what tactical steps will hit the targets. "We'll put our heads together tomorrow and get to the bottom of it" means real connections were made.

## Set few rules, but cast them in stone

The tenor and the structure of a meeting are inseparable. Side conversations, snarky comments and multitasking on phones disrupt the vibe and make people disengage. The four guidelines:

- **Turn off all devices.**
- **Only one person talks at a time. No side conversations.**
- **Give the speaker your full attention and keep an open, receptive mind.**
- **Disagree, but disagree respectfully.**

Pause the meeting to enforce them whenever necessary. Enforcement is real. Jacobs banned a charismatic chief customer officer who couldn't stop side conversations from all operating reviews for a full year, twelve rounds of sending representatives in his place. He returned a model attendee, and everyone else got the message.

"Give the person you're with your entire attention while listening to them non-judgmentally. This is the greatest gift you can give anybody."

## Validate, don't denigrate

- **Appreciate the constellation.** This exact set of people and circumstances has never existed before and never will again. Don't waste the time.
- **Being wrong and disagreeing must both be fair game**, with friendly back-and-forth to get the dialectical process rolling. Criticism can be misread as a personal attack, so encourage respectful critique and discourage snark. Never embarrass a colleague or play "gotcha," especially in front of others, even when someone says something demonstrably wrong.
- **Pair correction with validation, validation first.** For example: "I see your point, but I think the projections might be off." Or: "I agree with you directionally, but I want to make sure we're working with the right assumptions." Validation lowers the temperature and makes the other person less defensive.
- **Overruling the group's consensus**, which is rarely needed because the group usually lands in the right place:
  1. "I get what everyone is saying."
  2. List the main points behind the consensus, to show you listened.
  3. "But I'm coming out in a different place than the group."
  4. Explain where you've landed and why.
  5. Ask them to support you as you make the executive decision.
- **Pull rank without apology, but only after listening carefully to everyone.** The CEO has the widest view, the most sources, strategic information the group may lack, and relevant experience. Seniority confers both the right and the responsibility to lead to a different conclusion.

## End on an uplifting note

Rotate one or more closing exercises into every meeting, with every attendee participating:

- Share the coping techniques you use to handle stress.
- Share something about yourself this group doesn't know.
- Share your top two takeaways, or a good takeaway someone else offered.
- Name an MVP inside the company but outside the room, and say why. After the meeting, email each MVP to tell them what they were recognized for and thank them.
- Name someone whose star went up in your eyes during the meeting, and say why. ("Whose star went up in your eyes during the meeting, and why?")
- Look directly at someone and address them by name with one of these stems: "[Name], I respect you because …" / "[Name], thank you for …" / "[Name], you're really great at …" / "[Name], I'm proud to be on the same team as you because …" (For the last, have each person stand in turn.)
- Share what you're most proud of accomplishing at the company, your high-five moment (your happiest moment there so far), or what you expect your happiest moment over the next year to be.
- Tell the group: "I'm a great leader because …" / "I'm awesome at my job because …" / "I will fabulously succeed because …"
- Resolve to do something to improve the company, and tell the group what it is.

**The positivity exercise for important meetings.** Stand silently in a circle for about two minutes, or as long as needed. Look at each person in turn, sincerely think about what you respect about them, and silently wish them success and happiness in their career at the company.

## Boards

**Run board meetings like operating reviews**, with collaboration and open communication. "Board meetings shouldn't be choreographed. If all you're going to do is read a script, just send it as an email."

- Directors have a **standing invitation to every operating review** and may ask whatever they want. The structure is the same as the board's own meetings: "a formulaic approach designed to make the meetings anything but formulaic once the dialogue starts."
- **Engage the independent directors at every opportunity.** They respond by logging far more hours than typical directors. A rubber-stamp board that shows up four times a year for choreographed meetings wastes experienced, intelligent professionals.
- **Give them undistorted information**, so directors never have to work out the company's real story or second-guess management. Board access is covered in [culture-and-communication.md](culture-and-communication.md).

## External meetings: crowdsource the agenda

People won't remember everything you say, but they'll remember how you made them feel. The most effective way to show an audience respect is to **let the audience set the agenda.**

- **At a fireside chat, town hall or global teleconference**, take a minute or two to introduce yourself and a few high-level themes, then go straight to spontaneous Q&A. The message: you've honored me with your time, so I'll honor you by talking about what interests you.
- **Be the "ask me anything and I'll answer" guy.** Prefer rough around the edges to polished. A neutered monologue written by someone else and vetted by lawyers could be delivered by a robot. Pre-scripted panel answers, with questions agreed in advance as at professional associations and government events, are safe but boring. Unscripted Q&A makes you up your game: you must be quick on your feet and in firm control of your emotional reactions, and the payoff is connection.
- **Crowdsource agendas for peer gatherings too.** Jacobs was building an invitation-only series for senior executives in which the crowdsourced, top-ranked questions are answered off the cuff from experience.
