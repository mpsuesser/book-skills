# How to Make a Few Billion Dollars

Brad Jacobs's method, drawn from 44 years as a career CEO: five companies started from scratch (seven counting two spin-offs), each built into a billion-dollar-plus enterprise, through about 500 acquisitions. He calls himself a **moneymaker**. The book's summary of how it is done: *anticipating the important trends, building all-star organizations, doing accretive M&A, growing those businesses, and rearranging our brains to think differently.* Each of the six areas below is one lever. Its core rules are on this page, and its procedures, question lists and cases are in the sibling file it names.

The book speaks to founders, CEOs and executives pursuing big business goals, and equally to anyone with a big dream in sports, the arts, politics or philanthropy. **Results matter**: judge every move by whether it pleases customers and drives financial results.

## How to apply it

1. **Name the lever.** Is the user's situation about mindset, choosing a trend or industry, a deal, people, meetings, or culture and communication? Crises usually involve mindset (radical acceptance) and communication together.
2. **Load the branch file** for each lever involved, and work from its procedure or questions. The steps are in that file.
3. **Answer in Jacobs's terms** and at his confidence. He asserts, so assert. Hand over the exact questions, scripts and tests he uses, rather than general advice.

## 1. Rearrange your brain → [mindset.md](mindset.md)

Successful people **think differently**: they have *rearranged their brains* to prevail in turbulent environments where conventional thinking fails. The tools are **thought experiments** (*gedankenexperiments*, mind exercises that stimulate the imagination to create expansive experiences) and techniques drawn from CBT and mindfulness.

- **Embrace the problem.** His mentor Ludwig Jesselson's line: business *is* problems, so find them and even enjoy them, because each one is an opportunity to remove an obstacle and get closer to success. Problems are an asset. Run toward them.
- **Big, hairy deals.** Deals fall into four quadrants of size × risk. Big, low-risk deals don't exist. Small, low-risk deals are too small. Small, hairy deals are the worst. **Big, hairy deals with solvable problems are the "bingo quadrant"**: cut off the hair and de-risk.
- **Radical acceptance** (Marsha Linehan): accept reality as it is, not as you wish it to be, and let go of the illusion of control. Then make the forward-looking decision about what's likely to happen next, with the risk quantified.
- **Non-judgmental concentration**: put all your attention on one thing without labeling it good or bad. It is step one whenever a thorny issue arises.
- **Dialectical thinking**: look at something from different perspectives and interpret it in multiple ways that are all valid.
- **Think huge** but specifically: exactly how much, by when, and for what.
- **Stay humble.** Feeling invincible is the worst thing for decision-making.

## 2. Get the major trend right → [trends.md](trends.md)

Jesselson again: *you can mess up a lot of things in business and still do well as long as you get the big trend right.* **Technology is the dominant megatrend**, its rate of change is accelerating, and **AI is the mothership**: the central determining factor in whether businesses and whole industries collapse or prosper.

- Screen industries for **scalability** first: can this go from a few million to tens of billions of dollars? Is it growing much faster than GDP? Is it big ("even a small chunk of a huge, growing industry is still big")? Does size bring advantages and economies of scale? Is it fragmented and ripe for consolidation? Is there an AI threat or opportunity?
- In a roll-up, **the arbitrage between your cost of capital and the multiple you pay for acquisitions is the biggest value-creation lever. Improving the actual profitability of the business is the second-biggest.**
- Look for **"obscene profits"**: an industry where the leaders are earning outsized profits that a better operator can share in.
- Research in three parts: educate yourself exhaustively, compile the questions that matter, then take them face-to-face to the most knowledgeable experts.
- Plan to invest heavily in tech, with a bigger tech budget than competitors that is spent more wisely. The XPO mandate: **automate everything you can.**

## 3. Do lots of high-quality M&A without imploding → [m-and-a.md](m-and-a.md)

**The core formula: buy businesses at profit multiples lower than the multiple your stock trades at, then significantly improve them.**

- **The downside test**: do a deal only if the downside case is still good for the company, the base case is excellent and the upside case is off the charts. Accept no significant chance of missing the base case.
- **The rationale**: how will this deal convince more customers to wire money from their bank account to yours? A deal must earn a high return on invested capital, help thrill customers, differentiate the offering or fill a strategic gap. Otherwise it makes you **bigger, not better**.
- **Speed without pressure.** Research before first contact, so the offer arrives early with a two-week path to a definitive agreement. Talk to several targets at once, so no single deal ever feels like one you must do. "The deals I've avoided have contributed more to my success than the deals I've done."
- **Respect the seller.** Sellers are under great stress. Be 100 percent honest, show your enthusiasm, say yes to everything except your must-haves, and never renegotiate after the handshake.
- **Remove every trace of vanity** from deal-making.
- **Cultural integration is the single most important thing to get right after signing**, with operational integration (speed and standardization) a close second. Buy cultures that are compatible, even if dissimilar, because a culture cannot be force-fit.

## 4. Build an outrageously talented team → [talent.md](talent.md)

The most important thing a CEO does is recruit superlative people. Break the "good is usually good enough" rule for one thing only, hiring: "an empty seat is less damaging than a poor fit."

- Hire for four qualities: **intelligence, hunger, integrity, and collegiality.** A deficiency in any one is a risk not worth taking. Intelligence alone screens out 90 percent of candidates.
- **A, B and C players.** Aim for an A player in every seat and run **a C-free zone**. Run the **quitting thought experiment**: your gut reaction to imagining someone resigning tells you their grade. "Losing an A player is a cardinal sin." B players hire C players.
- **Fire C players, but be nice**: act decisively and compassionately, and don't be cheap on severance.
- **"Overpay"** for top talent, and tie the pay to performance. Use uncapped incentive compensation and above-market equity that reaches deep into the org chart. "The only way to earn windfall money in my companies is to make outsized contributions to value creation."

## 5. Run electric meetings → [meetings.md](meetings.md)

Boring, scripted meetings are a symptom of **"big company-itis"**. Electric meetings are *productively unpredictable*. They take **three ingredients: the right people, a crowdsourced agenda, and an atmosphere where everyone feels safe to respectfully disagree.**

- **MORs and QORs** (monthly and quarterly operating reviews) are the backbone of the business. The deck goes out in advance, each attendee submits takeaways and questions, the group ranks the submissions, and the meeting discusses them in ranked order under a moderator.
- Conflicting opinions are the purpose of the meeting, so unanimity is a warning sign. **Validate, don't denigrate**: state the validation first, then the correction.
- **Four rules, cast in stone**: turn off all devices; only one person talks at a time, with no side conversations; give the speaker your full attention with an open, receptive mind; disagree, but disagree respectfully.

## 6. Kill the competition instead of killing each other → [culture-and-communication.md](culture-and-communication.md)

Culture is the CEO's proxy for being in front of every employee every day. Model it on the **superorganism** (an ant colony is far more powerful than the sum of its members because its members communicate constantly), balanced with human individuality, the way a jazz group improvises.

- **It's impossible to over-communicate.** Keep a consistent cadence, because a CEO who goes silent invites speculation.
- **Give the board access**: open information, 100 percent unvarnished honesty, and directors free to call anyone.
- **Listen deeply**, and answer every inbound message. A listening channel that is ignored costs more credibility than having no channel.
- **Criticism from employees isn't dirty laundry, it's gold.** Run the quarterly four-question survey, read every response, and share the results with everyone.
- In a crisis, practice radical acceptance in public: state the bad news plainly to employees, customers and investors, together with the plan.
