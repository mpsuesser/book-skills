# Build an outrageously talented team

Hiring, grading, firing and paying people the way Jacobs does. The two-part goal for every key hire: **accomplish big things and have fun doing it.** The most important thing a CEO does is recruit superlative people who combine talent with the capacity for constructive interactions with others.

**Break the perfection rule for people.** Elsewhere, good is usually good enough. In hiring, make the choices as perfect as they can be, because few mistakes cost more than the wrong hire in money, time, energy and other employees' well-being. Even 90 percent hiring accuracy leaves one in ten misaligned, which can be disastrous at senior levels.

## Use multiple sources for assessment

- **Rigor beyond the norm.** Management candidates go through seven or eight interviews, sometimes more. Take all the time needed, and leave the seat open rather than hire someone you're unsure of: **an empty seat is less damaging than a poor fit.**
- **The leader's connection interview.** As XPO's CEO, Jacobs interviewed every headquarters hire, from the front desk to the C-suite. He did not test skills; he tested whether the person could connect with him and the team. Monitor your own reactions across the table: *Do I feel relaxed and strong in this person's presence? Am I inspired by them?*
- **A pre-interview questionnaire** of 45 questions (below), completed before the interview. The basics arrive up front, and the interview can go to the person behind the résumé.
- **Screen for deception.** A former CIA investigator and polygraph examiner (Dick Houston) has screened nearly every senior employee Jacobs has hired.
- **Guard against personal bias.** Trusted colleagues interview the candidate too and give honest assessments of qualifications and cultural compatibility. In the hiring discussion, colleagues are expected to respectfully disagree with the leader and with each other. That keeps the filter strong.
- **Discard any pre-imagined picture of the ideal candidate.** Jacobs's three successor CEOs look nothing alike:
  - Mario Harik, from Beirut, a tech prodigy who rose from CIO.
  - Malcolm Wilson, a patient, measured Englishman who arrived with an acquisition.
  - Drew Wilkerson, a straight-talking Southerner hired from a competitor.

  They are nearly identical in the four qualities.

## The four qualities: intelligence, hunger, integrity, and collegiality

If a candidate is deficient in any one, don't take the risk. If they score high in all four and have the skills for the role, snap them up. In short: smart, hardworking, honest, and kindhearted.

### Intelligence

The first screen, and it eliminates 90 percent of candidates. There's no substitute for smarts in multidimensional, evolving industries. Intelligent people treat problems as puzzles to solve quickly, or uncut diamonds to mine. A Spencer Stuart study in the 1990s, across companies at every performance level, found that the CEO trait most closely correlated with organizational success is **high IQ**, not EQ. "The smartest thing I do as a CEO is to make sure that most of the people I hire are smarter than I am."

- Ask objective questions (college GPA, IQ if known), but beware **confusing pedigree with ability**. SAT scores can be tutored up, admissions gamed, and big-brand jobs landed through connections. Drew Wilkerson went to a college ranked below the top 100 and runs RXO masterfully.
- Some roles need **focused intelligence** in a technical or operational area, not general genius.
- **Truly smart people are humble, not arrogant.** A self-described genius is a turn-off, and even a hint of arrogance is a red flag. Hire go-getters with big egos who still genuinely respect others: "a burning desire to win," and they "play fairly."
- **The two arrogance checks** to ask yourself in the interview:
  1. *Can this person think dialectically?* Can they think from multiple perspectives and reconcile streams of information that flow in different directions?
  2. *Are they capable of changing their opinion?* Rigid thinkers, at any IQ, are mired in their own point of view.

### Hunger

Hungry people have tenacity. They are energized by big projects, work nights and weekends, and don't give up when problems emerge.

- **Hire only people motivated to make a lot of money.** You can't pay the mortgage with advice, and compensation is the most effective motivator. If a candidate says "I'm not motivated by money," suspect that they're not being candid or that they lack the hunger a profit-driven enterprise needs.
- Hire people who **actively enjoy** an intense, results-oriented culture, not those merely resigned to hard work. Keep an entrepreneurial spirit and a scrappy competitive streak at any size.
- **Slightly understaffed, never badly understaffed.** Lean teams focus better and do less redundant busywork, and whether someone would thrive lean is a test of hunger. Substantially understaffed teams mentally check out.
- **Watch for change.** People lose their hunger through relationship issues, an about-face in philosophy, or making a lot of money and losing their edge.
- **Guard against burnout.** Every so often, tell the heaviest hitters to chill out: turn off the phone, rest, recharge, and come back swinging. That beats goading people into bare-minimum effort.

### Integrity

Everyone must do what they say they'll do. Teamwork runs on trust. One integrity-impaired person sets everyone else looking over their shoulders, so filter them out at hiring. The three lessons:

1. **If someone is willing to lie about small things, they're usually willing to lie about big things.** Trustworthy employees are leadership's eyes and ears; dishonest ones blind management to reality.
2. **Sooner or later, liars get caught.** Honest people are more successful in the long run. Liars are an existential threat at scale, and the team can sense them.
3. **Honest people don't have to tell you how honest they are.** They show it by doing what they say and speaking straightforwardly. Those are the cues to look for during hiring.

### Collegiality

"An organization is like a party. You only want to invite people who bring the vibe up." Collegial teammates usually outperform nasty ones, and cutthroat people don't help in the long run. "No one wants to work with a jerk, even a very talented jerk." Colleagues influence each other psychologically, emotionally and even physiologically.

There is a selfish reason as well. At roughly 5,000 good days left, Jacobs won't spend an hour with unkind people.

The target workplace has **quiet intensity plus emotional warmth**. Its fun is not the pool tables, bean bags and beer kegs inexperienced leaders use to distract people from hard work. XPO headquarters scored 9.0–9.5 out of 10 on anonymous job-satisfaction surveys. A jerk can slip through interviews, but one jerk puts a stick in the spokes for everyone, and jerks show their colors quickly when they can't work harmoniously.

## A, B, and C players

Letting good and mediocre employees coexist for months or years is lose-lose: it stifles the good one and drags down team spirit. Three techniques raise the caliber of the organization:

1. **The quitting thought experiment.** Imagine the person walking in unannounced to resign. They have another job in another city, have already bought a house, aren't open to a counteroffer, and want only to plan a responsible transition. Read your gut:
   - *"I was going to fire this person sooner or later anyway... and now we won't have to pay severance"* → **C player.**
   - *"I don't like this, but I can live with it... we'll run a search and find someone else, maybe even someone better"* → **B player.**
   - *Panic*: "We're so screwed!... There's no way we're going to find somebody as fantastic," too freaked out to hear the rest → **A player.** Treat it as a wake-up call: have HR check that person's comp package and promotion timeline now, before they ever think of leaving. **Losing an A player is a cardinal sin.**
2. **Companywide coding.** HR codes every manager-level employee and above as A, B or C, through a comprehensive process weighing **performance and potential**. In a results culture, someone hitting their numbers is an A or a B, depending on how impressive the targets are. Goal: an A in every position. Some Bs are inevitable, but be **a C-free zone**. "If you hire a B player, they'll probably hire C players. Then you'll have C players led by B players."
3. **The relationship appraisal** with each direct report. One-on-one, each of you rates the quality of your professional relationship from one to ten. If it isn't a ten, ask: *"What would make our professional relationship a ten?"*

## Fire C players, but be nice

An employee with consistent performance issues is probably a C player. Unless an external factor clearly outside their control is to blame, **graciously, immediately exit them**; otherwise today's bad results repeat.

- **Wayland Hicks's rule** (Jacobs's COO at United Rentals, formerly COO of Xerox): once it's evident someone isn't a match, act decisively but compassionately. Show kindness whatever the circumstances, let them leave with their head held high, and don't be cheap on severance. Don't create a situation where a decade later you still avoid them in the supermarket.
- **Don't reserve firing for misconduct.** Management must catch quiet, chronic underperformance that weakens the enterprise without causing overt trouble. If a **60-day improvement plan** produces too little headway, let them go. The traits that got them hired will lead them to a better fit.

## "Overpay" your employees

Nobody comes to work to make money for the CEO. They come to make money for themselves and their families: a house, college for their kids, a bigger dream. "Most people aren't making money for themselves—they're making money for the people they love." Jacobs has "overpaid" almost every direct report he has had, and many have made tens of millions of dollars, which he paid happily because the return was tens of billions in shareholder value. **The only way to earn windfall money in his companies is to make outsized contributions to value creation.**

- **Pay for the role that drives the result.** In a contract-logistics warehouse earning tens of millions a year, the warehouse manager matters most, more than the 100 staff and the dozens of people above them on the org chart. Saving $100,000 a year on salary makes no sense when a second-best manager can cost millions in lost profit.
- **Profit-sharing is the secret.** Dick Colburn, the billionaire who sold Jacobs U.S. Rentals, said success in business is mainly about compensating your people: designing a profit-sharing formula that marries their interests with the company's.
- **Pay like the big corporations.** Recruit from top-paying enterprises (Alphabet, Amazon, Goldman Sachs). Break the "penny wise and pound foolish" habit of low-paying industries. "Don't be cheap with what you pay for talent. Tie compensation to performance."
- **The CEO designs the incentive plans personally**, rather than delegating them as an HR task. Customize them by role, keeping the **domino effect** in mind:
  - A salesperson paid commission on revenue alone will sign customers who don't fit operational capabilities. That hurts efficiency, service, margins and profit, and creates tension between sales and operations.
  - So base sales commission **partly on top-line results and partly on profit**. Sales then partners with operations to pick the best-fit customers.
- **Every incentive plan needs two things**: a clear sense of the benchmarks, and a fair chance of meeting them through concerted effort. Get either wrong and the money motivates no one.
- **Don't cap incentive comp.** A salesperson who hits the cap six or eight months into the year just waits for the incentive to reset while collecting base pay. If incentive comp is open-ended with performance, they keep signing profitable business.
- **Base at market, incentives above market**, down to hourly employees. A strong work ethic plus the ability to earn a lot is a powerful personal tailwind.
- **Equity, above market and deep.** Equity attracts **true believers** aligned with the vision, and vesting builds in retention.
  - Review the vested and unvested holdings of the top 200 employees four or five times a year. Where someone's holdings fall short of the value they add, give a **spot equity bonus**. Where a top performer is nearly fully vested, grant more equity over an extended period.
  - Extend meaningful equity far deeper into the org chart than is typical, so as many people as possible feel part of something exciting.

Aligned pay makes employees happier and harder-working and managers more creative, and it moves the company forward on operational excellence, market share and profitability. Compensation is one of the smartest investments you can make.

## The 45 pre-interview questions

Sent before the interview to surface the candidate's self-awareness and behavioral traits.

**Strengths**

1. Adjectives or phrases that capture your essence.
2. What do you see when you look at your professional self in the mirror?
3. What motivates you, and what are you trying to accomplish at this point in your career?
4. Your biggest career accomplishment(s) so far.
5. Your biggest professional strengths.
6. The high point of your career so far.
7. The parts of your jobs you've liked most.
8. Your favorite professional activity.
9. Five reasons for your professional success.
10. What your subordinates think your strengths are.
11. Your favorite boss, and why.
12. Positive things your bosses and colleagues might say about you.
13. The most significant praise in a performance appraisal in the last five years.

**Areas for improvement**

14. Your biggest professional weaknesses or areas for improvement.
15. The low point of your career so far.
16. Your biggest mistakes, and what you learned from them.
17. The most significant criticism in a performance appraisal in the last five years.
18. Your least favorite boss, and why.
19. Negative things your bosses and colleagues might say about you.
20. What your subordinates think your weaknesses are.
21. The biggest frustrations or failures of your career.
22. The one thing you'd change about yourself.

**Miscellaneous**

23. Describe your character.
24. The quality you admire most in people.
25. The parts of your jobs you've enjoyed least.
26. Your toughest business decision: how you handled it, and what you learned.
27. Your favorite job, and why.
28. Your least favorite job, and why.
29. The defect a professional should never allow themself to have.
30. How you manage personal/professional balance.
31. Whom you admire in business, and why.
32. The reason for your separation from each of your jobs.
33. Your perfect job, in an ideal world.
34. Three of your biases.
35. Your working habits: a typical day and week, hours worked, travel.
36. What it takes to succeed in this job.
37. On a scale of 1–10, how well your skill set matches the job.
38. What would make your answer to 37 a 10.
39. On a scale of 1–10, subject to acceptable compensation, how much you want this job.
40. What would make your answer to 39 a 10.
41. Your top three reasons for interest in this position.
42. Would you accept this job if it were offered?
43. What more you need to know to decide whether the role is right for you.
44. Your questions or comments for us.
45. Why should we hire you?

**Close the interview on a positive note**: "What have been one or two of the happiest moments of your professional life so far?"
