# Rearrange your brain

The mental techniques for making good decisions in fast, stressful, turbulent business: deals in dispute, setbacks, anxiety, crises, and the ambition to think big. Jacobs counts them as absolutely critical to his success. "So much of success in business comes from keeping your head in a good place."

## Thought experiments

A **thought experiment** (Einstein's *gedankenexperiment*: Einstein pictured riding a beam of light and arrived at E = mc²) is a mind exercise that stimulates the imagination to create expansive experiences. It rearranges the brain so you can look at information from unusual perspectives. The aim is not to daydream your way to success but to see problems and opportunities differently. With expanded awareness you can shed the nitty-gritty and size up *what matters most, what I need to know, and what I should do about the issue at hand.* Jacobs meditates about half an hour a day (15 minutes morning, 15 at night), much of it spent in thought experiments. The profound calm they bring is "when many of my best decisions materialize."

- **Expand the possible.** Think in large arcs of time and space: billions of years instead of decades, light-years instead of miles. Let space expand until you identify with the totality of the universe, or a multiverse, and feel like a "bodiless mind floating in space" (Milton Erickson). Then do the opposite: contract space into the palm of your hand or the nucleus of an atom. For time, run the universe from the Big Bang 13.8 billion years ago to now, or retrieve long-forgotten childhood memories. A mind "thinking inside a box" makes its problems look bigger.
- **Feeling the brain** (Jacobs's own technique). The brain carries far more "dialogue" with the hands than with the elbow, and staring at your hands can trigger a trancelike state. Instead, close your eyes, let your attention gently float in the brain, and "feel" it like a tactile sensation. It often produces synesthesia. Use it to rejuvenate when energy is low or you're not in the zone, and to unleash creativity.
- **Awe recall**: relive a beautiful artwork, a sunrise, a piece of music, holding your child. Positive emotions matter most in chaotic business environments.

**The nine thought experiments.** Find a quiet moment, relax (slow, deep breaths), and see what your brain does with each:

1. You are at the Big Bang, expanding in every direction at the speed of light. You become elementary particles, cool into hydrogen and helium atoms, and feel gravity clump you into stars.
2. A star explodes five billion years ago. Its stardust is pulled together into our Sun and Solar System. Everything and everyone on Earth is made of elements from the same stars.
3. Every grain of sand on Earth is a star; multiply that by ten. That is about one septillion stars, with trillions of miles between them and 100 billion trillion planets orbiting them.
4. Expand your awareness step by step: body, room, building, country, continent, Earth, Solar System, Milky Way, Virgo Cluster, Virgo Supercluster, the entire universe (about 94 billion light-years, or 540 sextillion miles, across), then an infinite multiverse. Let your mind merge with it.
5. You are pulled into a black hole but keep your consciousness: zero mass, time and space obliterated.
6. Apart from your brain cells, none of your 30 trillion cells will be the same in seven years. Stomach cells are replaced weekly, skin monthly, liver every six weeks, red blood cells every four months.
7. Picture yourself as your grandparent, then as your grandparent's grandparent, and keep going back a couple of generations at a time for 195,000 years.
8. Trace kinship outward from siblings (shared parents) and cousins (shared grandparents) to the first human, about 10,000 generations back: the common ancestor of all eight billion people alive, your colleagues, customers, competitors and shareholders included.
9. About 50 percent of a banana's DNA is identical to yours. You're related to a piece of fruit.

## Throwing love vibes

Love is an expansive emotional state that neutralizes conflict. "It's much easier to make a lot of money with a team that's in the love vibe."

- **The exercise** (Jacobs used it officiating his daughter's wedding): imagine all the instances of love between two people from the beginning of time until now, and hold it for a minute of silence. Then feel all the love that exists on earth at this moment. Then all the love that could exist over the billions of years to come. Finally, gather the past, present and future love in your hands and pantomime tossing it at the people being honored.
- **In tense moments** (a deal stalled on indemnifications, representations or price), take a breath and tune into a moment when you felt intense love. The dialogue usually becomes more constructive.
- **Gratitude conversations** (Jacobs's version of Martin Seligman's gratitude letters): pick a colleague who deserves your gratitude. Write notes to yourself on why: they solve thorny problems, they're a customer-service all-star, they lift the tenor of the whole organization. Then find a quiet time, sit down with them notes in hand, and say it directly and personally. The happiness can last weeks or months for both of you.

## CBT tools for the businessperson's mind

Negative automatic thoughts ("I'm inferior… I'm a loser… It's hopeless", impostor syndrome) surface on their own, especially in dynamic personalities. Catastrophizing is a survival trait inherited from hunter-gatherer ancestors. Following Aaron Beck and Albert Ellis, you choose the mental lens you frame life with.

- **The two-step process** for a negative automatic thought: (1) acknowledge it as your natural, self-critical psychology kicking in; (2) challenge its validity, since the psyche was programmed millions of years ago to default to anxiety and fear. Then reframe the situation in a positive light. Treat internal chatter **as useful data rather than objective reality**.
- **Cognitive distortions** to recognize and correct: *catastrophizing* (thinking of small problems as enormous impediments), *perfectionism* (anything less than perfect execution causes intense frustration), and *dichotomous thinking* (rigid, all-or-nothing views).
- **The anxiety questions**: "What's the worst that can happen, and how would I cope with that?" and "If a friend had a similar worry, how would I advise them to handle it?" Distance yourself from the worry to think objectively.
- **Test your bias toward hope or fear**: are your projections too conservative? Is fear keeping you out of an opportunity? Is the anxiety fact-based, or are biases spurring it? Keep biases at arm's length from decisions. A *healthy* fear of failure keeps you sharp, so never take success for granted.
- **Ask about the happiest moment.** "What was the happiest part of your day?" lifts people more than "How was your day?" (Seligman's suggestion for children, who then kept their antennas up all day for it).
- **Give yourself a break.** Life is imperfect, so make fewer self-defeating demands for perfection on yourself and on family, colleagues, customers, vendors and shareholders. A team running fast in multiple directions will goof up, and accepting that keeps your equilibrium.

## Embrace the problem

Jesselson's lesson: making money in business means getting used to problems, because that's what business is. It means *finding* problems, embracing and even enjoying them, because each one is an opportunity to remove an obstacle and get closer to success.

- After the first flush of frustration at a setback, pivot with: "Great! This is an opportunity for me to create a lot of value. If I can just figure out how to solve this problem, I'll be much closer to my goal."
- Problems are the by-product of risk, and you won't create much value without courage. Be honest with yourself, recognize mistakes as soon as they surface, and course-correct with no ego.
- **Diagnose head-on**: Where are the inefficiencies in the process that created this problem? Where can the team reduce operational defects with Lean Six Sigma? Are the right people in key positions?
- "The big gunky problems—existential threats that are not yet terminal—can be where the best opportunities lie."
- **The four M&A quadrants (size × risk)**:

  | | Low risk | Hairy |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Big** | Everyone wants them; they don't exist | **The bingo quadrant**: real money if the problems are solvable |
  | **Small** | Exist, but too small to make much money | Worst: limited reward, odds stacked against you |

  For each problem in a big, hairy deal, decide whether you can **remove it, or quantify it and live with it**. If neither, move on.

## Acknowledge you're not perfect: three impediments to effective leadership

1. **The belief that you're right, no matter what.** Turbulent business requires zigging and zagging opportunistically, even rethinking a former premise. "Sometimes I'm wrong when I think I'm right." Be ready to change beliefs on new information.
2. **The belief that other people must hold the same opinions you do.** Build strong feedback loops and a culture of **dialectical thinking**: looking at something from different perspectives and interpreting it in multiple ways that are all valid. The right answer comes easier when people feel comfortable respectfully disagreeing. Feynman: "We are trying to prove ourselves wrong as quickly as possible, because only in that way can we find progress."
3. **The belief that every inch of a potential course of action must be analyzed before you act.** This applies to hiring, firing, deals, raising capital and capex. Waiting for perfect terms and ideal conditions creates **"corporate constipation."** Critique high-value decisions for prejudices, but don't procrastinate because things are imperfect.

Entropy increases, so expect problems to grow bigger by nature. Accept imperfection, cut your losses, adapt and improve.

## Radical acceptance

Marsha Linehan's principle: be fully present in the moment and accept reality. Let go of the illusion of control by acknowledging the facts as they are, even when you want them to be different. Mary Kissel's version: see the world for what it is, not what you wish it to be. Radical acceptance quiets the noise of yesterday's decisions and today's wishful thinking. The decision then rests on two things, what's likely to happen next and risk management. "Otherwise, you're just gambling, and most gamblers lose."

- **The oil-trader rule**: block out how much you won or lost on the trade and focus on the best thing to do right now. If you think the market's going up, buy more; if down, sell. Quantify the risk of each action.
- **Don't compound a mistake.** Jacobs bought road-rental companies to catch $600 billion of promised TEA-21 infrastructure spending. Only about a third was spent, in dribs and drabs, so he sold the companies at a $500 million loss as the best way forward.

## Leave judgment at the door

**Non-judgmental concentration** is the entry to radical acceptance: fully present, all attention on one thing to the exclusion of everything else. The object can be your heartbeat, your breath, your thoughts and your reactions to them, your emotions, or outside yourself the person you're speaking with, the room, the group, the business environment. It is step one for any thorny issue, because it keeps you in the present and quickly lowers stress. Project no bias onto the object. Good and bad are labels the self-centered mind attaches according to an object's effect on you: a fire at a comfortable distance is "good", the same fire burning your hand is "bad". People and things don't exist relative to you; they simply exist. Take yourself out of the equation for a clearer, more objective view.

## Think huge

Set goals bigger than what you currently think you can accomplish; that helps you achieve them. Then get specific: exactly how much money do you want to make? By when? How will you feel? What will you spend it on, invest in, donate to? Whose lives will you improve? Picture what a few billion dollars looks like (a ballroom stacked to the ceiling with $100 bills), ask what goods and services you could bring the world that would make that room yours, and resolve to make it happen. Narrow your focus to your most important dreams and tune out everything else.

## Worked case: the short-seller attack

The case shows the mindset in action. On December 18, 2018, a short-seller's smear report on XPO hit the financial media. Such firms lever up with derivatives, knowing the bots and part of the press will spread the misinformation, and XPO's stock fell 26 percent in one day. Companies under attack often go to ground, try to stoically ride it out, or complain through the media, which makes things worse. XPO did the opposite:

1. **Concentrated without judging and radically accepted** the situation before seeking a solution, rather than snarling back.
2. **Took the report apart.** The C-suite spent five hours that afternoon going through it page by page, identifying every place the data had been twisted, so that by dinner the team had its arms around every nuance for the fund managers attending. The next day an investor lunch became a Q&A firing squad, and the long-term holders were climbing back on board by afternoon.
3. **Reframed the damage as opportunity.** The cheap stock was "manna from heaven." Bankers urged a $100–200 million buyback and called $2 billion too big a share of market cap, with no precedent. The high-yield market was in the pits, so a bridge loan was needed. Being first doesn't make an idea bad, though. Jacobs called two board meetings, fully informed the directors of the report's distortions and the bankers' view, and recommended they "throw deep and buy big."
4. **Decided within 48 hours.** The board approved a $2 billion buyback. Those shares were later worth $6 billion, a $4 billion profit, and the bridge was refinanced into bonds once the market recovered.

## Stay humble

Nothing is worse for decision-making than feeling invincible, especially right after winning.

- **Value is relative.** Jacobs's old music teacher, Bill Dixon, told him he was wasting his life chasing "silly money stuff," and Jacobs keeps that memory as a touchstone for balance. Make billions only if you truly enjoy it, and only if it gives you more satisfaction than using your time some other way.
- Your knowledge is an insignificant rounding error compared to all the knowledge out there. "Thinking you know it all is a trap... If you stay humble, you'll keep advancing."
- **Identify with the totality.** There are about 400 billion stars in the Milky Way and a septillion in the observable universe, and "we are all star stuff" (Carl Sagan). Contemplating this makes you feel both larger and smaller. Thinking becomes less rote, with a wider perspective and greater calm.
- **Go "all in."** Intensity of focus is magically connected to the end result. Minimize frivolity and maximize what makes a difference. It is in your own hands to make life meaningful rather than just pass time.
