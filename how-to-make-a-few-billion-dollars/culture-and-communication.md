# Kill the competition instead of killing each other

Building a unified team culture through communication and listening, with employees, the board, customers, investors and the market. **Collaboration is the most effective lever for furthering any company's strategy and vision.** Self-motivation is valuable when it helps the team succeed as well as the individual. A CEO can't stand in front of every employee every day urging them to perform together, so **corporate culture is the proxy.** "People want to be part of something big and exciting—and they want to know how they fit into it. Tell them!"

## Superorganisms and saxophones

- **The superorganism** (Hölldobler and Wilson). An ant colony of 20,000 to 100,000 members works as a single coherent superorganism, far more powerful than the sum of its members. Its members guard the colony against threats, rescue each other from collapsed tunnels, and tend the injured.
- **Communication is the superorganism's signature trait.** Ants communicate energetically through pheromones, movement, touch and sound, and that communication enables a division of labor sophisticated enough to raise every member's performance. Bacteria coordinate the same way. They signal with autoinducers in two "languages," one for their own species and one universal, and **quorum sensing** lets them act only once their numbers are sufficient. Every well-run human operation shares this hallmark.
- **The jazz group.** Bill Dixon could pivot off any sound another musician played. He brought out the best in each player by opening a space for experimentation, where bold ideas could be tried without fear of being put down.
- **Balance unity with individuality.** "We can learn from ants, but we're not ants." A culture that unifies a group must also make room for people to be their authentic selves. XPO unified everyone around a common purpose, yet felt staffed by upbeat entrepreneurs.

## Over-communicate with the full team

**It's impossible to over-communicate with the team.** Every employee survey shows that people want to hear more from management. At XPO, "communication" ranked high, sometimes above compensation.

- **Every message is a human-to-human connection.** A rallying cry for the vision, gratitude, news, a request for input, a data-dense update or a friendly touch-base: none is meaningless. Be the opposite of an ivory-tower CEO.
- **Use every channel that works**: email, social media, site visits, town halls, phone calls, video chats. That includes onscreen meetings with groups of frontline workers such as truck drivers.
- **Keep a consistent cadence.** It reinforces openness. A CEO who communicates every week or two and then goes silent invites speculation that the company is in a jam.
- **When something goes wrong, say clearly what happened** and share the plan if you can. Some people won't like the plan, but they'll see you aren't hiding things, and buy-in becomes more likely.

## Give the board access

"We work for the board": the directors are fiduciaries for the shareholders who own the business.

- **Information flows freely**: a steady stream of emails from the CEO's office, plus access to the internal social media community, the CEO's newsletter, the key operating and financial reports, and meaningful data on virtually every aspect of performance.
- **Speak to the board as you require employees to speak to you**, with 100 percent unvarnished honesty. Connect as often as is reasonable, as openly as possible.
- **Give directors full access to the organization.** Many companies keep their board away from executives. Instead, let any director pick up the phone and call anyone, which has repeatedly benefited both sides.
- The commitment starts at the top and must be upheld by everyone who deals with the board. "Being 'all in' is energizing; it's also highly contagious."

## Become a much better listener

Listening is harder than outbound messaging. **Deep listening** means directing your complete attention at another person, and it draws on non-judgmental concentration. Because people are used to getting a tiny fraction of anyone's attention, complete attention creates a profound connection, even in a minute or two. Marina Abramović's *The Artist Is Present*, in which she sat in silent, total presence with one visitor after another, moved people to tears. Checking your phone mid-conversation wastes both people's time.

- **Open a channel at the top.** Share your phone number and email with employees, and invite them to tell you whatever they think you should know. It signals that leadership values the free flow of ideas and information.
- **Answer every inbound message.** If you fail to attend to them, your credibility takes a bigger hit than if you'd never opened the channel. An employee once reported that a roof had leaked for two months while its repair sat stuck in internal bureaucracy. Expediting the fix proved there was no black hole at the end of the feedback loop.

## Be sincere about improving the business

A listening company lets information flow unobstructed. That is a prerequisite for synergies, especially those an acquisition promises, such as cross-fertilizing best practices and cross-selling.

- **Mine your best sites.** Across GXO's roughly 1,000 warehouses in 27 countries, listen deeply to the employees at the top performers, find what they do better than the network overall, and apply it to the rest. It costs a fraction of a consultant. "Employees usually have the answers and will share them with you—if they believe you'll listen and be respectful."
- **Make it safe to share underbaked ideas, and to dissent respectfully.** Back dissent with evidence and sound reasoning, as the scientific method does. That protects the collaborative environment that lets a team capitalize on collective expertise.
- **The agree/disagree exercise.** At the end of a meeting, each attendee names two statements made by others, one they agreed with and one they disagreed with, and says why. Making disagreement a routine group exercise depersonalizes it.
- **Embed listening and feedback across the organization**, and want mixed input. The danger is an "optimistic mirage, where the sun always shines and people tacitly agree to overlook problems." The aim is a culture that faces hard realities, assesses them honestly and triumphs over them.

### The all-employee survey

XPO dropped the Net Promoter Score question ("How likely are you to recommend this company to someone as a place to work?") for this purpose. Satisfied employees were answering it negatively because their site wasn't hiring, or because they had no friends who did warehouse work. Use questions that measure what you mean. The four questions:

1. Are you happy in your job?
2. For those who aren't, how can we fix that?
3. "What's your single best idea to improve our company?"
4. "What's the stupidest thing we're doing as a company?"

- **Cadence**: every 90 days, by email to everyone with a corporate address and by app to frontline workers.
- **Senior management reads every response**, usually tens of thousands, within ten days. Run **word cloud analysis** to detect macro trends in sentiment and clusters of ideas in different parts of the company.
- **Send summaries of the findings to every employee**, favorable and unfavorable alike, with nothing held back. **Criticism from employees isn't dirty laundry—it's gold**, because whoever points out an imperfection hands you an improvement. Feedback will sometimes leak outside and be misunderstood. If you're confident in your leadership team and sincere about improving, the merits far outweigh that hazard.
- **Hear the disgruntled.** A 100 percent satisfaction score is unattainable. Writing off unhappy employees as disgruntled is a mistake, because their discontent may be rooted in a real problem that will eventually "disgruntle" others. Chronic low scorers are **valuable auditors**: hypercritical by nature, they catalog everything suboptimal.
- **Follow up.** A team going through the motions would never have caught the NPS contradiction. When leadership listens to an issue and follows up to resolve it, employees know they have a voice.

## Widen the circle

Extend the communication culture to customers, investors, the trade media, the public and competitors.

### Customers

"In order to make lots of money for our shareholders, we have to make our customers giddy with the quality of the service we provide." Run feedback loops with each enterprise customer and use them proactively to stay ahead of expectations.

**Use feedback loops creatively.** United Rentals used each customer's rental history to audit the equipment the customer owned, and reported back:

- buy a machine you need nine or ten months a year rather than keep renting it from us;
- sell underutilized assets to get the depreciation off your books, and rent them for the few months you need them.

These free audits deepened relationships and led to more business, and the analysis was later automated as a product. A customer relationship is like any other: be clear about how much you value it, and keep working to improve it.

### Investors and crises

Earnings periods are intense, and misses happen. Crises call for proactive communication, attentive listening, and trust that was built beforehand.

**The case.** XPO's largest customer offered to buy the company at an eye-popping premium. Its terms were so one-sided they amounted to a nearly risk-free option for the buyer, including heavy costs to XPO if antitrust regulators blocked the deal. XPO declined. Days before earnings, the customer pulled two-thirds of its business, about $600 million in annual revenue. The response was **radical acceptance in public**. "Bad news is a reality; hiding it is just a waste of time."

1. **Employees, immediately.** The message: you've heard the story; we've lost a chunk of business, and we need to backfill it. Then everyone went to work on precisely that.
2. **Customers.** They are the top priority, and the situation won't disrupt their service.
3. **Investors and media.** Yes, we lost a lot of revenue, which is regrettable, but we'll redeploy our assets and replace this business over time.

Because trust already existed with every audience, fallout was minimal. Most of the revenue was replaced within about a year, and the stock tripled in a little over 26 months from its low.

### Competitors

Don't obsess over rivals, and don't ignore them. Focus mainly on customers' day-to-day activities, and let a strong communication culture, including the monthly operating reviews, carry competitive intelligence from the field and from sales to senior management for strategic consideration. "In some businesses, there's a perceived conflict between quality and speed. A world-class organization figures out how to achieve both."

## The entrepreneurial spirit

The values the culture aims to instill, embodied by a small HVAC contractor Jacobs admires:

- He answers a Sunday heat-wave call without hesitating: "I'll be right there."
- His self-esteem rises and falls with his customer satisfaction scores, and he is unembarrassed about earning well for his family.
- He owns his occasional mistakes, sets them right in a hurry, and never pads an estimate.
- His old employer, cost-cut by private equity, now has "no pride in their service."

The book is written for people who "work their tails off, outsmart the competition, put their customers on a pedestal, and make a lot of money for their families." That takes creativity and an enthusiasm for change, and it can be fostered in a family business or in a multibillion-dollar company.

**The fiduciary frame.** You manage other people's money. Perform that duty by filling an unmet need in the economy with a strong business model and a responsible organization:

- a healthy workplace with good pay;
- pampered customers, and lawful operation that reduces their risk;
- practical optimism that opens minds to solutions.

Most of the value created flows outside the company, to the teachers, nurses, police and union members whose pensions and 401(k)s own the stock.
