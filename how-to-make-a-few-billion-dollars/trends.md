# Get the major trend right

How Jacobs picks the industry, and the trend inside it, that will carry a business from millions to tens of billions of dollars. He is obsessive when learning about an industry, and trendspotting is central to it. "Be a futurist and figure out the bullish and bearish trends driving your industry. If you get the major trends right, you can make mistakes and still succeed."

## The megatrend: technology

Technology is the dominant megatrend "in our part of the universe." To forecast its impact, first visualize its history. Stone tools came more than two million years ago, fire about a million, shelters about 500,000. The past 2,000 years are jam-packed: the abacus, coins, the compass, gunpowder, the printing press, steam, electricity, the telephone, the automobile, flight, computers and nuclear power, all before 1950. The modern era followed: the transistor, ARPANET, the microprocessor, 3D printing (1983), Amazon (1994), the iPhone (2007), cloud computing, CRISPR, AlphaGo, ChatGPT. Two things stand out. **The frequency of technological development is accelerating, and humans are merging with the technologies we create.**

- Technology can now destabilize every company and industry. Don't chase every innovation, but **be the first to capitalize on the most impactful trends in your industry.**
- Most of Jacobs's success came from **innovating traditional business practices**: automation, robotics, data science, information sharing. "If you want to make a lot of money in almost any industry, plan to invest heavily in tech." Be an early adopter but a realist. Have a bigger tech budget than competitors and spend it more wisely.
- **Step outside the noise.** The hard part of spotting tech trends is not a lack of information. The implications are so big that people can't grasp them, and we have an ingrained tendency to discount technological and social change, believing things will stay the same. Examples: Thomas Watson in 1943, "a world market for maybe five computers"; Clifford Stoll in *Newsweek* in 1995, arguing the internet lacked salespeople, the year after Bezos founded Amazon.

## AI is the mothership of the future

For two million years, technology served our physical well-being. Now it is directed at expanding our minds: memory, reasoning, the senses. **"Humans are waning and computers are waxing—that's the single biggest trend I see."** AI is the central determining factor in whether businesses and entire industries collapse or prosper. No one knows what it will do in even six months, and its impact will exceed that of e-commerce, social media and on-demand everything. Any business that ignores it is likely to get smacked in the face.

**The AI lens**, applied to every prospective venture:

- How will this industry be disrupted by advances in AI?
- How soon could that happen?
- What are the risks and opportunities in that disruption?

Jacobs's verdicts, as calibration:

- **Passed on**: a roll-up of accounting firms, because generative AI could close the books and the business would vanish into a black hole. For-profit education, where AI is a headwind: Chegg fell 47 percent the day after its CEO named ChatGPT as a threat to growth. Some financial services firms.
- **Expected to shrink**: human insurance brokers within ten years; much of paralegal and legal work; many jobs in journalism, advertising and communications; coding.
- **Beneficiaries over 20 years**: parts of healthcare, retailing and manufacturing.
- **Friend more than foe**: homebuilding, where AI helps design and marketing but people still need a real bed, sink, shower and toilet, along with staples like clothing and food.
- **Rule for replacement**: every human function that can be broken down into rules, systematic processes and logical decision-making based on historical results can be replaced, CEOs included.

Jacobs studies AI the way he studies any industry. He reads the visionaries (Ray Kurzweil, whose *The Singularity Is Near* is the most impactful book he has read; Masayoshi Son on autonomous freight and warehouses), talks to Menlo Park VCs and his own top tech people, and uses generative AI to synthesize what he gathers. His forecast: better than 50-50 odds that *Homo sapiens* goes extinct this century, probably by merging with technology into a new species. The trend runs from carbon-based humans to silicon-based technology.

## The industry screen

Run these questions on a candidate industry. The survivors make the short list for deep research.

**Scalability** is one of the first things to study.

- Can you envision taking the business from a few million dollars to tens of billions? What is the path, and how fast can you move along it?
- Is the industry growing much faster than GDP, so you get top-line growth "just by showing up"?
- What base price/volume combination do you need to be profitable? How realistic is it to significantly raise the margin over time? What could prevent you?
- Is the industry big? Even a small chunk of a huge, growing industry is still big.

**Structure**

- Does the industry give advantages to size and economies of scale?
- Can the business grow organically, through M&A, or both? Is the industry fragmented and **ripe for consolidation**, with thousands of potential acquisitions lacking computerization or standardization?
- What multiples will you pay for acquisitions, and is that a large enough discount to the multiple your own company should trade at? **The arbitrage between your cost of capital and the multiple you buy companies at is the biggest value-creation lever in a roll-up. Improving the actual profitability of the businesses is the second-biggest.**

**Technology**

- Is there a tech threat, an opportunity, or both? Can processes be automated, and customer service driven higher at a lower cost of labor?
- How could AI affect the industry, positively and negatively?

**"Obscene profits."** The signal that drew Jacobs into oil, waste, rentals and logistics was leaders earning outsized profits in a business he could run better. When he read that the two largest waste companies each made about half a billion dollars a year, his thought was: how hard can it be to have trucks pick up trash, deposit it somewhere safe, and send out an invoice?

**Stay objective.** Never pick an industry because it's in vogue. Keep an open mind about industries you've never heard of: equipment rental came from an analyst's offhand suggestion.

## The three-part research methodology

1. **Educate yourself on the industry as thoroughly as possible.**
   - Read journals, periodicals, newspapers and trade publications, plus employee reviews on recruiting sites.
   - Study the websites and social media of the major players and the up-and-comers. Set Google Alerts on CEO names and keywords, and watch CEO interviews on YouTube.
   - Use paid services (Bloomberg, AlphaSense, Thomson Reuters), sell-side and buy-side analyses, and the SEC's EDGAR database (IPO documents, financial reports, proxies).
   - Go to the most valuable industry conferences, especially the bank-sponsored sell-side conferences, where you meet management teams and hear what investors ask. Mine trade associations' research.
   - Have a team distill the information, and note your observations as you go.
2. **Compile a list of the questions that matter.** More will arise as you research; the process isn't linear.
3. **Get in front of the most knowledgeable experts on each topic**, face-to-face where possible, and listen intently. Experts to seek:
   - CEOs with a track record of growing businesses and creating shareholder value. Peer networks such as the Business Council and the Economic Club of New York give entrée to CEOs in almost any industry.
   - The investment bankers most active in the industry: close to management teams, advisers on many of its acquisitions, raisers of billions of dollars.
   - Venture capital firms, which watch the big trends.
   - Buy-side institutions and fund managers with **battle scars**: they have made and lost money in the industry, so ask what they learned.
   - Industry vendors, shareholder activists, and journalists, who are skeptical by nature.

Then **synthesize**. Make detailed lists of pros and cons, and **word clouds** that bring out the macro themes in everything gathered. Judge strengths and defects from the standpoint of making money for investors, the prime mission of a public company. Finally, detach from the details and lock onto the big questions: **Which direction will this industry move in over the next five years? Ten years?** The answers will be shaped by technology, and especially by AI.

## Finding the next trend inside your business

- **The dream-tech exercise.** Ask customers what their dream technology would look like, assuming anything is feasible and cost isn't a factor; that removes psychological blocks. What capabilities would it have? What data would they push or pull? How would it help them succeed? Evaluate the avalanche of ideas for impact, cost and returns. Short-list the highest-value, highest-return projects, stack rank them, and set an affordable budget. Ask employees and vendors the same.
- **Make sure your tech organization is in direct contact with your customers and employees**, so it prioritizes the highest-impact innovations.
- **Watch the disruptors that haven't hit their stride.**
  - 3D printing (industrial additive manufacturing) means local autonomous manufacturing: digital specifications are transmitted instead of goods, logistics becomes more of an information industry, and demand for cheap overseas labor erodes.
  - Electric trucks: drivers love them, but they cost about three times as much as diesel and aren't competitive without subsidies.
  - Autonomous vehicles: regulation lags in the short term, but they win in the medium to long term on safety and operating cost.

## How the big trend made each company

- **Amerex and Hamilton Resources (oil).** The first edge was arbitrage: Amerex was the first to trade between the New York and Chicago futures markets and the cash markets in Houston, London and elsewhere. Those markets dealt in the same oil yet were largely oblivious to each other, so an identical barrel carried different prices. *Trend*: the need to capture and share information more quickly. Pricing data sat in isolated pockets around the globe, and rival brokers ran on siloed tribal knowledge. Amerex built its own crude global IT system, and every employee entered what they learned about buyers, sellers and prices. Brokers in Houston, London, New York and Tokyo shared the same data within hours instead of days, which gave the firm objective insight into supply, demand and pricing. Hamilton took that data advantage into trading and reached $1 billion of revenue. When well-capitalized banks and trading houses arrived in 1989 to play catch-up, Jacobs parlayed the value into a new venture.
- **United Waste Systems.** *Trends*: landfill capacity was becoming precious as regulation closed small dumps, and hauling and disposal were integrating, which made end-to-end consolidation possible. The tech edge was route optimization while mom-and-pops ran by the seat of their pants. Off-the-shelf software plus pushpins on a map meant 20 trucks did in three days what 50 had done in five. Routes then adapted remotely to weather, traffic and accidents. Some competitors were still on pencil and paper years later.
- **United Rentals.** *Trend*: rental penetration was only 15 percent, while the other 85 percent of equipment sat idle on worksites "getting rusty and dusty." The industry was growing organically, had one national provider, thousands of acquisition targets, and no widespread computerization or standardization. Equipment rental is a service business whose credibility is reliably meeting commitments. Data science managed pricing and **asset utilization** across hundreds of branches. Jacobs bought Wynne Systems, the software most large rivals ran on. That gave him an industry-best platform plus aggregated, anonymized industry data that flagged gluts and shortages early, so pricing could be set proactively. Example: with too much of one machine type in the Southeast, cut the rate there and target multi-month renters.
- **XPO.** *Trends*: brokers taking a growing share of for-hire road freight, plus automation in an industry still working by phone. Brokers are the only ones connected to the whole freight landscape on both sides: shippers see only their own carriers, and small carriers can't reach many shippers. The broker that uses information most efficiently delivers with the fewest empty miles, the best fuel efficiency and the lowest cost. The tailwind was that everyone in the chain would come to demand digital service. Jacobs took control of a logistics company with about $175 million in revenue through a PIPE (private investment in public equity) and built on that. XPO had three levers (scale, data science, automation) and heavy tech investment from day one. Its third hire was an MIT machine-learning expert. It went from zero digital loads to 96 percent with a digital component. The vision: **automate everything you can.** Later, as a value-creation strategy, XPO was split into three public companies: GXO (contract logistics), RXO (tech-driven freight brokerage) and XPO (less-than-truckload).
