# Do lots of high-quality M&A without imploding

Jacobs's acquisition system, from about 500 deals: choosing targets, running the numbers, negotiating, and integrating at speed. He has a bias toward industries ripe for consolidation. Acquisitions are the fastest way to scale and gain the advantages of many locations and greater market share. Scale lets you professionalize operations by integrating best practices, spread the cost base over a larger revenue base, and attract desirable customers and management talent.

## The value-creation formula

**Buy businesses at profit multiples lower than the multiple your stock trades at, then significantly improve them.** The multiple arbitrage is the biggest lever in a roll-up, and operating improvement is the second.

**The United Waste pattern**:

- Don't go head-to-head with the largest players. Go into the tertiary markets the big incumbents overlook (rural Mississippi, Michigan's Upper Peninsula) and be **a big fish in a small pond**.
- Roll up the landfills first, then buy the collection companies hauling waste to them. That brings geographic density and economies of scale.
- Before entering, ask about 100 people who make money in the industry for their advice.
- Time it to the trend. Regulation was forcing small landfill owners to sell rather than fund new compliance costs, and rising disposal fees squeezed haulers into vertical integration.

**Go public.** Free markets value companies correctly over the long term, and public equity lets you raise capital, build vibrant cultures and build brands on a grand scale. At United Waste it supplied the capital to go full throttle on acquisitions.

## Make sure your rationale holds true on the downside

Anyone can buy a company by signing an agreement and wiring the money. The risk must be offset by higher-than-average rewards. A good deal drives stockholder appreciation over the next five or ten years. A pop on announcement day is not the point.

**The return math**, run on the target's most recent available earnings:

1. **Baseline**: ten years of earnings at zero growth.
2. Assume the **organic revenue growth rate** you can achieve over ten years, and the **margin** you can realize.
3. Estimate the **annual and cumulative cash flow** the business will generate or consume over ten years.
4. Your projections must be **substantially higher** than what the target generates now, because of synergies with your larger company, your better operating ability, or both. "There's no such thing as a sweet deal without a clear path to significant growth."
5. **Stress-test every element**, including the underlying assumptions. How likely is the base case? What is the upside scenario? The downside?

**The downside rule.** Do the deal only if *the downside scenario is still good for the company, the base case is excellent, and the upside case is off the charts.* Accept no significant chance that the acquisition misses its base-case projections. If the analysis shows more risk than that, move on.

**The rationale test.** Avoid data murk by stating the rationale up front, then checking whether it holds up during analysis. Every acquisition's rationale reduces to one question: how will this deal contribute to the two main drivers of shareholder value, **pleasing customers and propelling financial results**? Put another way, **how will this deal convince more customers to wire money from their bank account to ours?**

- **The salesperson check**, before any math: ask your key salespeople how existing customers will react to hearing you bought Company X. Will they be happy because it adds value, or see no benefit and worry you'll be distracted?
- **Bigger, not better.** A deal must produce a high return on invested capital, help thrill customers, differentiate the offering or fill a strategic gap. If it does none of these, it just makes you bigger. The expected payoffs must be in the rationale from the start.
- Bigger is usually better, but not always. Know the pros and cons of size: some service industries are better off local.
- **"Going forward" beats "today."** If you can't nail down the benefits of the deal today, you can't see how it dovetails with the big trends, and the deal is too aspirational. Don't buy something just before it falls off a cliff: Time Warner paid over $183 billion for AOL just as dial-up began to die.
- **Remove every trace of vanity.** Never acquire to boost your image or win press coverage. Never knowingly do a deal that dilutes earnings. Jacobs estimates 99 percent of his deals were accretive.

**Consultants.** Until you have enough experience to mitigate M&A risk yourself, weigh hiring a management consultant. Hire one only when the value you expect them to add is **at least ten times their fee**. Point them at high-impact areas: salesforce effectiveness, procurement, SG&A rationalization, pricing. They can also analyze large data sets or lend people when several deals are under review at once.

**Cover your flank, buying or selling.** What comes out of left field kills deals. Cerberus agreed to buy United Rentals for $7 billion, then walked away in the financial crisis. The stock fell 31 percent in 24 hours and later reached $5. The company collected the $100 million breakup fee, chose not to seek another buyer, and rebuilt to well over $350 a share.

## Keep up the pace, not the pressure

Speed is the sharpest edge in competitive deals. Do enough research **before first contact** to cut diligence and negotiation from two or three months to a few weeks. By the first or second meeting you can say: *this is what we're prepared to pay for your business, on these terms; if this is acceptable, we can be signing a definitive agreement in two weeks.*

- **Be selective.** XPO looked at about 2,000 prospects and did 17 acquisitions in its first four years, passing on abysmal deals and on "okay, but not great" ones. "The deals I've avoided have contributed more to my success than the deals I've done."
- **Prefer deals whose primary risk is operational execution.** A recruited team of top operators can fix most flawed operations. You expect to own the business a long time, so hunt for hidden flaws that may surface later.
- **Work bottom-up.** Do the disciplined, meticulous analysis of the most diligent junior analyst at an investment fund, inside a systematic evaluation process that lets you move fast with less risk.
- **Avoid deal pressure.** Talk to multiple targets at once, so you never feel you must do any specific deal.

**The pre-contact questions**, answered as thoroughly as possible before contacting a seller:

- *Growth*: What will be the big drivers of profitable growth over the next five to ten years? What do we have to believe to be confident of that growth? Are those assumptions reasonable, or easily derailed? Are there big hockey sticks in revenue or margin without credible explanations? Can we accelerate scale faster, at a much lower cost base, with AI, automation or robotics?
- *Synergies*: What synergies can we expect from integration? If operational execution isn't up to par, can our training or technology fix it? What are the redundant positions, overhead and real estate costs? What procurement savings come with the added scale? Can we cross-sell to the rest of our company?
- *People*: What's the level of morale? Are the right people in place, with an effective org chart? Where is the company overstaffed or understaffed? Is the salesforce topnotch, or trainable? The same question applies to HR and to financial planning and analysis. Do the compensation plans motivate people to deliver the business plan?
- *External*: How do customers regard the business? Is pricing too high or too low for the market? Could trends in regulation or political policy help or hurt?

Being thorough and fast together telegraphs how serious you are.

## Control your outcomes

- **Align the acquisition process with the business plan.** At United Rentals, Jacobs knew exactly whom to buy: hundreds of small companies doing what he intended to do on a grand scale. He also knew what he would do with them. He would manage the branches as geographic groups on one technology platform, consolidate shared services, rationalize SG&A, share equipment among branches to raise utilization, professionalize pricing, and above all serve customers better, using that loyalty to drive revenue.
- **The tenor of negotiations controls outcomes.** A compatible mindset across the table (entrepreneur to entrepreneur) raises the odds of success.
- **Build the integration playbook before closing.** It is "math and music": hundreds of prescribed action points, with room to be creative about the human side. Visualize every aspect of harmonizing the acquired operation well before closing.

## Respect the seller

Two events can make a sane person temporarily insane: being told their spouse wants a divorce, and being fired. Jacobs adds a third: selling their company. Sellers are stressed and not at their best.

- **Drop the hard-to-get act.** In his first hundred or so deals, Jacobs posed as aloof, down to not returning sellers' calls. It bred anxiety and mistrust, and some sellers resolved to sell to anyone but him. Instead, tell sellers how excited you are and what price and terms work for you. The dating analogy: if you want someone to marry you, why play it cool? Tell them how much you love them.
- **Speak to the seller's legacy.** Will you retain the employees and treat them well? How will you treat customers and vendors, some of them friends? How will the sale affect their communities? Public boards are bound to maximize price, but they often weigh these things too, and if they don't, management does. These emotions tend to rear up between the decision to sell and the signing.
- **Communicate genuinely.** If you admire what they've built, say so. If you're eager, not on the fence, say so. Explain how the negotiation and post-sale processes will work. Offer no false flattery: if you can't say something authentic, stay quiet. **Always be 100 percent honest with sellers. Don't play games.**
- **The "3 Ps"**: always be patient, pleasant, and polite. They lead to better dialogue, and dialogue leads to trust.
- **Must-haves, then yes.** Fix your must-haves in advance (price, certain terms). During negotiation, say yes to everything else the seller asks for, as long as it's legal, however off-market or silly it seems. What matters is what the seller thinks is important.
- **Between handshake and signing**, the seller is sizing up your integrity. Be careful what you promise and empathetic about their anxiety. **Never renegotiate price after the handshake**, except in the extremely rare case that the seller misrepresented something big. Never make a seller play the waiting game.
- **Secure the A-players early.** Reach out to the target's high-caliber people as soon as you're permitted, and commit to retaining them.
- **Read people for deception.** Jacobs brings Dick Houston, a former CIA investigator and polygraph examiner, into diligence meetings and senior hiring. Houston once saw "neon-red flags" in how a target's management talked about its accounting. Jacobs walked away, and the company was later charged with criminal accounting irregularities.

## Don't expect to force-fit a culture

Wholesale change to a business culture is hard, if not impossible. Buy companies whose cultures are **compatible** with yours, so change happens naturally as the integration proceeds. **Dissimilar can still be compatible**: New Breed and Norbert Dentressangle had different cultures from XPO, and the combination created a new, more dynamic culture.

Incompatibility drains the energy from integration and breeds years of pushback:

- A fast company that buys a sleepy one takes on extra effort it didn't need to invite.
- A results-and-accountability culture clashes with a company that stops answering emails at 4:59 p.m.
- A dishonest culture won't turn honest because you send it a link to your code of ethics. In one waste deal, the seller signed a noncompete, and his daughter promptly opened a competing hauler down the street.

**The top-15 interviews.** During diligence, sit face-to-face with the top 15 or so people in the target, about 90 minutes each, at extra-high priority. You learn what is going on inside, good and bad, and whether these are straightforward people you can trust. You also find the improvement initiatives the integration playbook needs, so execution can start right away.

## Buckle up for the integration

"Everyone has a plan until they get punched in the face" (Mike Tyson). Expect left hooks. United Rentals made six acquisitions in its first three months to give the concept a physical presence. It went on to integrate about 250 businesses, including the industry's largest deal, U.S. Rentals.

1. **Set priorities up front and don't stretch the team too thin.** Otherwise service deteriorates in the acquired business, the legacy business, or both. Competitors poach customers and people during change, and any slip-up leaves you exposed.
2. **Celebrate with a high five, then get back to work.**

### First, cultural integration

Cultural integration is the single most important thing to get right after signing. It is the foremost reason Jacobs's companies could do so many high-speed acquisitions without blowing up.

- **Use the cultural intelligence gathered in diligence** as the basis for credible communication with employees, customers and other stakeholders.
- **Come in respectful, never heavy-handed or my-way-or-the-highway.** Many acquirers earn an "F" for internal communications. Be respectful in words, actions and even thought. It can't be faked. Cultivate gratitude toward the people who sustained the company you bought. Everyone is on the same team.
- **Don't upend practices wholesale, but don't be blindly loyal either.** People who can't meet the business plan's numbers have to go. "I don't know of any way to unlock huge amounts of value without creating a lot of change, but most of it can be positive and all of it can be fair."
- **Cross-fertilize.** Keep the qualities that made the deal attractive. Accept that your own culture will never be perfectly formed, and listen.

### Another first, operational integration

Operational integration depends on **speed and standardization**.

- **Into your technology ecosystem as fast as possible**: one enterprise platform, one HR system, one CRM, one business intelligence database, one internal social media community, one KPI dashboard, one training curriculum, one email system. Every new employee gets the procedures and policies manual, including the code of business ethics.
- **Close the acquisition books cleanly.** Standardize the financial statements, monthly operating reviews and budget format. Move everyone onto your incentive compensation and benefits, so you can benchmark people and locations and instill accountability.
- **Rebrand immediately.** Set internal and vendor schedules for the name, logo and signage. Mismatched business cards and nonstandard job titles look amateur. The ideal: a visitor can't tell whether a site was opened from scratch or acquired ten years ago, or one.
- Then move through the shared services: IT, finance, accounting, sales, marketing, procurement, HR. Do it seamlessly, because the customers you acquired are watching daily.
- **Lead with the big, mission-critical parts.** Don't run two different businesses. Aim for clean numbers, fast data and consistent information channels.

### Overorganize

Integration has a mystique, but it is all quantifiable: hundreds or thousands of tasks.

- **Assign each task to one individual, not a working group**, accountable for delivering it on time.
- **Set a monitoring cadence**: daily or weekly, less often for slow tasks. That removes confusion about progress and about who has the wheel.
- **Update the playbook after every integration**, for continuous improvement toward "wash, rinse, repeat." Each company's integrations were easier than the last: experience, better technology, and not repeating old mistakes.

### Set up early feedback loops

Acquired employees, managers and even executives have often never been asked what they would do to improve the company. Ask them, through whatever fits the size of the employee base and the timeline: surveys, town halls, one-on-one interviews, group meetings, internal social media. Asking is a form of respect. It dispels the insecurity of being acquired, and it lets you say clearly that this is a results-focused company where everyone is accountable and everyone succeeds or fails together.

**The opening questions**:

- What's the business doing well, that we'd be crazy to change?
- What's the business doing not so well, that we'd be crazy not to change?
- What's your best idea to improve the business?
- What's something that could be made more profitable? Or more customer-friendly? Or better for the workplace environment?

Document the avalanche of answers and discuss them at length as a senior leadership team. Make clear this is no one-and-done HR/PR stunt, and never shoot from the hip with big changes. The loops also show you the talent pool the deal brought in, and sometimes that talent is the main coup of the acquisition. Keep the key talent. Be bold in identifying the people who aren't moving the company forward, and offer them a generous exit package.

### Don't promise what you can't deliver

New employees size you up on first impressions. Are you honest? For real? Competent? Winners? Do you understand their kind of business? Do you want them to succeed? What is the new CEO like? Many decide early whether to stay, so get the messaging exactly right.

- **The one promise.** Jacobs promises new teams only one thing, 100 percent: despite his best efforts he will mess some things up. He asks them to give him a break and time to get it right, and to tell him when he's messing up, because he'll listen. Everyone is on the same team now.
- **Promise to consider, not to comply.** Don't promise to act on every suggestion. Promise to take each one seriously, think hard about it, and share it with the rest of the team. Crowdsourced answers conflict. Ask a group about pricing and you'll hear raise it, lower it, and leave it alone, each driven by the person's own volume, revenue or margin. Taking everyone's advice "leads to nothing but quicksand."

### Forget sleep

Before committing to high-level M&A, weigh what rapid-fire cultural, operational and corporate integration will demand of you personally. In 13 months, XPO put more than $7 billion into its three largest acquisitions: Norbert Dentressangle, New Breed and Con-way. It ran the three **integrations simultaneously, not sequentially**, because sequential integration means repeatedly doing and undoing shared services, IT systems and branding. The result was one cohesive company with a single org chart.

That meant 14-hour days on the "triple tracks of strategic execution, M&A integration, and daily operations," with no lapse in customer service or sales. The results: New Breed became the cornerstone of GXO, Con-way's LTL EBITDA nearly tripled, and Norbert's profit doubled in three years. "Sleep is overrated—and... it's actually more fun to be awake."

## Ego is the deal-killer

The CEO of Hertz, the equipment-rental leader that had taken 37 years to reach the $1 billion in revenue United Rentals passed in 13 months, invited Jacobs to lunch. He scolded him for costing Hertz its tagline as the largest equipment rental company in the world and predicted United Rentals would go bankrupt. Jacobs answered: go out there and beat me; win your tagline back. The rival left steaming and went on to find fault with every acquisition that might have put Hertz back in the race. Decades later, United Rentals' forecast EBITDA was more than four times that of the Hertz unit's successor. Treat competition for customer dollars as business, never as something personal.
