# Case studies: seven designs, one format

Brooks analyses designs in five media in a single format. Use it for any design retrospective or case study:

1. **Highlights and peculiarities**: why this case is worth studying; the bold decision or decisions; the budgeted resource.
2. **Introduction and context**: place, owners, designers, dates, the situation at the start.
3. **Objectives**: the primary goal, other objectives, and objectives *discovered* along the way.
4. **Opportunities**.
5. **Constraints**, plus explicitly listed **non-constraints**.
6. **Design decisions**, each with its **corollaries**, meaning the further decisions it forces.
7. **False starts**, and changes made before, during and after construction.
8. **Assessment**: firmness, usefulness and delight.
9. **What I would change if I "threw one away."**
10. **General lessons learned.**

## Beach house "View/360" (house, 1972–1997)

**Bold decision.** Place the house as close to the ocean as the warranty-deeded lot allowed, about 40 ft forward of the neighbors. It was a risk.

**Budgeted resource.** Inches of oceanfront, for view and breeze. The 55 ft available (660 inches) was rationed room by room, by priority:
- The Living Room took the southeast corner.
- The bedrooms sat forward over the dining room and kitchen, so the breeze reached the sleepers.
- The Guest Room went front, as a reading retreat.

Room widths followed the rule "minimum room width equals door width plus bed length."

**Decisions and their corollaries:**
- **Build over a relaxed period.** Put up a dried-in, campable shell; spend the cash on square footage and windows; let the family finish the interior.
- **Turn the house sideways** to exploit the 75-ft lot width. Corollary: a custom plan, not a book exemplar.
- **A Tower glazed on four sides.** Corollary: the roof pitch is limited so the ridge stays below the Tower windowsill.
- **Catch the breeze rather than install central air.** Corollaries: every bedroom gets ocean frontage, and moisture and salt get everywhere, which drives the interior paneling and the omission of carpets.
- **The Tower stair** was settled by a reasoning chain: front of the house, to the Living Room, to its west wall, to a square footprint, to a spiral, to wood because of the salt, to featuring it as sculpture. That last step was serendipity: a cramping constraint became spatial art.

**False start.** A breaking-wave roof drew the structural engineer's comment: "If you can design a house to keep the rain out, boys, you will have done well."

**Mistakes:**
- **The West Deck** spent 42 inches of the critical frontage and was rarely used. A later decision to put showers under the house removed its reason to exist: "I should have rethought the deck decision when the shower decision was made."
- **The pilings** were not placed under the centers of weight, so loads were unequal and settling uneven. Nobody "carefully compared the main-floor plan and the piling plan." The westernmost row sat under the deck edge instead of the wall.
- **Casement windows** with steel cranks needed replacing every 5 years on the spray side: "Inadequate weighting of maintenance in a long-life project."
- **Unexploited opportunities.** Changes made during construction improved delight, but not every opportunity was taken.

**Assessment.** The house came through three hurricanes. The open plan, the spiral stair and the Tower study delight. Its rationality is a private delight; the engineer called it "the damned-logical beach house." Unplanned uses emerged, among them buffet serving and the dining room as a second living room.

**Lessons:**
1. Check a professional architect's work very carefully, and ask for the rationale.
2. Inspect often and thoroughly during construction.
3. Think hard about all aspects of maintenance; a successful design is maintained a long time.

## House wing addition (house, 1987–1992)

**Bold decisions:**
- **"Defer budget constraints; design for function; then value-engineer."** It was made halfway through, when nothing was working. Translating the budget into 1,000 square feet had inhibited thinking, and dropping it "radically freed up our thinking." The same holds for graphics systems: "the best way to make a cost-effective application system is to make an effective one, then cost-reduce it."
- **Move the Master Bedroom into the midst of the public spaces.** It was decided late, from a low-frequency use case.

**Key decision.** Buy a 5-ft strip of land from the neighbor, which was cheaper and faster than seeking a variance.

**The design program** listed:
- explicit **non-constraints**: budget not absolute, resale value irrelevant, plenty of land, plenty of design time;
- **discovered objectives**: a student group of about 40 meeting biweekly, and their roughly 40 coats.

**Partitioning the problem** into three almost separate problems "proved liberating; all later design used it."

**Convergence through use cases.** The coats question surfaced when the design had congealed enough to run use cases in detail. The coats had gone on the Guest Room bed, and that room had vanished from the design. Keeping the Guest Room in the west end as the Master Bedroom made the East End redesign unnecessary. Two corollaries followed: Nancy's Study became the westward extension, and the Music Room went north. Then other things fell into place:
- a 12-ft sliding door joined the Living Room and Music Room;
- one porch gable both marked the main entrance and hid an awkward ceiling joint, one decision solving two problems.

**Late-caught issues.** During construction, Fred's Study windows were lengthened when their view turned out to be treetops. Plans and elevations would never reveal that, "but … a virtual-environment simulation would have shown." Separately, a draftsman's misaligned foundation forced the main floor to be enlarged by 1 ft.

**Assessment.** "The redesigned house turned out to meet needs that we didn't know we had... this is usually, not exceptionally, the case for products." Examples: the sun porch became a meeting venue, and the music room took recitals for 30–50.

**Lessons:**
1. Spend time on design.
2. Talk many times, at length, with the principal users, showing them prototypes they can understand.
3. Run lots of use scenarios.
4. Double-check the work of professionals, and make sure you understand it.

## Kitchen remodeling (room, 1995–1996)

**Bold decisions:**
- **Move the exterior wall**, which transformed the design.
- **Cut a door from the kitchen to the living room** through 8 in of brick, which transformed traffic.

**Budgeted resource.** Width. The tightest pinch stacked a table (≥30 in), a passage (≥24 in), a sink island (≥24 in), sink-to-stove clearance (≥36 in, which mock-ups showed must be 44 in because of pull-outs) and the stove and counter (≥27 in). The total, at least 12 ft 11 in, exceeded the 12 ft available.

**Searching the tree.** Months went into moving the stairs, of which the only workable form was an exterior spiral tower. Concluding "that all 'move the stairs' alternatives were unworkable or unacceptable was a critical moment, radically narrowing the field." This was Simon's tree search in action: explore instances, go up a level, rule out the branch. Exterior mock-ups then showed that pushing the wall out 24 in looked better than a bay window.

**Constraints stated as function, not placement.** The North Hall closets' functions and sizes had to be kept, but not their location. Thirty years of use scenarios lay behind them, which a wealth of precedent kitchen designs could not have revealed.

**Tools compared:**
- sketches, rationalized in layered CAD;
- a design log;
- cardboard models, richer than isometric drawings;
- **full-scale mock-ups** (mattress boxes for the wall; tables and sawhorses for counters, with kitchen scenarios run at various spacings), which found minimum tolerable clearances;
- a **virtual-environment walk-through**, which showed that hanging cabinets over the island broke the visual space and that a hanging lamp intruded.

Mock-ups and the virtual environment each delivered value the other did not.

**Lessons:**
1. The kitchen is the most important room and rewards extensive design.
2. The regrets were minor, because the designers were the users and had plenty of time to test pseudo-prototypes (mock-ups and the virtual environment) against extensive use cases.
3. Very wide consultation with friends yielded crucial ideas, including the basic configuration.
4. Full-scale mock-ups with use scenarios are invaluable. They are worth their cost for intensively used or widely replicated spaces.
5. The virtual environment gave information beyond plans and mock-ups, especially about visual space and feel.

## System/360 architecture (computer family, 1961–1964)

**Boldest decision.** Drop development of all six existing product lines for one new line. Other bold decisions:
- **Strict upward and downward binary compatibility** under one architecture.
- **The 8-bit byte**, which obsoleted existing I/O. It was the biggest and most hotly debated decision.

**Key decisions:**
- **A design competition.** A six-month stack architecture failed at the low end, and Amdahl's design competition produced the base-register design.
- **24-bit addresses**, adopted reluctantly. Branch and Link inadvertently used the upper 8 bits: "a clear example of the danger of team designs. I had failed to indoctrinate the whole team strongly enough with our vision for future expansion, and none of the reviews caught this mistake."
- **A standard I/O interface**, which later enabled weekend model upgrades without changing I/O or software.
- **Error detection** was mandated though customers did not ask for it, because people trust the answers.
- **Microprogrammed implementation** was mandated unless an engineering manager could show a 33% performance/cost advantage for conventional logic. It made emulation of earlier machines possible, and 1401 emulation won the 1964 product fight inside IBM.

**Mistakes:**
- No virtual memory, which was "expert designers going wrong in a big way."
- Decimal arithmetic: its hardware cost was small, but its software cost and conceptual complexity were substantial.
- Failing to see that the I/O channel was just another computer.

**Assessment.** Firmness: still compatible after 45 years, against a predicted 25. Delight: strict compatibility "imposed a strict discipline that protected the low end from functional deficiency and the high end from excess," like a strict page limit.

**Lessons:**
1. Allow plenty of project time for design.
2. Multiple concurrent implementations protect the architecture from bad compromises. With one implementation, "it is always easier, cheaper, and faster to change the manual rather than the machine."
3. When the first design runs aground, a design competition brings concurrence, spotlights crucial differences and lifts morale.
4. For a totally new design, establish early the metrics for performance and essential properties, and approximate cost surrogates such as bits of register.
5. Market-forecasting methods are built for follow-on products, so the designers of a new product must get the forecasters on board early.

## OS/360 (software, 1961–1965)

**Bold decisions:**
- one software package for the entire range of machines;
- mandated disk residence;
- the system, not an operator, in control;
- multitasking;
- device-independent I/O through *access methods*, which Brooks calls "the most important innovation in OS/360."

**Weaknesses of design:**
- "OS/360 is too rich." Disk residence removed the size constraint "that had disciplined earlier OS designers—we put in many functional goodies of marginal usefulness."
- It had two debugging systems, the best batch debugging ever, obsolete from birth.
- System generation was wondrously flexible and onerous; a few standard packages would have served.
- Control blocks were shared system-wide. Parnas's information hiding would have avoided the grief.
- JCL (see `style-and-exemplars.md`).

**Weaknesses of process:**
- It should have been written in PL/I, the best high-level language then available, with careful training in writing good PL/I, rather than in PLS, a syntactically sugared assembler. It would have been just as fast, far cleaner, more reliable and built more swiftly.
- The architects should have kept rigid control of all interfaces, with every external declaration *included* from libraries rather than crafted anew.

**Lessons:**
1. Give the system architect full authority over the design.
2. Take the time necessary for sound design and prototyping, whatever the schedule pressure. The project would have finished sooner, not later.

## The book *Computer Architecture* (book, ~1971–1997)

**Bold decisions:**
- **A narrow, precise definition of architecture**, which made *program compatibility* definable.
- **A zoo of 30 machines** described in one standardized format.
- **Executable simulators.** Building them forced close scrutiny and precision.
- **A matrix organization**: each design decision treated twice, systematically by domain and in the context of each machine.

**Sequence.** "In any expository writing, sequence is the hardest single design decision. The general graph of interrelated concepts must be cut to a tree structure, so it can be mapped onto the linear structure of the text." The book used both orders, conceptual and machine by machine, with cross-references.

**Mistake.** The authors had unlimited time, and "This was a major mistake." The book arrived too late for maximum influence.

**Lessons:**
1. A less ambitious work sooner is more useful.
2. "Book writing has logarithmic convergence." The last uncertain facts, glitched figures and obscure references take an inordinate share of the time.

## Triangle Universities Computation Center (organization, 1964–1992)

**Bold decision.** Three universities (Duke, NC State and UNC-Chapel Hill) co-own and operate one high-performance computation center. Pooling exploited the quadratic performance/price curve: "spending *n* times as much typically bought at least *n*² times as much computing capability." There were no organizational models to follow.

**Budgeted resource.** "The budgeted commodity in the design was power": how to protect each owner's distinct interests while enabling efficient decisions.

**Constraints:**
- operating decisions had to be fast, and upgrades were frequent;
- Duke, the smallest and only private owner, was wary of being outvoted by the two state campuses, and NC State was the heaviest user;
- the center's budget needed stability, while the owners had long budget lead times;
- the CEOs had much at stake but little time;
- some individuals were fractious and stubborn.

**Design decisions:**
- **Careful separation of policy from operations.** A board meeting monthly set policy; a staff under a President/Director ran operations, with liaisons to the campus systems programmers and to the campus computing center directors.
- **A ten-member board**: three from each campus, plus the Director.
- **Voting alternatives considered**: unanimous consent of members; simple majority of members; unanimous consent of institutions; majority of institutions.
  - Unanimous consent was rejected as too hard; avoiding the need for consensus greatly eased decisions.
  - Normal decisions went by a majority of *members*, not of institutions, to make the board think as a unit.
- **"Issues of Fundamental Importance"** were spelled out in the By-Laws: selecting or discharging the Director; an annual budget increase over 10%; modifying the Articles or By-Laws. These required a unanimous vote by institutions, with two-thirds of each owner's delegation deciding its vote. Brooks points to this as the crown-jewels principle: constrain only the matters of fundamental importance.
- **An escape hatch.** Any owner could declare any issue fundamental, by a "suitably onerous" route: its representatives tabled the issue for a month, then its CEO elevated it by letter.
- **Rationing power.** Power was rationed between staff and board, between majority and minority (by institutions and fractious individuals), and between academic and administrative users. The chairmanship rotated with two-year terms.

**Assessment.** The center lasted 18 years, through two Directors and three generations of mainframe. The escape hatch was never used, but it gave great psychological comfort. Divisions ran by area of responsibility, not by school.

**Lessons:**
1. Careful, explicit identification at the start of the vital interests of each partner, and of the director, sped agreement on the organization's mechanics.
2. An ultimate appeal procedure, though not easy to invoke, "assured each participant that it wouldn't be trodden upon."
3. Get each partner's internal interests represented in its delegation: finance, campus centers, faculty users.
4. A governing board easily becomes a rubber stamp; meeting monthly prevented it.
5. Some CEOs fill board meetings with presentations rather than real issues, and few use board members severally as advisers. Brooks calls that a real loss.
