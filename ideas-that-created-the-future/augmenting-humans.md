# Augmenting humans

Papers 11, 20, 22 and 24: Bush's memex, Licklider's symbiosis, Engelbart's framework for augmenting intellect, and Sutherland's Sketchpad.

## 11. Bush, *As We May Think* (1945)

**Set out to.** Ask what scientists, thrown off stride by a war that was not a scientist's war, should do next. Bush's answer is to make the growing record of knowledge usable.

**The problem.** "There is a growing mountain of research", and "we are being bogged down today as specialization extends". The investigator is staggered by thousands of findings, and "the effort to bridge between disciplines is correspondingly superficial". Our methods of transmitting and reviewing research are generations old. Mendel's laws were lost for a generation because his publication never reached the few who could extend it, and "truly significant attainments become lost in the mass of the inconsequential". The trouble is not that we publish too much: "publication has been extended far beyond our present ability to make real use of the record", and we thread the maze with means from the days of square-rigged ships.

Leibniz's calculator failed on economics: building it took more labor than it saved, and its complexity made it unreliable. Babbage was like a Pharaoh given designs for an automobile. Now, with interchangeable parts, typewriters, telephone exchanges and a thermionic tube built for thirty cents, we are in an "age of cheap complex devices of great reliability; and something is bound to come of it."

**Construct.**
- **The record.** "A record if it is to be useful to science, must be continuously extended, it must be stored, and above all it must be consulted."
  - *Recording.* Bush imagines a walnut-sized camera worn on the forehead, taking 3 mm pictures, and dry photography using diazo dyes or electrically sensitive paper.
  - *Compression.* Microphotography at a linear ratio of 100 cuts bulk by a factor of 10,000. That puts the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* in a matchbox, a million-volume library in one end of a desk, and a billion books in a moving van. On microfilm, the *Britannica* would cost a nickel and mail for a cent. "Mere compression, of course, is not enough."
  - *Speech to text.* The Voder (keys to speech) and the Vocoder (speech to keys), combined with the stenotype, suggest a machine that types when talked to.
- **Repetitive thought.** "For mature thought there is no mechanical substitute. But creative thought and essentially repetitive thought are very different things. For the latter there are, and may be, powerful mechanical aids."
  - When facts are combined by established logical processes, the creative part is only the selection of the data and of the process; the manipulation that follows is repetitive and "a fit matter to be relegated to the machine".
  - A mathematician is "primarily an individual who is skilled in the use of symbolic logic on a high plane, and especially he is a man of intuitive judgment in the choice of the manipulative processes he employs". The rest can go to the mechanism.
  - "Whenever thought for a time runs along an accepted groove—there is an opportunity for the machine."
  - Mathematical symbolism "grew like Topsy". A new, probably positional symbolism must come before transformations can be mechanized; then we will "click off arguments on a machine with the same assurance that we now enter sales on a cash register".
- **Selection.** "The prime action of use is selection", and our tools for it are "a stone adze in the hands of a cabinetmaker".
  - *Simple selection* examines every item in turn: the employees in Trenton who know Spanish, or a fingerprint among five million.
  - The *telephone exchange* selects by classes: first digit, then the next.
- **Association.** "Our ineptitude in getting at the record is largely caused by the artificiality of systems of indexing." Items are filed alphabetically or numerically, one place only, and found by descending from subclass to subclass under cumbersome rules. "The human mind does not work that way. It operates by association." Its trails fade, and the machine cannot match its speed, but it can beat the mind on permanence and clarity. "Selection by association, rather than indexing, may yet be mechanized."
- **The memex** is "a device in which an individual stores all his books, records, and communications, and which is mechanized so that it may be consulted with exceeding speed and flexibility. It is an enlarged intimate supplement to his memory."
  - It is a desk with slanting translucent screens, a keyboard, and sets of buttons and levers.
  - Its microfilm store would take hundreds of years to fill at 5000 pages a day. Most contents are bought on microfilm; the user's own notes go in through a transparent platen.
  - A code typed on the keyboard brings up a book's title page. A lever pages forward or back, by one, ten or a hundred pages.
  - Several items can be projected at once, and marginal notes are added with a stylus.
- **Associative indexing.** "A provision whereby any item may be caused at will to select immediately and automatically another. This is the essential feature of the memex. The process of tying two items together is the important thing."
  - *Building a trail.* Name the trail and enter it in the code book. Put two items in adjacent positions and tap a key, and they are joined permanently, by a code word and photocell dots giving the index number of the other item.
  - *Following a trail.* A lever reviews it like turning pages, "as though the physical items had been gathered together from widely separated sources and bound together to form a new book." Any item can be in numerous trails.
  - *Example.* The user follows why the short Turkish bow beat the English long bow in the Crusades: an encyclopedia, a history, the user's comments, a side trail on elasticity, a longhand analysis. Years later the trail serves a discussion of why people resist innovation, and a reproducer photographs the whole trail for a friend's memex.

**Results and prophecies.**
- New encyclopedias will come "with a mesh of associative trails", used by lawyers, patent attorneys, physicians working from analogous case histories, chemists and historians.
- "A new profession of trail blazers, those who find delight in the task of establishing useful trails through the enormous mass of the common record."
- What a disciple inherits from a master includes "the entire scaffolding" of the master's trails.
- "Prophecy based on extension of the known has substance, while prophecy founded on the unknown is only a doubly involved guess." Bush still speculates about intercepting nerve currents directly.
- With a full record, people may "reacquire the privilege of forgetting", confident they can find things again.

**Led to.** Bush built the differential analyzer, an analog and mostly mechanical machine, at MIT and hired Shannon to run it, which led to Shannon's thesis (8). He directed the wartime Office of Scientific Research and Development, and his *Science, the Endless Frontier* led to the NSF. The essay appeared in the *Atlantic* and then, illustrated, in *Life*. Its vision is of "instantaneous, ubiquitous, associative information retrieval". The storage media of the day could not support it, but the functional vision inspired many systems: Licklider (20), Engelbart (22), who read the essay at war's end, and Sutherland (24).

## 20. Licklider, *Man–Computer Symbiosis* (1960)

**Set out to.** "Man–computer symbiosis is an expected development in cooperative interaction between men and electronic computers." It means very close coupling, with two main aims:
1. "to let computers facilitate formulative thinking as they now facilitate the solution of formulated problems";
2. "to enable men and computers to cooperate in making decisions and controlling complex situations without inflexible dependence on predetermined programs".

Men set the goals, formulate hypotheses, determine criteria and perform evaluations. Computers do the routinizable work that prepares the way for insights and decisions.

**Construct.**
- **Symbiosis.** The fig tree is pollinated only by the insect *Blastophaga grossorum*, whose larva lives in its ovary; each depends on the other. Webster defines symbiosis as "living together in intimate association, or even close union, of two dissimilar organisms". No man–computer symbiosis exists yet. The hope is that, in not too many years, "human brains and computing machines will be coupled together very tightly, and that the resulting partnership will think as no human brain has ever thought and process data in a way not approached by the information-handling machines we know today."
- **Between two alternatives.** In the "mechanically extended man", the human supplied initiative, direction and criterion, and machines extended the arm and the eye. Automation now goes further. The men who remain help the machine more than it helps them, doing what is infeasible to automate: "humanly extended machines", semi-automatic systems that fell short of full automation. Neither is symbiotic.

  Licklider concedes "dominance in the distant future of cerebration to machines alone", but expects a fairly long interim of intimate association. An Air Force study put real military thinking by machines alone at 1980. That leaves five years to develop symbiosis and 15 to use it: "The 15 may be 10 or 500, but those years should be intellectually the most creative and exciting in the history of mankind."
- **Why present computers fall short.** They solve preformulated problems by predetermined procedures, with every alternative foreseen in advance. If the user could think a problem through in advance, symbiosis would be unnecessary.

**The time-and-motion study.** In spring and summer 1957, with himself as subject, Licklider timed his own technical thinking. "About 85 per cent of my 'thinking' time was spent getting into a position to think, to make a decision, to learn something I needed to know." He spent more time finding information than digesting it, and hours plotting graphs. In one case he had six determinations of speech intelligibility against speech-to-noise ratio, each defined differently: hours of calculation, then seconds to see the answer. His choices of what to attempt were determined "to an embarrassingly great extent by considerations of clerical feasibility, not intellectual capability."

**Men and computers differ genotypically.** "Men are noisy, narrow-band devices, but their nervous systems have very many parallel and simultaneously active channels." Computers are fast and accurate but do one or a few operations at a time. Men program themselves contingently; computers are single-minded, bound by pre-programming. Men speak redundant languages with 20 to 60 elementary symbols, organized around unitary objects and coherent actions; computers use nonredundant languages of usually two symbols. The dissimilarity is what makes them supplement each other.

**Separable functions.**
- *Men* set goals, supply motivation, formulate hypotheses and ask questions. They think of mechanisms, procedures and models, and remember that someone did relevant work back in 1947 and where it was published. These are "approximate and fallible, but leading, contributions". Men also define criteria, evaluate, handle very-low-probability situations (whose combined probability is often too large to neglect), and fill the gaps in a solution.
- *Computers* convert hypotheses into testable models and test them, answer questions, simulate, carry out procedures, display results, and transform and plot data ("cutting the cake" as the operator specifies). They "carry out the routinizable, clerical operations that fill the intervals between decisions". Statistical inference and decision theory serve the elementary evaluations; diagnosis, pattern matching and relevance recognition take a clearly secondary place.

**Prerequisites.**
1. **Speed mismatch: time-sharing.** A large computer is too fast and costly for one person, so its time must be divided among many users, with protection against "clobbering" anything but one's own program. In 10 or 15 years, Licklider envisions a "thinking center" with library functions. Such centers would be networked by wide-band lines and reached by users over leased wires.
2. **Memory hardware.** Storing an appreciable fraction of the technical literature needs billions of bits. Store the quantitative parts and the citations; "books are among the most beautifully engineered, and human-engineered, components in existence", and the computer should expedite finding them. **Indelible** memory (write once, read indefinitely) and **published** (read-only) memory could be more compact and cheaper.
3. **Memory organization.** Retrieval should work by name and by pattern, faster than serial search. Fredkin's **trie memory** stores both functions and arguments, where common memories store functions at locations named by arguments.
   - *How it works.* An argument enters one character at a time from a standard initial register. Each register has one cell per character of the alphabet, and each cell holds the address of the next register. An end-of-argument marker leads to the function.
   - *Properties.* Retrieval just follows addresses. Arguments with common initial characters share storage, and arguments need not be of the same or specified length. No room is reserved until something is stored. A function can be the argument of another: enter "matrix multiplication" and retrieve the whole program. Examining one level shows the items similar so far: with no citation for Egan, J. P., step back to Egan, James.
   - It brings storage "into resonance with human operators and their predilection to designate things by naming or pointing."
4. **Language,** perhaps the most serious obstacle. FORTRAN, IPL and ALGOL are strides. But "instructions directed to computers specify courses; instructions directed to human beings specify goals." Paraphrasing Wiener, "men bound for Los Angeles try continually to decrease the amount by which they are not yet in the smog." There are two routes to instruction by goals: problem-solving and self-organizing programs, so far only a "demonstration in principle"; and the simpler, earlier route of preprogrammed segments and subroutines called by name in real time, "connected together like the words and phrases of speech".
5. **Input and output,** the least advanced. Typically there is nothing better than an electric typewriter, and nothing approaching "pencil and doodle pad or the chalk and blackboard".
   - *Desk-surface display and control.* Man and computer draw graphs, write notes and equations, and define new symbols on the same surface. The computer reads block capitals and posts them back in precise type. The "other engineer" is "a precise draftsman, a lightning calculator, a mnemonic wizard".
   - *Computer-posted wall display,* for several people controlling interacting vehicles.
   - *Automatic speech production and recognition.* A commander cannot be sent away to learn typing, and it is "dangerous to count on having more than ten minutes". Speech production is easier. Bell Labs and Lincoln Lab report 98 per cent correct recognition of spoken decimal digits. A symbiotic level, about 2000 words (1000 of basic English and 1000 technical), cannot be done now but could be undertaken on a five-year basis.

**Led to.** Licklider was a psychologist of auditory perception who came to "human factors" through Cold War air defense: how could a computer help an operator make split-second decisions about incoming missiles? Lewis notes that video games, virtual reality and cat videos were born of those nuclear-risk concerns. He wrote this at BBN (Bolt Beranek and Newman), which later built the earliest ARPANET gateways, and it owes something to Bush (11). At ARPA from 1962 he helped give birth to the ARPANET; Engelbart's (22) funding came partly through him, and Sutherland (24) succeeded him. Sketchpad realized the symbiosis he predicted.

## 22. Engelbart, *Augmenting Human Intellect: A Conceptual Framework* (1962)

**Set out to.** Give a conceptual framework for a research program. **Augmenting human intellect** means "increasing the capability of a man to approach a complex problem situation, to gain comprehension to suit his particular needs, and to derive solutions to problems." The capability is a mixture: more rapid comprehension, better comprehension, comprehension where a situation was too complex before, speedier and better solutions, and solutions to problems that seemed insoluble.

It applies to diplomats, executives, scientists, attorneys and designers, on problems lasting twenty minutes or twenty years. It is not a set of isolated clever tricks but "a way of life in an integrated domain where hunches, cut-and-try, intangibles, and the human 'feel for a situation' usefully co-exist with powerful concepts, streamlined terminology and notation, sophisticated methods, and high-powered electronic aids." The complexity of problems grows faster than our means of meeting them. Engelbart expects gains comparable to "those made in personal geographic mobility since horseback and sailboat days".

**The augmented architect.** A working station has a display screen about three feet on a side, controlled by a computer (the "clerk"), a small keyboard and other devices.
- The architect is designing on a steep hillside site. A perspective view shows the roadway, symbolic trees and utility tie points; a pointer picks two points and gets their distance and elevation.
- A reference line makes the excavation appear. Specifications are entered: a six-inch slab floor, twelve-inch concrete walls eight feet high. The structure takes shape, and handbook data comes up as needed.
- The architect views a slice of the interior from the roadway, and finds that one window reflects glare onto the roadway between 6 and 6:30 on midsummer mornings.
- Occupants' daily sequences combine to show traffic, congestion and peak load on the utilities.
- The working lists grow into "an ever-more-detailed, interlinked structure, which represents the maturing thought behind the actual design." The design and its thought structure go on tape as a design manual, where builder and client can append notes for others.

**Construct.**
- **Process hierarchy.** Every process of thought or action is made of sub-processes: a pencil stroke of muscle movements, a letter of strokes, a plan of letters. There is no point seeking an ultimate bottom. The individual's **repertoire** of process capabilities is "like a tool kit", and it forms an inter-knit hierarchy, the **repertoire hierarchy**. Its capabilities are of three kinds:
  - **explicit-human**: within the human integument;
  - **explicit-artifact**: done by artifacts without human intervention;
  - **composite**: hierarchies of both.
- **H-LAM/T**: "Human using Language, Artifacts, Methodology, in which he is Trained". The whole system has the capability and performs the process. Language shows the effect: once the process of packaging information formally and distributing it exists, so do the concept and its name, *memorandum*.
- **Basic perspective.** Effective individuals are already considerably "augmented". Improving them is a system-engineering problem: study H-LAM/T as an interacting whole. A change in language, artifacts or methodology matters only through its use in a process, and a new capability anywhere in the hierarchy makes latent changes practical elsewhere. No analytic theory is adequate, so the method must be experimental.

  Everyday work is composite. It involves external manipulation of symbols, and distinctly human activities: playing with forms and relationships, cut-and-try multiple-pass development, listing and then rearranging. Even an artifact that only duplicates and rearranges text before one's eyes would change the whole repertoire hierarchy. Change is normally evolutionary; an active research effort could make it a "guided evolutionary process". A personal digital computer is "more radical innovation".
- **The source of intelligence** is "a very clever organizational hierarchy". In a computer the levels run down from problem-oriented language statements (ALGOL, COBOL), through lesser functions and machine commands, to hardware units, then gates and flip-flops, then transistors and diodes, and finally physics. Intelligence is "elusively distributed throughout a hierarchy of functional processes". **Synergism**, cooperative action whose total effect exceeds the sum of the parts, is "our most likely candidate for representing the actual source of intelligence".
- **Intelligence amplification.** The term is Ashby's, and Engelbart first rejected it, then accepted it. Native intelligence is not increased. Instead the system "amplified the intelligence of the human by organizing his intellectual capabilities into higher levels of synergistic structuring." The H-LAM/T system has the amplified intelligence; the LAM/T augmentation means are the amplifier. Natural evolution works by the same synergistic structuring, so "the development of 'artificial intelligence' has been going on for centuries."
- **Two-domain systems (Fig 22.1).** Human processes and artifact processes meet across the **man–artifact interface**, each side with a strip of matching processes, and both connected to the outside world. "Man–machine interface" is a recent term; the man–artifact interface has existed for centuries. Matching processes couple the deeper processes on each side: finger motions drive typewriter key linkages, so that deeper human processes choose a word and deeper artifact processes imprint ink marks.

**The hypothetical system.** Joe, an experienced user several years ahead, gives a demonstration. He has the computer do some of his symbol manipulation "so that he can use more powerful concepts and concept-manipulation techniques".
- Two display screens sit side by side, almost horizontal like a drafting table.
- Each hand has a keyset about two feet apart, and a light pen hangs in the air for each hand.
- With one hand on a keyset and the other on a pen, images change dynamically, for free and rapid "shaping" of ideas.
- Abbreviations and symbols mix with words, and the parts of a statement are not laid end to end.
- Joe explains that the new higher-level capabilities cannot be shown without the new low-level ones they rest on, which no one would think "powerful" in themselves.

**Conclusions.**
1. "Man's problem-solving capability represents possibly the most important resource possessed by a society."
2. "We can begin developing powerful and economically feasible augmentation systems on the basis of what we now know and have." There is no need to wait for understanding of mental processes or smarter computers.
3. Action should start now, on an aggressive scale, in many research communities, within a dynamic discipline that cycles through better understanding, better means and better systems. "We spend great sums for disciplines aimed at understanding and harnessing nuclear power. Why not consider developing a discipline aimed at understanding and harnessing 'neural power'?"

**Led to.** Engelbart read Bush's essay (11) in the Navy at war's end and spent his career putting flesh on it, at SRI in Menlo Park with ARPA funding, some under Licklider (20). Some parts never took hold, like the one-handed keyset of five keys giving 31 combinations. Others, above all the mouse, had enormous influence, and distributed collaborative work was later rediscovered.

In "the mother of all demos" at the 1968 Fall Joint Computer Conference in San Francisco he showed the mouse, networking, videoconferencing, hyperlinks, collaborative text editing and windowing. The work never became mainstream: SRI sold the lab a decade later, and it did not flourish. Engelbart won the Turing Award in 1997 "for an inspiring vision of the future of interactive computing and the invention of key technologies to help realize this vision."

## 24. Sutherland, *Sketchpad: A Man–Machine Graphical Communication System* (1963)

**Set out to.** Make drawing a medium of communication with a computer. "In the past, we have been writing letters to rather than conferring with our computers"; typed statements are cumbersome for the shapes of parts or the connections of a circuit. The aim was to learn which facilities are useful by building them. Sutherland was not computerizing drafting tools by brute force, but looking for simple new facilities with wide use, including unforeseen uses.

**Construct.**
- **Equipment.** Lincoln Laboratory's TX-2, with push buttons, toggle switches, a **light pen**, a display, and four knobs to rotate and magnify the visible part (Fig 24.2). The pen both positions parts and points at them. Buttons name commands ("draw", "move", "erase", "copy", "delete"). There is no written language except legends. A plotter inks the drawings.
- **Primitives.** Straight line segments and circle arcs. Symbols are built of segments, arcs and previously defined symbols.
- **What a computer drawing does differently.** It offers accuracy, ease and speed of erasing, but above all "the ability to move drawing parts around on a computer drawing without the need to erase them".
- **Three capabilities**, which with the light-pen language make "a system of extraordinary power":
  - **Subpictures**: arbitrary symbols, used as often as desired and defined in terms of simpler ones. "Any change in the definition of a symbol is at once seen wherever that symbol appears."
  - **Constraints**: relating parts in any computable way. The built-in atomic constraints make lines vertical, horizontal, parallel or perpendicular, put points on lines or circles, keep symbols upright, aligned or of equal size, and relate symbols to points and lines. They grew from five to seventeen kinds in about two days. "Conditions can involve anything computable", even the forces in the members of a truss bridge.
  - **Definition copying**: building complex relationships from combinations of atomic constraints. For example, both parallel and of equal length; or a horizontal line becoming a dimension line with arrowheads and the correct length number.
- **Topology.** Sketchpad stores how the drawing is tied together as well as how it looks. Move a polygon vertex and both adjacent sides move; move a symbol and the lines attached to it stay attached. Connections recorded while sketching can feed programs that need topology, such as circuit simulators. The **ring structure** lets topological information be processed "with no searching at all".

**The introductory example (Figs 24.1, 24.5).**
1. Press "draw". A line stretches like a rubber band from its start to the pen. Draw six lines, ending near the start of the first, which makes the end "lock on" exactly. A flick of the pen ends the line, leaving an irregular hexagon (A).
2. Press "circle center", point to the center, then to a point fixing the radius, and draw an arc (B).
3. Press "move" on each corner and flick it onto the circle (C, D); the corners stay on the circle thereafter.
4. To make the sides equal, point to one side, press "copy", point to another and flick: "make this line equal in length to that line". Five such statements, with the toggle switch on, make the computer satisfy every condition, giving a regular inscribed hexagon (E).
5. "Delete" erases the circle (F).
6. Designate the six corners as attachment points and file the hexagon. On a fresh "sheet of paper", call up seven hexagons as subpictures, six around a central one (G). Each is positioned whole by the pen and rotated or scaled with the knobs; its internal shape is fixed.
7. Fasten two corners of each outer hexagon to the inner one, and the computer repositions them (H).
8. Treat the group as a symbol and call it seven times on another sheet to get the pattern of Fig 24.1. All of this takes "less than five minutes".

**Implications.** "The Sketchpad drawing itself is entirely different from the trail of carbon left on a piece of paper." "It is this ability to store information relating the parts of a drawing to each other that makes Sketchpad most useful."
- **Linkage (Fig 24.6).** Drawn in minutes, with constraints holding the member lengths constant. Rotating the short central link moves one end of a dotted line vertically, and a number constrained to show that line's length reads 1087 in all four positions: a straight-line linkage.
- **Pattern (Fig 24.7).** Change the master hexagon to a semicircle and the whole pattern becomes fish scales on the same lattice.
- **Circuits.** All transistor symbols come from one master, so a change appears at once in all of them. The computer "knows" a transistor is meant, and moving a component moves its wiring.
- **The design process.** Constructing a drawing is "itself a model of the design process". Point and line locations model the design variables, and geometric constraints model the design constraints. Satisfying them models the good designer meeting the conditions of materials and cost. Adding design conditions to the drawing helps "not only in arriving at a nice looking drawing, but also in arriving at a sound design."

**Present usefulness**, after about a hundred hours of experience, in four areas:
1. Small changes to existing drawings. A library of stored drawings can be reused, with constraints carrying design conditions so that changing a critical part changes the related parts.
2. Understanding operations that can be described graphically, such as linkages, or crystal structures built from a basic element.
3. Topological input for circuit simulators.
4. Highly repetitive drawings, with subpictures within subpictures, as in memory arrays and micrologic.

**History.** "Had I the work to do again, I could start afresh with the sure knowledge that generic structure, separation of subroutines into general purpose ones applying to all types of picture parts and ones specific to particular types of picture parts, and unlimited applicability of functions (e.g. anything should be moveable) would more than recompense the effort." Sutherland had to follow a "stumbling trail". He hopes future workers will "grasp the power of generality at once and strive for it". "It is to be hoped that future work will far surpass my effort."

**Led to.** Sutherland learned the concise language of blueprints as a boy, covering his schoolbooks with his civil-engineer father's discarded ones, but lacked the patience to draw. With time on the TX-2 as an MIT graduate student, he programmed the computer to help. The dissertation was written under Shannon, "and thus was born the entire field of computer graphics and interactive computing".

The TX-2 was transistorized and had 64K 36-bit words. Its display was a circular CRT whose only instruction flashed a point at given coordinates; there were no raster, line-drawing or character displays. Software flashed successive points repeatedly, redrawing the whole image in under a thirtieth of a second to avoid flicker. The light pen was a photocell on a wire, located by matching the moment it fired to the point being displayed.

The durable contribution was human–computer symbiosis as Licklider (20) predicted. Sketchpad was the first computer-aided design program. Its constraint satisfaction and object hierarchies sowed the seeds of non-procedural programming and object-oriented design (39).

Sutherland went on to build the first virtual-reality system (Harvard, 1968): a head-mounted display tethered to the ceiling, showing a wire-frame cube floating in space. He also built the graphics for the first practical flight simulator at Evans and Sutherland. In 1964, fresh from graduate school, he replaced Licklider at ARPA, where their visionary funding stimulated computer science research across the U.S.
