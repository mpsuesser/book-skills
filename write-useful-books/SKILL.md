# Write Useful Books

Rob Fitzpatrick's method for nonfiction that grows for years on reader recommendations, with no heavy marketing and no large author platform. Design the book like a product, test it with real readers at every step, and treat marketing as a temporary job: seeding the first few hundred readers. **"The goal of book marketing is to stop needing to do it."** The method covers the process and the product of the book. It does not cover prose craft, for which Fitzpatrick defers to Zinsser's *On Writing Well*.

The through-line: **stop writing in secret.** The typical author writes, rewrites, and edits a full manuscript and only then finds out whether readers want it and whether it works, when fixing big problems means redoing everything. Find (and fix) the book's mistakes *before* launch, while a fix costs an afternoon.

## Pleasure-givers vs. problem-solvers

Classify the book first. Nonfiction divides by its purpose to the reader:

- **Pleasure-givers** ("interesting", "fascinating", "beautiful") are crafted like art, as a solitary act of genius. They become hits through PR momentum or a famous, well-platformed author: winner-take-all.
- **Problem-solvers** ("useful", "actionable", "clarifying") are designed and built like products, through reader-centric testing and refinement. Their success is more meritocratic, and they can be *proven* valuable before publication.

"Problem" is loose. A problem-solver delivers any tangible outcome: achieve a goal or undergo a process; answer a question or understand a concept; improve a skill or develop a toolkit; resolve a fear or inspire a change; adjust a perspective or improve a life. This method is for problem-solvers. Nearly all conventional "writing a book" advice is about pleasure-givers and is irrelevant, or harmful, for a problem-solver. (Gladwell's storytelling and suspense work because his books are pleasure-givers; don't emulate that style for a problem-solver.)

Nearly any topic can be reframed as a problem-solver around a clear promise. Prescriptive how-tos are the obvious case, but *The Artist's Way* (daily journaling exercises to reconnect with creativity), *The Dictator's Handbook* (a mental model for politicians' bizarre decisions), *Quiet* (introverts leveraging their strengths), and *The Personal MBA* (a concise reference to a full MBA) are problem-solvers too. Sarah Bakewell repurposed Montaigne's essays into *How to Live*. The Durants distilled their eleven-volume *Story of Civilization* into the 100-page *The Lessons of History*, which outsold all their other books combined "because it offered readers an *outcome* instead of just a story."

## DEEP: what a problem-solver needs to be recommended

1. **Desirable**: readers want what it promises.
2. **Effective**: it delivers real results for the average reader.
3. **Engaging**: it is front-loaded with value, has high value-per-page, and feels rewarding to read.
4. **Polished**: it is professionally written and presented.

**Earlier requirements dominate later ones.** If nobody wants it or it doesn't work, engagement and prose don't matter. Effectiveness is where nonfiction most often misses. A messy-but-effective book still gets recommended ("terribly written and full of typos … but it's got something inside that's just too important to miss"). A polished book that doesn't work never is. The test of Effectiveness: six months later, ask a reader what they are doing differently because of the book.

**Pseudoteaching** is the failure to watch for. It is the lesson that looks flawless to an observer (clear, energetic, coherent) while students fail to learn. The knowledge fails to **cross the air gap**, from author to book or from book to reader. Measure the reader's outcome, not the author's performance.

By the end of beta reading the manuscript is a **DEE_ book** (Desirable, Effective, Engaging), ready for the missing 'P'.

## Scope

**Scope = Promise + Reader profile + Who it's not for + What it won't cover**, plus recommendability and longevity as the final two considerations. Like a business's executive summary, it is the briefest description of what the book is, who it's for, and why they'll pay. A book "is under no obligation to start from the beginning, to serve everybody, or to cover everything. Pick the piece you're best at, for the people you care most deeply about serving, at the moment in their journey where you can really help them."

Three lines of questioning strengthen a scope:

1. **The promise.** When someone buys and reads it, what are they trying to achieve, and why are they bothering? After finishing, what's different in their life, work, or worldview?
2. **"What does my ideal reader already know and believe?"** If they already believe the topic matters, cut (or hugely reduce) the sections convincing them. If they know the basics, skip those.
3. **Who it's *not* for, and what it's *not* doing.** Without this, the author writes into rabbit holes, narrow topics only a subset of readers care about. Deciding who it isn't for clips those tangential branches.

Scopes are fixed by *deleting*, not adding. *The Mom Test* began as "Everyone interested in business should read this book", then explained startups, then Customer Development, then argued that talking to customers is worthwhile, and only then taught how: low value-per-page, guaranteed to flounder. Deciding that readers who didn't already believe in Customer Development weren't the audience gave: "If you're a tech entrepreneur struggling to run useful customer interviews, this book will help you understand why the conversations are going wrong and how to run them properly." That told him who it was for, which tone to take, what to include and leave out, and let it "start with a bang." The author's own interests count too: *The Workshop Survival Guide* assumed readers already had a workshop to teach and called the business side (tickets, clients, reputation) out of scope.

**Decide who it isn't for.** A promise is meaningless until paired with a type of reader. The best TV for everyone is impossible; the best TV for the hearing impaired, or for executive boardrooms, is instantly tractable. Writing for too many reader types means "every chapter that the amateur adores, the expert endures, and vice versa." **"Nobody recommends the second-best solution"**, so become the best for someone. "Far better to be loved by someone than ignored by everyone." Filter all criticism through who the book is for: a beginners' book needn't worry that experts find it elementary.

**Make a clear promise and put it on the cover**, in (or strongly implied by) the title or subtitle. The secret to a five-star rating: "be clear enough about what your book is promising that people can decide they don't need it." Bad reviews come from too broad a promise luring the wrong readers. *How to Stay Alive in the Woods* sold 800k copies against arguably better books: from the cover you can guess the promise, judge its relevance, and know which friends to tell. Specificity is a feature: focused titles like *Single-Handed Sailing* are valuable "not in spite of their specificity, but because of it." To find the words, describe the book in one or two sentences whenever someone asks what you're working on, then "shut up and listen to them completely misinterpret". Revise each time. When people get it immediately, with no clarifying or correcting, put those words on the cover.

Document the scope as a current best-guess hypothesis. Reader data will revise it.

## The recommendation loop

You don't *hope* for recommendations; you *design* for them. A **recommendation loop** is triggered when someone complains about, or seeks a solution to, a high-priority problem, and someone else replies "Oh, I know the solution to your problem. It's this book…" (or a search turns up an article saying the same). Run often enough, the loop makes growth self-sustaining. Readers who succeed with the book become recommenders, a virtuous cycle of organic evangelism. *The War of Art* has one and *Hemingway's Boat* doesn't (10,000 reviews vs 250), though both are about the same topic and rated alike, because the former "(1) convincingly solves (2) a painful problem (3) for a certain type of reader (4) who often mentions it." The loop works even when people don't know they want a book: someone new in a city confides, "It's been hard meeting people, and I guess I'm feeling a little bit lonely," and a friend names the book that helped them.

Write out the recommendation story for the book, then strengthen it:

1. Is the promise Desirable enough that people readily complain about it, ask and give advice, and search for solutions? Is solving it a top priority or a nice-to-have?
2. Of the possible promises, does one have more **"hair on fire"** urgency for a certain reader? (*The Workshop Survival Guide*'s loop fires in the stressful days before an important workshop: high cost of failure, a date that can't move.)
3. Of the possible reader profiles, does one search for or give advice more actively, or feel the pain more sharply? That reader fuels a stronger, faster loop.

Adjust the scope until the loop is strong. **Recommendability removes competition**: a reader told your book is *the* solution buys yours, which creates a minimonopoly and pricing power ("a boat and a moat").

## Back catalog: built to last

Useful books' organic growth compounds, so time is an ally. Selling 50,000 copies in a twelve-week launch is hard; 100 copies a week for ten years gets there too. **Back catalog** titles (those that stay relevant for years) earn 90% of publishing's profits on 2% of its marketing budget (Seth Godin). Beyond DEEP, two requirements:

1. Pick a promise that will stay relevant and important for 5+ years.
2. Avoid overreliance on temporary tools, trends, and tactics.

"When only a portion of the book remains relevant, the whole is diminished" (dated tactics in *The 4-Hour Workweek* hide its good stuff). Some dated references are necessary, and over-avoiding them makes a book frustratingly abstract. "The bits that date should be brief, infrequent, valuable for today's readers, and easy for tomorrow's to skip." Move timely material to the author's website, where it doubles as marketing, email capture, or upsell. *The Workshop Survival Guide* cut a frequently requested slide-theme tutorial for this reason. For inherently fast-changing topics (programming, regional tax law), release updated editions.

The formula: "do the best job of solving an important problem for a reader who cares, without anchoring yourself to temporary tools, tactics, or trends."

## The process

Work the stages in order. Each closes on the stated signal. When the user arrives mid-process, locate their stage, check the earlier DEEP requirements that stage depends on, and work from there.

1. **Scope and recommendation story.** Done when the scope (all six considerations) and the loop are written as a hypothesis.
2. **Listening conversations** to verify the scope and rebuild reader empathy, escaping the **curse of knowledge**. Done when the scope resonates with future readers. → [`reader-conversations.md`](reader-conversations.md)
3. **Takeaway ToC and teaching conversations.** Write a detailed table of contents whose titles state takeaways ("Good sales is about asking good questions", not "Sales 101"). Then **become the book**: teach slices of it to real readers and revise the ToC wherever you had to improvise, repeat, or rephrase. Done when the taught material works for the people you help (Desirable and Effective verified). → [`reader-conversations.md`](reader-conversations.md)
4. **First draft** of at least the first big piece of value, following the tested ToC: "The first draft is just to help you think." → [`drafting-and-reader-experience.md`](drafting-and-reader-experience.md)
5. **Second and third drafts for reader experience**: word counts on the ToC, then front-load the value and delete the fluff. Done when the manuscript is "just barely coherent enough" for a motivated beta reader to muddle through, *not* when it's polished. → [`drafting-and-reader-experience.md`](drafting-and-reader-experience.md)
6. **Beta reading in iterations** (2-8 weeks each, 3-5 engaged readers per round). Start pre-sales in the second half. Done on the three finished signals: recruiting new beta readers feels easy, most reach the end and receive the value, and some bring their friends. → [`beta-reading.md`](beta-reading.md)
7. **Seed marketing to the first 500-1,000 readers**, ideally begun before launch. → [`seed-marketing.md`](seed-marketing.md)
8. **Polish, publish, optimize**: editing and production; Amazon funnel; reviews; pricing; extra formats; piracy; fans; self-publishing vs a publisher. → [`publishing-and-sales.md`](publishing-and-sales.md)

Reassurances the author gives a hesitant writer: "Start Before You're Ready". Writing "is like driving a car at night. You can see only as far as your headlights, but you can make the whole trip that way" (Doctorow). "Every first draft is a dumpster fire." "Creating useful nonfiction is a task of manual labor, not genius": "bad at writing" usually means unwilling to spend time on feedback and editing, and slow is not unable. A recent amateur's memory of being a beginner is an edge over the "natural expert." Feedback, support, and pre-orders arrive long before the book is finished.

## Working with this skill

- **An idea or scope.** Classify it (pleasure-giver or problem-solver). Draft the scope formula, writing out who it's not for and what it won't cover. Write the recommendation story and run its three questions. Check the five-year promise and the dated-tactics risk. Propose cover-ready promise wording, then have the author test it in conversation. Next: listening conversations ([`reader-conversations.md`](reader-conversations.md)).
- **A ToC or manuscript.** Rewrite topic titles as takeaways ([`reader-conversations.md`](reader-conversations.md)). Add word counts and mark the slow start, long slogs, and fluffy sections. Find value enablers delaying real value. Judge length against the promise ([`drafting-and-reader-experience.md`](drafting-and-reader-experience.md)).
- **Reader or beta feedback.** Read disengagement, confusion, and drama as data about the manuscript: "The problem isn't you. The problem is the problem." Prescribe deletion before addition ([`beta-reading.md`](beta-reading.md)).
- **Launch or sales.** Confirm DEEP first. Marketing a book that doesn't work only spreads disappointment ("if you give away 250 boring books, it might have a negative effect"). Then [`seed-marketing.md`](seed-marketing.md) before launch, [`publishing-and-sales.md`](publishing-and-sales.md) for production and after.
- Speak in the book's terms (DEEP, scope, recommendation loop, value enablers, value-per-page, back catalog, seed audience) so the author can carry them into their own work.
