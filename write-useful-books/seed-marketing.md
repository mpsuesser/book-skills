# Seed marketing: find your first 1,000 readers

Read this when planning a launch or pre-launch marketing, choosing marketing channels, writing in public, building an email list, or getting a finished book its first readers.

Organic growth can't start until some happy readers have received massive value and can recommend the book. These early evangelists are the **seed audience** (seed readers). Until they exist, the author is on the hook for manual, hands-on marketing. After that, ongoing marketing is "an *option* rather than an *obligation*." The target is the book "into the hands and hearts of 500-1,000 seed readers before taking my foot off the gas": a few weeks to a few months of part-time work. April Dunford instead commits to a stretch of time: **"Launch is a year, not a day."** A longer horizon removes the do-or-die launch, so small mistakes stop mattering.

Recommendability is leverage. *The Mom Test*'s 800 manually seeded readers led to 50,000 organic sales, about 60 copies per seeded copy, which is "how to flip book marketing into a profitable equation." Seeding a book that doesn't work fails: "Successful marketing isn't really about marketing at all — it's about product design, testing, refinement".

## The four options

You need only one good approach; play to your strengths, preferences, and constraints.

- **A. Digital book tour** via podcasts and online events: *most scalable*.
- **B. Amazon PPC** (pay-per-click) ads: *easiest but unscalable*.
- **C. Event giveaways and bulk sales**: *fastest if you have the contacts*.
- **D. A small author platform** via content marketing and "writing in public": *most reliable and valuable, but time-intensive*.

*The Mom Test* was seeded in days by event giveaways. *The Workshop Survival Guide* took months on PPC ads alone. *The Mom Test*'s revival after a five-year hiatus used the podcast tour, and *Write Useful Books* is building a platform. Starting with zero resources, reputation, or connections: mainly D, complemented by B.

**Choosing:** the platform is the most time-intensive, but it is "a permanent, compounding asset that will travel with you from project to project." Invest in it if the book is the cornerstone of your next career stage. If you're moving to a different field afterwards, the other three seed more efficiently. Otherwise follow the easiest path. Client work makes bulk sales easy. Speaking or teaching makes giveaways or the podcast tour natural. Take low-hanging opportunities that fit (travel, fashion, or lifestyle books on Instagram and TikTok). You can also choose the hard way deliberately, to start a blog, channel, or speaking career you wanted anyway.

## A) Digital book tour (most scalable)

Physical book tours are "the biggest waste of time you can possibly do" (Chris Voss). A digital tour from home gets better results "in a twentieth of the time." Podcast hosts and online event organizers need valuable content, and the author of a useful book supplies it.

- The first gig is the hardest; invitations follow. "Spiral your way up from the obscure to the influential." Voss hunted down podcasts of any size and outworked his publisher's PR, and one big show (Lewis Howes's) made the others jump on. His one exception to staying home: TV on opening day, when the book is news.
- One decent podcast can beat a well-attended keynote in an afternoon. Most shows are tiny (2019: 750,000 active podcasts, median 141 listens per episode), so start small and work up.
- Not a natural speaker? Compensate with prep. Derek Sivers asks hosts for questions a week ahead, spends hours writing answers from different perspectives, picks the most interesting, and delivers them so they sound spontaneous ("Your first reaction is usually outdated").

## B) Amazon PPC ads (easiest but unscalable)

Amazon's native book ads hugely outperform other platforms for useful books, because Amazon searchers are ready to pay for a book. Profitable campaigns don't scale far: clicks are limited and get dearer. They mostly complement another approach, but with patience they can seed alone. *The Workshop Survival Guide*'s ads sold 25-100 copies a month at $2-3 back per $1, and within a few months organic sales were 10x the ad-driven ones.

- PPC works better for self-published authors, whose higher royalty per copy affords higher per-click bids. That is one more reason not to price too cheaply.
- You can't customize Amazon ads: "Your book's cover and title/subtitle *are* its advertisement." An illegible cover or mysterious title means the ads won't work.
- Expect a few days learning the basics, then about four hours a week for the first few months. Start from Amazon's automated campaign suggestions. Avoid the "Kindle Lock Screen" ad type, which converts poorly for this kind of nonfiction.
- Royalties pay out two months later but analytics are immediate. Test at under $20 a day and raise spend only once it proves profitable.
- Expect modest numbers (e.g., $369 in sales from $99 of spend, 24 extra seed readers, in a month with 500 organic sales). Seed marketing *starts* organic growth; it doesn't replace it. Other ad platforms are situational (fashion authors on Instagram and TikTok). Try a few Amazon ads as soon as the book is on sale.

## C) Event giveaways and bulk sales (fastest)

Never set up a stall to *sell* books at an event: the opportunity cost is too high. Jump at any chance to give a book to an event's worth of ideal readers. A giveaway to every attendee beats being the keynote speaker. *The Mom Test*'s 800 seed readers came from 500 copies at one event (which covered £2.50/copy printing), 200 at another (bulk at £10/copy), and 100 through content marketing. Tendayi Viki bought 250 copies of *The Corporate Startup* and signed them for a queue at a conference launch, once, and word of mouth did the rest. But "if you give away 250 boring books, it might have a negative effect."

- Recipients must be close to the ideal reader profile: "they don't count if they won't love it."
- Every attendee must actually get one: on chairs before people arrive, in gift bags, or a giant frictionless pile at a key junction.
- **Online events**: include or link the book in an email to all attendees. Best is the event paying for digital licenses, even at a steep discount, because they'll push what they paid for. A special purchase link in an attendee email is weaker but better than nothing. Expect close to 100% penetration from a direct giveaway, 10-20% from a good purchase link.
- To the organizer, a "free" book is never free: a giveaway is a tacit endorsement with reputational risk, so they must believe it's useful to attendees. **Trick:** research relevant events while writing and coax organizers into being beta readers. That asks for their time, not their reputation, and builds the relationship.
- Paperbacks cost roughly $2-5 each to print. Once an event accepts the reputational risk, it usually finds the budget.
- Consultants and speakers can upsell **custom-branded editions** (client logo on the cover, custom foreword). The author has sold five or ten thousand to corporations and universities. Bundling books with services adds negotiating room, and clients can pay from separate budgets.

No connections? Then the "real" way: repurpose the manuscript into its own marketing.

## D) Author platform: content marketing and "writing in public" (most reliable)

Arvid Kahl grew from zero subscribers and 400 followers to several thousand subscribers and 8,000 followers while writing *Zero to Sold*, enough for #1 in its categories and $20,000 in six weeks. "All of my 'marketing' was just sharing the work I was already doing on the manuscript." "Doing that consistently, every single week, will build an audience whether you like it or not." Two insights:

1. Reuse the book's content as its own marketing. **Your manuscript *is* the marketing.**
2. Start very early, even with rough drafts and tiny excerpts.

Excerpts often need extra framing, since sections lean on what came before. Make four decisions up front, because "it's hard to be consistent if you're always improvising":

1. **Where to post.** Your own blog, newsletter, or following if you have one. Otherwise the online communities your readers already gather in (find them by searching or by asking readers where they hang out). Communities welcome the helpful, educational, interesting, inspiring, and sincere. Each has explicit submission guidelines and an implicit "culture" about self-promotion. Research each one and keep a list of community, size, what they like, and priority, then scan it whenever you have something to share.
2. **What to post:**
   - *Your writing, drafts, excerpts, and deletions.* Use Gary Vaynerchuk's model: create **pillar content** (a large piece that can be clipped, such as the manuscript), repurpose it into **micro content** (articles, quotes, images, stories, remixes, rants), and distribute it across platforms. "Your manuscript is not one piece of content — it's a thousand." A screenshot of a manuscript excerpt with highlights or commentary takes moments. A Reddit post assembled from excerpts took half an hour and won new customers. Mine the cutting room floor document too.
   - *Your research and references*: facts, anecdotes, quotes, links. Original research doubly: the author recorded (with permission) and posted his interviews with other authors.
   - *Your process and progress.* "Human beings are interested in other human beings" (Austin Kleon). Lawyers might want your learnings on publishing contracts, entrepreneurs your marketing and earnings data, creatives your struggles and breakthroughs, a fitness crowd your plans and pitfalls. A **momentum launch** (Dunford's term) uses the book's own milestones as stories to tell: #1 in pre-orders, the first royalty dollar, the fifth perfect review. Arvid Kahl shared beta-reader insights while writing *The Embedded Entrepreneur* and ended up with 500+ beta readers and a seed audience. Milestone posts also attract podcast invitations. **"Make things and tell people."**
3. **When: get accountable with a content schedule.** Posting early work is "too emotionally demanding for a loose, 'I'll do it when I feel like it' sort of approach." Pick a mechanism and use it: a weekly checklist reset each week; Kahl's newsletter as a public accountability scheme (each section becomes a blog post, the same newsletter, and a podcast reading, queued ahead with scheduling tools); or Kleon's "Once a day, after you've done your day's work, find one little piece of your process that you can share."
4. **How to capture interest respectfully.** If the book is on pre-sale, link straight to the purchase page. Otherwise collect emails, which are worth "orders of magnitude" more than social followers.
   - During development, use a **multi-tier signup**: join beta reading, or wait for the finished book.
   - Beat newsletter fatigue with a **lead magnet**, a digital gift valuable to them and free to give: a bonus chapter, video tutorial, case study, research report, templates, worksheets. Ryan Holiday built a 40,000-person list for *The Daily Stoic* with one free meditation every morning. The author's five-part email series (one common customer-interview mistake per email) has reached about 5,000 people.
   - **Low-tech alternative: "email me if you want it."** Alex Hillman skipped the landing page for *The Tiny MBA* and posted a few photos with an email address. 90 people emailed within 24 hours, and he hit #1 in pre-sales. He read every personal message and replied to each at launch, so his message arrived as a reply to theirs, safe from spam filters.

Building a seed audience from scratch always feels slower than you'd like, "but once it's done, it's done." Then optimize: [`publishing-and-sales.md`](publishing-and-sales.md).
