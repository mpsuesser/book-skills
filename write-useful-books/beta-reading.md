# Beta reading: find readers, gather better data, build a better book

Read this when planning or running beta reading, interpreting reader comments or drop-off, deciding whether a manuscript is finished, or timing pre-sales.

**Beta readers** are "neither paid professionals nor kindhearted friends." They are "actual, honest-to-god readers who want what you're creating so badly that they're willing to endure an early, awkward, broken manuscript just to get it." They yield three kinds of insight:

1. **Qualitative**: what they say in their comments.
2. **Quantitative**: where they become bored, start skimming, stop reading, and stop commenting.
3. **Observational**: how they apply the book's ideas in their lives.

Beta reading runs *after* the third-ish draft and *before* professional editing. A developmental editor, if used, joins while it's under way. It ends with a DEE_ book, ready to Polish.

## Begin while the manuscript still has problems

The biggest mistake, besides skipping it, is starting too late, after hundreds of hours of detailed editing. That fine work gets torn up, and "you'll receive *more* helpful feedback by showing a *less* polished product." Asked about a finished oil painting, people compliment it and offer small suggestions to spare your ego. Asked about a rough sketch, they'll say they just don't get it. "If you're not embarrassed by the first version of your product, you've launched too late" (Reid Hoffman) applies 100% to the pre-launch manuscript. "If you're completely proud of it, then you've waited too long."

## How many and how often

- **Iterations of 2-8 weeks**: the first one or two weeks gather the bulk of feedback, the rest work it into a revision. "You'll start by fixing chapters and end by fixing paragraphs."
- **A new set of 3-5 deeply engaged readers per iteration**, which takes inviting 12-20 who say they'd love to read it. About half never open the document, and half of the rest leave about one comment and quit. Invite roughly four times the number you want.
- Early batches hit a major obstacle, massive confusion or boredom, and stop. That's what you want. Don't force people through with guilt trips or nudges: **"Their disinterest *is* the data"**, showing what to fix next. In *The Workshop Survival Guide*'s first round nobody got past chapter two. Over subsequent rounds, with new readers each time, they got further until nearly everyone reached the end.
- Early iterations go faster because you only improve the chapters people got through. You can also edit recklessly, moving a whole chapter without fixing transitions.
- **Rarely reuse beta readers** across iterations. They've given a lot of time, and their feedback degrades: no longer first-time readers, they comment on tone and typos instead of whether they want it, whether it works, and where they get bored.
- **How many iterations:** the more you taught the material beforehand, the fewer (*The Mom Test* two, *The Workshop Survival Guide* six, *Write Useful Books*, untaught, more). Plan at least two full iterations (one to four months), ideally continuing until the readers show you're finished.

## The three signals that it's finished

1. Recruiting new beta readers feels easy, because they want what you're offering (**Desirable**).
2. Most are receiving the value and reaching the end (**Effective and Engaging**).
3. At least some are bringing their friends (**the recommendation loop is running**).

One or two true: on the right track. All three: ready to be Polished, and to become "an uncommonly excellent book that can soar on reader recommendations." Real constraints (time, energy, a deadline, a big marketing opportunity) justify a line in the sand, since iterations eventually give diminishing returns. *The Mom Test* launched with 200+ serious typos to meet a 500-copy conference giveaway and needed five rapid post-launch updates: not recommended, but sometimes life intervenes. Never skip beta reading, and ship before you run out of steam.

## Finding and managing beta readers

A six-week cycle needs only two to four new potential readers a week to reach 12-20 interested and 3-5 actual. Find them as you found reader conversations (see [`reader-conversations.md`](reader-conversations.md)): ping friendly first contacts, including everyone who enjoyed a reader conversation; mention the book as "your thing"; plant a flag online (email signature, relevant social profiles and sites). After one or two iterations, start pre-launch marketing, which seeds the launch audience and lets you invite the most eager fans. With an existing audience or waiting list, invite only a subset each iteration so the list lasts the whole process. If you can't find *any* readers, do some soul-searching about whether it's the right book: a small scope adjustment can unleash excitement on all sides.

**Tools:** write in any tool, but for beta reading move the manuscript into a cloud-hosted tool with live commenting. Exported PDFs gave weeks of silence, then thousands of comments on an outdated version, and integrating them was a time-sink: "You really don't want to be exporting and emailing anything." Google Docs works: comment/suggest but not edit, optionally with download and duplication disabled, shared individually or by public link. The author and Devin Hunt built *Help This Book* (helpthisbook.com) for beta reading: readers react to a passage with "Love this bit", "Confusing", "A-ha! Useful", "Feels slow", or "Thought", and an Overview dashboard counts each reader's reactions chapter by chapter, showing how far each one got. Whatever the tool, it must be on the web.

**Tell readers what feedback you need**, or they spend their time flagging typos while you're still working out the core content. These instructions "will vastly improve the quality of feedback received." *The Workshop Survival Guide*'s note:

> **Dear review reader:**
>
> Thanks for taking a look. This review is **not** about spotting typos (there are lots). Instead, the most useful feedback is about stuff like:
>
> - Where you get confused or lost or have an unanswered question
> - Where you disagree, or have different experiences
> - Where you start to get bored and feel like skipping ahead or giving up
> - Anything you find especially interesting or helpful

It closed by saying where to comment: inline in the Google Doc ("easiest for us, if possible") or by email.

**Save the most influential readers for last.** Most people review once, so hold potential testimonial-givers and evangelists until the manuscript is fairly strong. Don't expect or force evangelism, which only burns bridges. If an influential reader says they love it, ask for a testimonial for the cover or Amazon page, keep them posted on launch timing, and send a few copies (signed if possible) at publication. *The Workshop Survival Guide* sent signed copies to fifty influential readers, asking for nothing, and some went out of their way to help. *The Mom Test* launched with zero testimonials, a mistake, and added unsolicited ones later: "When you've built a book to last, you're able to breeze past a lot of early blunders."

## Reading the data

Where readers will find the value, or get bored, can rarely be predicted. Find it in the data, then act on it.

**Evidence of value.** Comments like "I had a big 'a-ha moment' here" mark the value. When readers find major value in a minor side point, emphasize it or expand it into its own section. When a tone or style lands ("OMG. This is my favorite part so far. Literally, tell me what to do... I love it."), confirm it with a few readers and use it throughout. (Shifting from "here are all the options" to "here's what you should do" was one such change.) *The Mom Test*'s single goofy mock conversation was a sheepish accident. Beta readers loved it for making an abstract concept concrete, so absurd-but-useful example conversations went into nearly every chapter, "a big reason that the book ended up working." Encouraging comments ("Mind blown by this... Seriously", "WOW!") also sustain the author through the grind.

**Confusion, skepticism, missing information.** Skeptical comments ("Spidey-sense alert. Triggers a strong raised-eyebrow response"; "This is a leap of faith for me. It's not immediately obvious why this is true, yet.") flag a flimsy argument, so add justification. Lost readers ("This feels like the summary after the explanation. I couldn't really take it in."; "This section threw me off. I thought I missed something between the earlier section and this one.") flag a wander into the weeds. Treat confusion as a "precious signal". The eye-roll response ("C'mon, pay better attention!") preserves the problem. Think instead: "A-ha, my book has a weakness; let's see if I can fix it so nobody else gets lost in the same way." Even if you explained it earlier, if one reader gets stuck others will, and that hinders the recommendation loop.

**Factual inaccuracies, oversimplifications, overgeneralizations.** Expert or enthusiastic-amateur readers catch the places where you present your personal preference as "the One True Solution" (the author's workshop-timekeeping preference, until a reader who used a minutes-since-start / minutes-until-end timer straightened him out and the suggestion went into the final book), reason sloppily ("This feels a bit circular, like: 'a great skeleton is great'. I was expecting something more actionable and specific."), or are plain wrong. A diverse set of readers catches the inadvertently insensitive, rude, biased, or offensive.

**Delete the sentences drawing unnecessary drama.** A few minor sentences attract disproportionate criticism, confusion, and debate. *The Workshop Survival Guide*'s asides about traditional classrooms made teachers feel their profession was trivialized. Deleting every mention, and using freelance-workshop examples instead, made the problem disappear, and teachers started liking the book. The rule: first try to clarify and fix. If you can't and the point is educationally essential, accept the outrage of a small percentage to serve the rest. If it's tangential or nice-to-have, delete it. (Cormac McCarthy: "Don't worry too much about readers who want to find a way to argue about every tangential point.")

**Fall in love with negative feedback.** Hearing it now beats being blindsided by Amazon reviews later. "The moment you start disregarding or rationalizing negative feedback is the moment you lose your ability to improve your book." One reader wrote "I am literally so excited to be reading this", then a few pages later "This is fluff — I don't like fluff", and never came back. She was right. Treat "each draft as a hypothesis or experiment: your job is to gather data to test that version of the manuscript and figure out what's wrong with it". If it fails, only the experiment failed (Bec Evans). "The problem isn't you. The problem is the problem" (Pressfield): you *and* your readers against the manuscript's problems. If you're tempted to argue with a beta reader, step away for a night or a week. Trying to "win" a comment thread shows you're taking it personally, and convincing one reader outside the manuscript leaves the next to stumble on the same thing. You needn't obey every suggestion, especially from outside the ideal reader profile: "Use reader feedback as a lens to see what's going wrong, and then come up with your own way to fix it." One maddening cohort turned out, after a vacation, to be non-native English speakers tripped by fancy idioms carrying core concepts. Simplifying the language ended the confusion.

## Detect boredom and abandonment

The most helpful feedback is where readers get bored, and it is hidden. Readers are too polite to say so, or they blame themselves ("not paying attention", "feeling tired") for what is a failure of reader experience design.

- **Find where readers quietly give up**, estimated by **where their comments stop**. It's an imperfect proxy, but close enough. A reader whose life "may have just gotten crazy" is a risk for the finished book too: "if your manuscript was sufficiently valuable, they would have made the time."
- Abandonment in Chapter 3 means either Chapter 2 was a low-value grind that exhausted them or Chapter 3 is.
- **Nine times out of ten the cause is low value-per-page in the surrounding sections.** "Wade into the surrounding sections with a chainsaw, aiming to slash their word counts by half or more." "You'll never cure boredom by adding more words… Deletion is your savior." (See the word-count ToC in [`drafting-and-reader-experience.md`](drafting-and-reader-experience.md).)
- Each iteration should show readers getting further.
- This is why beta readers must be *actual* readers. Pressured friends and hired professionals feel obliged to finish, which hides where they'd have given up.

## Follow up: did the book actually work?

Comments show the experience *while reading*. If the book aims to change behavior, mindset, skills, work, or life, follow up in the weeks after a reader finishes. *The Workshop Survival Guide* authors sent a few readers per iteration a hand-written note: "…wanted to return the favor by making ourselves available to help with any questions or problems. Have you had a chance to design or run a workshop since reading it? Did anything not work? Are you stuck on anything? We'd love to help." This is invaluable whenever the reader must act. "'Sounded good in theory but didn't work for me' is a death blow to an otherwise recommendable book."

## Pre-sales: second half of beta reading

Start pre-sales once you're fairly confident the structure is right and the knowledge works. That builds the seed audience and earnings without much risk of slipping timelines. Selling earlier backfired twice: a "book" sold as a ToC ended in five ineffective rewrites, apologies, and refunds, and pre-sales in early beta reading were followed by a full restructure and badly missed dates. Two forms:

1. **Pre-order**: they pay today and get it after it's finished. KDP takes Kindle pre-orders, which helps gather verified reviews and launch at #1 in its categories.
2. **Early access**: they pay now and get the current manuscript immediately, plus updates and the finished copy. This means more eyes on the manuscript but a more complex setup, needing a store such as Gumroad.

When beta readers signal Desirable, Effective, and Engaging, start seed marketing if you haven't ([`seed-marketing.md`](seed-marketing.md)) and Polish ([`publishing-and-sales.md`](publishing-and-sales.md)).
