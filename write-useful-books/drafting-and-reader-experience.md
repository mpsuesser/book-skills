# Drafting and reader experience

Read this when the author is starting a first draft, is stuck or blocked, or is revising a draft for engagement, pacing, length, or value-per-page.

## First draft: expand the tested ToC

Draft enough to deliver the first big piece of value so beta reading can start on it. That may be one chapter, several, or the whole book.

- **The first draft is supposed to be a mess.** "Write drunk, edit sober" (Hemingway); "vomiting words onto the page" (his editor). Don't reread, self-judge, fix typos, or rework paragraphs. Follow the ToC verified in reader conversations. "The first draft is just to help you think."
- Skip front matter (introduction, foreword) and back matter (appendix, resources). Write the introduction last, once you know what the book has matured into.
- **Use your comfort tools to find your natural tone.** Write in any tool, pen and paper included. Stuck on tone or blocked? Draft in your email client: put the section title in the subject line, address it to a friend who knows what's going on, and type the shortest possible explanation or justification of the subject line. That's the first draft of the section. Write to a specific individual who wants your help, not an anonymous crowd. Stop trying to sound smart, and use the tone you'd use explaining to a friend or colleague. If speaking comes easier, record each section (following the ToC) and transcribe it.
- **Define your schedule, do the work.** Pick a time and sit at the desk religiously: "You don't have to write, but you aren't allowed to do anything else." Eventually boredom outweighs writer's block ("Boredom is the writer's best friend"). The author gives his best few hours first thing, before checking messages. Tendayi Viki wrote three award-winning books in three years, with a full-time job and young kids, on two undistracted early-morning hours a day. "Two hours is plenty, but you've got to carve it out, defend it, do the work, and then show up again tomorrow."

## Reader experience: value received over time spent

To the reader, the book is a multi-hour journey experienced as **value received over time spent**. If too much time passes before the next meaningful value, engagement drops and they drift away. Designing the reader experience means deciding how to pace, and where to place, the major insights, takeaways, tools, actions, and "a-ha" moments. It is how the book meets DEEP's Engaging requirement: a page-turner rather than a grind.

Readers of a useful book aren't buying storytelling or suspense. They stay engaged as long as the book regularly delivers on its promise, so it "can be as 'boring' as you like" if it keeps delivering. A charming, funny book that taught nothing in twenty pages got thrown away. *Atomic Habits* got dog-eared for a steady drip of a-ha moments. **"At least every few pages, you want your reader to be thinking, 'Oh wow, I can use that.'"** Once enough value has landed, readers happily skip chapters that don't apply to them. Reviews register it: "Not a page is wasted, no topic lingered on too long, the reading time between each insight is perfectly calculated."

### Value enablers vs. actual value

"Just because some piece of knowledge is *necessary* doesn't mean that it is *valuable*", at least from the reader's perspective. Theory, context, and foundations are **value enablers**, not actual value. The most common way to ruin a reader experience is spending too long on foundational theory first ("Let's get the theory out of the way").

Teaching a kid chess by explaining every piece from the pawns up is a theoretical slog that collapses engagement. Teach only the king and castle, then play a simple checkmate puzzle. Engagement spikes, which earns goodwill for another minute of theory and another scenario, repeated until they have it all. Arrange content around **the learner's goals instead of the teacher's convenience**: deliver small pieces of real value as fast as possible, and interleave the enablers.

### Add word counts to the ToC

Add word counts to every section and chapter title. The ToC becomes an "x-ray view of your book's 'takeaways over time'", showing how many words (at **250 words per minute**, how many minutes) sit between two pieces of value. This depends on takeaway titles and plenty of subsections: you want word count per learning outcome, not per topic. Remove the counts before publication.

Detect three weaknesses:

1. **A slow start**: how many words before the first major piece of value?
2. **Long slogs**: lengthy, back-to-back sections without big "a-ha" moments.
3. **Fluffy sections**: high word count relative to value.

Fix all three by rearranging, editing, and deleting.

### Increase value-per-page by deleting the fluff

Cutting 10,000 words while keeping the value saves a reader 40 minutes, like 40 extra minutes in traffic to the same destination. Halving a section's word count doubles its value-per-page. Stephen King deletes twice as many words as he keeps. *The Workshop Survival Guide* went from 120,000 words (eight hours) to 55,000 (three and a half); *The Mom Test* took a 50% haircut to 30,000 (two hours). Deleting whole chapters is scoping ("Oh, they don't actually need this!"); deleting anything smaller is editing plus reader experience design.

Keep a **cutting room floor** document and paste every cut chapter, section, and paragraph into it. It isn't wasted work, and the cuts often return as content marketing. "The challenge isn't to add more good stuff. It's to delete all the fluff that's delaying readers from getting to it."

### Front-load the value

Recommendation likelihood depends on the value received before finishing or abandoning, and readers most often abandon at the start. Lengthy forewords, introductions, and theoretical foundations are speed bumps "from a place of author ego instead of reader empathy." Three ways to front-load:

1. Delete or reduce the front matter (foreword, intro, bio).
2. If the book opens with value enablers, rearrange to insert pieces of real value far earlier.
3. If the book builds toward a grand conclusion or set of tips, **start with the big reveal.**

The third is controversial because authors fear that "giving it all away" lets readers take the goods and run, but that fear isn't justified. *The Mom Test* delivers most of its big ideas in the first three chapters (about 7,500 words, thirty minutes). The rest is supplemental detail for those who need help putting it into practice: beginners read cover to cover, and advanced readers get the value and move on, grateful. Derek Sivers's whole introduction to *Your Music and People* is a few short paragraphs of bare-minimum credibility and context ("So here's my context, as short as can be… Now listen up, and I'll tell you everything I know."). He still "kinda wished I had none at all."

### Too long vs. too short

**"A book should be as long as is necessary to convincingly deliver on its promise, but never any longer."** Length is bad only when it exceeds the need: *The Personal MBA* runs nearly 500 dense pages and passed a million copies. Longer books are slower and costlier. Twice the length can take five times as long to edit, at the same price and with higher printing costs, so consider splitting a big subject into two titles. A paperback feels thin below about 100 pages (around 20,000 words). Add *pages* through thoughtful layout (diagrams, illustrations, typography, as in *Business Model Generation*) rather than padding *words*. Brevity can also be the brand: *The Tiny MBA*, Oxford's *Very Short Introduction* series.

## Revise into a third draft, then stop

- Reader experience work starts from the second draft. Take a week away from the manuscript between drafts.
- From the second draft on, reread while writing (Hemingway): "read it all every day from the start, correcting as you go along, then go on from where you stopped the day before. When it gets so long that you can't do this every day, read back two or three chapters each day; then each week read it all from the start." If you're prone to endless fiddling, keep a strict "write first, edit second" instead.
- Revise for structure, clarity, and reader experience. Leave grammar, typos, and wordcraft for later. **Tighten the early sections more than the later ones**: "A strong start can keep folks going through a weaker ending, but a strong ending can't save a disappointing start."
- Losing perspective? Print it, go somewhere pleasant, and read front to back for repetitions, inconsistencies, and slogs.
- If the content splits into standalone parts, hone the first and start beta reading on it while preparing the next.
- "Revisions are not copy edits; they are major surgery and they suck" (Delilah Dawson). Polishing typos on a first draft is "digging up a lump of coal and spit polishing it in front of Tiffany's." Read the draft "like it's your worst enemy's magnum opus and your job is to expose its every tragic flaw. … If you get bored reading it, so will your audience." Deleting yesterday's full day of work is healthy.
- **Stop when it's barely coherent**, enough for "a sufficiently motivated beta reader to muddle through and mostly figure out what you're trying to say." Perfecting it now is wasted, since beta reading will tear it up. Next: [`beta-reading.md`](beta-reading.md).
