# Publishing, polishing, and optimizing sales

Read this when deciding between self-publishing and a publisher, polishing and producing the finished book, or improving a published book's sales: Amazon page, cover, reviews, pricing, audiobook, other formats, piracy, and fans.

## Motivations and business models

A useful book is "too big of a project to be undertaken cynically," so be clear why you're writing it. Emotional motivations: **Beginnings** (explore, plant a flag, and build a reputation in a space you'll stay in), **Closure** (capture a life stage's lessons and move on), **Impact** (spread knowledge beyond your direct reach), **Curiosity**, **Craft**. Financial models, all valid: **Freedom via royalties** (passive income), **Increased earnings via reputation** (a multiplier on speaking fees, consulting rates, and career), **Entrepreneurship via audience-building** (cross-selling products, services, or events). If royalties are the goal, self-publish.

## Publishers vs. self-publishing

- Self-publishing earns about 5x the royalties for life (50-70% instead of 8-15%) and keeps full control. A publisher must sell at least 5x more copies to compensate.
- Publishers "wait and see" until a book is de-risked by **reputation** (already a best-selling author), **platform** (an adoring audience), or **momentum** (10,000+ copies sold). Negotiations with an unproven author center on how *you* will promote *your* book. Until you're proven, a publisher is "your book's investor and production assistant rather than … its marketer."
- Gauge where you stand from the advance plus guaranteed marketing; for big publishers the two correlate. $50k+ means they take it seriously and will likely fund marketing. $10k or less (typical for unproven, unplatformed authors) means wait-and-see. Some small indie publishers offset lower advances with more guaranteed marketing.
- **Hybrid:** self-publish the first 10,000 copies, then move to a publishing deal. That earns about $55k more on those copies (roughly $7 vs $1.50 royalty each) and you negotiate from strength (*Traction*; *Business Model Generation*).
- The method works for either route, but suits self-publishers maximizing long-term passive income best.

## Polish: add the P to DEE

Once beta readers signal Desirable, Effective, and Engaging, polish: hammer the prose through detailed, repeated editing passes. Now fix the little problems: tighten, clarify, cut extra words, print it and read front to back, follow your sense of craft. A publisher guides this. Self-publishers should hire a little help. Self-publishing checklist:

- **Copyediting** (clarity, conciseness, phrasing): about $500+ for a freelancer, priced by word count; strongly recommended. Copy editors don't fix structure, so settle that first through beta reading or developmental editing. A **developmental editor** is the larger, pricier option if you're stuck on the big picture.
- **Final review readers** to cover beta reading's gaps: influential readers (pre-launch testimonials for the cover and Amazon "editorial reviews"); expert readers (inaccuracies, overgeneralizations); sensitivity readers (unintentional bias, insensitivity, marginalization); legal review (libel or IP risk from anecdotes and quotes about real people or entities, and mandatory to insure the book as a business asset).
- **Proofreading** (typos, grammar): about $150+, "terrific value for money." Proofreaders traditionally work from the formatted PDF. The author has them work in the manuscript doc, for fast integration, and checks layouts himself.
- **Interior layout** (paperback, ebook, optional PDF): learn it yourself if you'll update often; otherwise hire. An index, glossary, or fancy typography makes a freelancer more important. DIY tools are either "easy and beautiful but inflexible" (Vellum, Reedsy, Kindle Create) or "fiddly and tedious but customizable" (Word with a template, Google Docs with add-ons, InDesign). Scan every page of the exported PDF for dangling list or paragraph lines, headings at a page's end, and displaced illustrations leaving whitespace.
- **Covers**: front for the ebook; front, back, and spine for the paperback, with an empty corner for the ISBN barcode; square for the audiobook. Use an online spine-width calculator (page count, paper weight, dimensions). $50-500 freelance, or DIY templates.
- **Print-on-demand and storefront**: Amazon KDP (which also handles paperbacks), or IngramSpark plus Shopify for your own store.
- **ISBNs**: free from Amazon or IngramSpark (with limitations worth researching), or buy your own and publish under your own imprint (needs a business entity). Use one ISBN per format and per major new version.
- Keep fast-changing how-to material (KDP setup, ad setup) out of the book and in updatable web guides: the back-catalog rule applied to itself.

These tasks are fiddly but "predictable, solvable, and low-risk."

## Optimize for sales and growth

Once the book is working and selling, 10-50 hours of optimization can double sales. Optimization won't make a book *start* selling, but it makes it sell *more*. Fixing *The Workshop Survival Guide*'s funnel raised sales 50% overnight (more than $10k a year). The full list applied to *The Mom Test* at year six roughly doubled sales (more than $50k a year). Amazon's data is hedgy, so the exact impact can't be pinned down. The options:

1. The Amazon purchase funnel (50%+ sales increase).
2. Percentage boosts from extra platforms and products (5-20% uplift apiece).
3. Pricing, piracy, and engaging fans.

### The Amazon purchase funnel

Two decision points:

1. Shoppers **click your book instead of someone else's**, based on the cover, title, subtitle, and reviews.
2. They **press "buy" instead of "back"**, based on the store page, description, and reviews.

**A "clickable" cover makes a clear promise that's legible as a thumbnail.** In any search other than for the exact title, the levers are the cover as a tiny thumbnail (under 300 pixels tall), the title and subtitle, and the review count and star rating. The first two are yours to control, and the cover is "*by far*" the most important. Most shoppers insta-click the first cover that looks relevant. In a search for "customer development", a shopper who hasn't heard of the books by name will click any of the other three top results before *The Mom Test*: their promise is legible at thumbnail size, and *The Mom Test*'s sits in small print. It loses the click, and it can't be advertised. *The Workshop Survival Guide*'s promise is clear even when small. The cover can change after launch, but the title and subtitle are much harder to change, so make one of them define who the book is for and what it will do for them.

**Reviews:** credibility tips over at about **20-40 reviews** (Hassan Osman), so encourage authentic reviews until you reach it. Reviews raise both clickthrough and conversion.

**The store page should sell, not just describe.** *The Workshop Survival Guide*'s main fix replaced a back-cover blurb with a benefits description written to standard copywriting principles:

- Speak directly to the reader's situation and goals, listing the promise and its benefits, in readers' own words from conversations and beta reading where possible.
- Write more than feels right (at least five paragraphs) so buyers can read until they've convinced themselves.
- Use headers, lists, and bold text for skimmers.
- If in doubt, take the book's **five most compelling learning outcomes** from the ToC as sub-headings and add a paragraph of explanation or justification under each. That is the first draft.
- Pitch the salesiness in the middle: one hundred percent is probably too much, and zero is absolutely too little.

Fill the overlooked sections too: the author profile and featured blog posts or videos (via Author Central), **editorial reviews** (written by experts, submitted by you; effectively a giant free-text field), customized "Look inside" samples showing the strongest pages, and extra product images. Formatting uses a quirky HTML subset.

**You can't "game" reviews, but you can encourage real ones.** Aim for 20-40 verified reviews for social proof, plus a steady organic stream of daily sales and reviews for Amazon's algorithm. Per Chris Fox, five consecutive days of organic *purchases* trips a hidden flag that makes a book eligible as a "suggested product". Amazon values consistency over volume and ignores big incentivized spikes. Accelerant: launch the Kindle edition at the lowest price ($1.99) and ask early readers for a "two-dollar favor" of a fair look and an honest review. Kindle Exclusive allows a free launch that still counts as verified, which costs nothing if you were Amazon-only anyway. Raise the price once the early reviews arrive. If asking feels uncomfortable, usefulness alone gets there: beyond occasionally mentioning that reviews helped, the author never explicitly asked for one and has 675 and 175 on Amazon, plus 4,500 on Goodreads.

**One-star reviews:** "It's not for them" (Seth Godin). They are an inevitable by-product of success: enthusiastic recommendations occasionally reach readers the book wasn't written for, who feel swindled. A four-star average made of mostly fives and a few ones beats uniform fours: "better to be loved by someone than ignored by everyone."

### Percentage boosts: extra formats, platforms, and support

Weigh every nice-to-have: **Increased reach + increased profits vs. Time cost + financial cost + stress cost.** "Not every 'good idea' is worth doing." The author declined a partner's online course for a 14,000-person semi-relevant audience at 20+ hours plus stress, though he'd have taken it during seed marketing. These activities have high time costs and small percentage gains, so there's no pressure to do them before launch. Once the book is working and growing, they compound. Options, roughly in order of impact:

- **Audiobook**: a 20-30% sales boost, 5-10% to royalties. Per copy it's the least profitable format (*The Mom Test*: about $17 per $30 paperback at 55% after printing, $7 per $10 ebook at 70%, under $3 per audiobook because of Audible subscriptions). Yet it is under 10% of profits and over 30% of "readers": more people able to recommend the book, and little cannibalization, since many people *only* buy audio. The first one cost about 30 hours plus $250 for an audio editor. Record it yourself if you can: professional narrators keep a steady tone "because they don't know what matters", while you know what to emphasize, where to pause, and where to speed up.
- **More platforms** (iBooks, Google Play, or all at once via IngramSpark or Draft2Digital): 5-15% in total.
- **Direct sales on your own site** (PDF, MP3, EPUB via Gumroad or similar) at 97% royalties: a 38% royalty boost on each copy sold there.
- **Bulk orders** for events, universities, and corporations: potentially huge, especially combined with consulting or teaching, but dependent on networking, sales, and logistics (see [`seed-marketing.md`](seed-marketing.md)).
- **Press pack** (a credibility brochure): valuable only if you do lots of direct sales to universities, corporations, or conferences.
- **Translations**: a whole extra book, but each language needs a fresh seed audience.
- **Support for teachers, trainers, and consultants** (worksheets, slide decks, suggested readings, lesson plans): top priority for academically viable books, meaningless for others.

### Pricing, profit per copy, and situational upsells

- Charge **$20-30 for the paperback and $9.99 for the ebook**. Useful nonfiction can be priced above a typical book, though not as high as it deserves.
- Never charge the minimum. "If you can sell an ebook for $2, you can sell it for $10. If you can sell a paperback for $10, you can sell it for $20." Bargain prices signal low value and undercut PPC.
- $9.99 because Amazon pays 70% at or below it and 35% above (the threshold varies by region). Doubling to $20 earns nothing extra at the same volume, and the Kindle price effectively sets the PDF and other digital prices.
- Exceptions: charge *more* for a bundle sold through your own site, or *less* for the book when the long-term customer relationship is monetized through other offerings.
- **Upsell bundles** (videos, tools, worksheets, templates, tutorials, personal support) must add *complementary* value, never *withhold* it. Withholding turns the book into "a lengthy infomercial", destroying value-per-page and organic growth. "The book should feel complete and self-contained on its own, while the upsell offers a compelling — but non-essential — bonus for the especially motivated." A typical scheme: $10-30 for the book, $50-75 with digital educational bonuses, $150-250 for the full set of tools, templates, and time-savers, which business buyers who value their hours find a no-brainer. Only 10-20% buy the upsell, but that doubles profitability. It needs traffic to your own site; it doesn't work through Amazon.
- **A business serving the same readers** makes the book's price nearly irrelevant. A self-published lawyer spent tens of thousands of pounds placing his book in London train-station bookstores, sold 500 copies, and closed several hundred thousand pounds of work from readers who had spent two hours learning why he was the right lawyer. Alex Hillman pulled *The Tiny MBA* from Amazon to sell only on his site: fewer copies, but each worth 10x because it came with the buyer's email. Write something useful enough that readers want more from you, and give them a way to get it.

### Leverage piracy by turning the book into its own marketing

Assume the book will be pirated (*The Mom Test*'s pirated PDF is a top Google result, and its top-rated Amazon review is titled "I pirated the book. Stopped reading. Went to Amazon and bought it."). Recommendable books tend to benefit: pirate readers recommend it to people who pay, and many couldn't have bought it anyway (often because of a weak local currency), so count it as social impact. The more of your revenue comes from ongoing customer relationships (consulting, training, other products), the more piracy helps, because the book is worth more as lead generation than as royalties. Royalty-dependent authors should be more strategic. The question: "what hooks can you put into it (without withholding value or frustrating your readers) that will allow even a 'free' reader to end up helping you?" This book mentions its author's beta-reading software, community, and other content.

### Most authors ignore their fans, so engage with yours

"No New York book publisher knew the names of their core and dedicated readers" (Kevin Kelly, *1,000 True Fans*). Readers love reaching an author, and engaging them creates super-evangelists and PR opportunities. Amazon owns the customer contact, so give readers the ability and the motivation to reach you:

- The "salesy" route: a lead magnet on your site in exchange for an email and permission to contact.
- The authentic route: be available and spend a few minutes replying. Put your contact details everywhere, *especially inside the book*. Respond, eventually if not instantly. Watch social media for mentions and join in when appropriate. Social media is ineffective for outbound marketing (unless you already have a big audience) but "the author's best friend for reader engagement and support", with contact coming inbound.
- Overlap engagement with platform-building: answer reader questions as public videos and send the link. Sustainable marketing integrates with what you already do.
- Watch for mentions by influential fans (Google Alert, social search) and send thanks: "Hey, I saw you mentioned the book — big thanks and let me know if there's anything I can help with." Some of the author's biggest podcast appearances came this way.
- You won't sustain personal service forever; that's a problem for when success arrives.
