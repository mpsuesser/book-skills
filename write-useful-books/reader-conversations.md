# Reader conversations: improve the book before it's written

Read this when verifying a scope, building or testing a table of contents, or when the author can't find (or is avoiding) readers to talk to.

The house-builder who piles up bricks without asking the family, then tears everything down for each suggestion, is what most authors do with manuscripts. Product design's rule is that the more iterations in front of real users, the better the product. Rewriting a manuscript caps the number of iterations, so iterate on the scope and structure in conversation instead, never rewriting anything larger than a ToC. "Listening and Teaching Are Part of Writing."

## Two styles of reader conversation

1. **Listening/understanding conversations** verify and improve the scope and rekindle reader empathy.
2. **Teaching/helping conversations** refine the ToC and iterate on the book's education design and structure.

They blur together in practice (a teaching conversation may become a loose Q&A), and that's fine. *Neither* asks for opinions of the book idea ("So what do you think?"), which "is just fishing for compliments." The earliest conversations, while scope and ToC can still change sweepingly, pay off most. After that, a few per week is optional; the author keeps doing them.

Friendly first contacts are biased (supportively), and the bias matters only if you pitch the idea and ask for opinions. Keep the conversation on their life (listening) or on helping them (teaching) and the bias drops out.

## Listening: escape the curse of knowledge

The **curse of knowledge** is having so much expertise that you become unintelligible to the novice, and "it kills an awful lot of books" (chess books read like "just be smarter"). Jeremy Silman, already an International Master and bestselling author, had students talk aloud before and after each move and wrote down their thoughts: "I was soon seeing problems that I never imagined they possessed." *The Amateur's Mind* stayed a top-twenty chess bestseller for twenty years.

**Reader empathy** (getting back inside the reader's head) lets the author anticipate questions, objections, concerns, and confusions, and strike the right tone, tempo, and level of detail. It also shows which reader types care most (a Desirable scope), and these first contacts often become the earliest beta readers, testimonials, and evangelists.

A listening conversation is a friendly exploration of the reader's experiences, worldview, and decision-making:

> You've been dealing with X recently, right? Would you mind talking me through what you did and how it went? How did you decide to do it that way?
>
> What else did you try? What did you give up on or find unhelpful? Where did you search for help or guidance? What were the most frustrating moments? How did you eventually get over them? Did you read any books or blogs about it? Why (or why not)? Which ones were helpful and which were a waste? Why? What's still worrying or blocking you? Are you doing anything about it, or is it not that big of a deal?

Casual openers work too: "Oh, you're writing a book? Amazing! How are you approaching it?" or "You said you've always wanted to write a book — what's getting in your way?" For *The Workshop Survival Guide* the authors asked where people got most stressed or stuck preparing, which parts were hardest, where they wasted the most time, what went wrong at their last workshop, and what scared them about the next. "Without this sort of baseline understanding, you're throwing darts in the dark."

**Don't pitch, or even describe, the book.** Pitching exposes your ego, which discourages negative feedback. It also implies the details are settled, which discourages big-picture feedback. "You want insights into their life, not opinions about your idea." (These listening conversations are the subject of Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test*.)

When the scope resonates, shift to testing Effectiveness, starting with a first-guess detailed ToC.

## Fill your ToC with takeaways, not clickbait

The ToC is the blueprint of the book's education design. To work as a design-and-feedback tool it needs (1) clear, descriptive language and (2) detailed subsections.

- Title each section with its **learning outcome or takeaway**, not its topic. "Sales 101" says nothing; "Good sales is about asking good questions" is the takeaway, so it is the title.
- A section with several major takeaways keeps a category title and gets subsections, each titled with its takeaway.
- Example (*The Workshop Survival Guide*): "Don't start with the slides (do start with the Skeleton)", "Add the coffee breaks before designing the content", "Each Learning Outcome is a cluster of related ideas", "Workshop Outline + Schedule Chunks = Workshop Skeleton", "Vary the Teaching Formats to improve energy, attention, and learning".
- Counter-example: a survival guide whose sections read just Pine, Willow, Poplar, Oak forces the reader to page through every section, which is dangerous in a real field emergency. Better: "Pine for fire-starting, food, and bandaging injuries", "Willow for carving, weaving, finding water, and as a pain-killer".
- Authors resist this for fear of inconsistency, since some sections defy clean summary. Aim for informative titles anyway, even at the cost of consistency, and fall back to as descriptive as possible plus extra subsections.
- Clever or punny titles, or long chapters without subsections, come later, toward the second half of beta reading, once the structure is proven.

This descriptiveness shows exactly what a reader learns, and when. It is also what makes the word-count ToC work later (see [`drafting-and-reader-experience.md`](drafting-and-reader-experience.md)).

## Teach the book to test its contents

Comedians test new material in basement bars because the first version of a joke "is a guess, not a guarantee". George Carlin spent a year in front of audiences before each HBO special. **Become the book**: teach its contents to future readers, to learn what they need, in what order, which examples resonate, and which exercises work.

- Isolate one or two chapters that offer a desirable outcome for the particular person. Teach that slice of the ToC so they actually receive its value and achieve its promise; describing what it will contain isn't enough. A Q&A or coaching format also works.
- Take notes on what works. **"Whenever you're forced to improvise, repeat, or rephrase, treat it as an opportunity to iterate and improve your ToC."**
- The ask is easy, since you're offering free expert help on an outcome they care about:

  > Hey, I remember that you were thinking about doing X a while back. Is that still on your mind? If so, I'd love to grab some time, answer any questions, and help you think through how to approach it.
  >
  > The reason I bring this up is that I'm starting to work on a new book about the topic, and helping you through it would be super useful for me as research. And hopefully I can be helpful in return.

  A more "professional" version offers free coaching or consulting in exchange for a follow-up call in a few weeks to hear how it worked.
- **Adopt** a reader who cares deeply. Each *Workshop Survival Guide* author spent 20-40 hours with one aspiring facilitator, testing the full process, seeing where they got stuck, and finding the ideas that sounded good in theory but failed in practice.
- Teaching cures imposter syndrome: *prove* the advice is worth sharing instead of trying to believe it. The two adopted facilitators' daily fees quadrupled (about £500 to £2,000).

**"Writing is teaching, but harder."** Live, you can notice confusion and improvise; a finished book can't. Test and solidify the education design in advance, catching the biggest issues while a fix costs an afternoon. Beta reading will catch the rest.

## How to find people to talk to

You don't need many: a few at the start, then one or two a week. No scalable strategy is required; position yourself for serendipity.

1. **Friendly first contacts.** Prioritize those matching the ideal reader's experience level.
2. **Plant a flag online.** For example, an email-signature line saying what the book is and how people can help. A trickle is enough.
3. **Mention the book as "your thing"** when people ask what you've been up to. When someone lights up ("Oh my gosh, you're writing a book about that!"), start learning right then rather than scheduling a chat for later.
4. **Scalable:** begin pre-launch content marketing (see [`seed-marketing.md`](seed-marketing.md)) and invite superfans to opt in to conversations and beta reading.

Skip direct cold outreach unless there's no other option, since strangers almost never accept. "Spend your time finding the people who already care. And if *nobody* cares, their disinterest also means something."

## What if nobody wants to talk to you?

Three causes, each a book-killer if ignored:

1. **Readers don't care about the promise.** A lukewarm reader is evidence of an undesirable scope: adjust the promise or the reader profile until it excites them.
2. **You can't find even one potential reader.** This is usually an excuse born of nervousness, from believing you must show a polished product. "If you can't find even one reader now, then you're unlikely to find thousands of them later." Make the ask about their life:

   > Hey, I'm planning out a book about setting up an apartment veggie garden. I remember you once mentioned doing something like that before — would you mind talking me through what you tried and how it went? It would help me out so much and should only take about fifteen minutes. But I know everyone is super busy these days, so of course no worries if it's not a good time.

   If you truly can't reach anyone, deal with that *today*, not on launch day.
3. **You don't care enough** to fuss with testing and refining. This is existential: adjust the topic or audience until you're excited to engage with them.

"Hiding from your readers is a slippery slope": skipped conversations lead to skipped beta reading and a launch without testimonials, reviews, or a seed audience. "The more you're scared by the idea of talking to your readers, the more important it is to deal with now."
