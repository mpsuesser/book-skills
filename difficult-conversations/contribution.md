# Abandon Blame: Map the Contribution System

This file is the cure for the **Blame Frame**. Blame can be explicit ("I don't know how you could have let this happen!") or implicit ("Let's do better next time"). Either way, someone is cast as at fault. Blame is bad "because it inhibits our ability to learn what's really causing the problem and to do anything meaningful to correct it." It is also often irrelevant and unfair: it grows out of misunderstanding what gave rise to the issue and out of the fear of being blamed. And "blaming also serves as a bad proxy for talking directly about hurt feelings." Telling people "Don't blame others" doesn't help; what they need is the concept of *contribution*.

## Distinguish blame from contribution

"At heart, blame is about judging and contribution is about understanding."

- **Blame is about judging, and looks backward.** It asks three questions at once. (1) Did this person cause the problem? (2) How should their actions be judged against some standard: incompetent, unreasonable, unethical? (3) If the judgment is negative, how should they be punished? "This was your fault" condemns on all three counts. Expect defensiveness, strong emotion, interruptions, and arguments about what "good assistants," "loving spouses" or "any reasonable person" should do. It offers them the role of "the accused."
- **Contribution is about understanding, and looks forward.** It asks two questions. First: "How did we each contribute to bringing about the current situation?" or "What did we each do or not do to get ourselves into this mess?" Second: "Having identified the contribution system, how can we change it? What can we do about it as we go forward?" "Too often we deal in blame when our real goals are understanding and change."

*Example (ExtremeSport):* an ad agency flies you to Boulder for a client pitch, and your assistant has packed the wrong storyboards: "Right client, wrong campaign." The contribution version opens like this: "Let's retrace our steps... I suspect we may each have contributed to the problem. From your point of view, did I do anything differently this time?" Her side came out:

- she was handling three accounts at once;
- you got angry when she asked which boards to pack, and she felt intimidated;
- she meant to double-check but had to run to the copy center;
- she knew you usually double-check the briefcase yourself.

You did usually double-check, and this time you forgot. Your commitments: "we'd both better double-check every time," and "I need to work on being less impatient and abrupt. But if you're unsure, I need you to ask questions no matter what kind of mood I'm in." You add a system for tracking which appointments are for which campaigns. The pattern you uncovered: you get anxious and snap, she withdraws, something falls through the cracks, you become more annoyed and distrustful, then more abrupt and unapproachable, and mistakes multiply. A blame conversation reinforces that loop: "she'll eventually conclude that you're impossible to work with, and you'll report that she's incompetent."

**Contribution is joint and interactive.** "As a rule, when things go wrong in human relationships, everyone has contributed in some important way." When a batter strikes out, both the batter and the pitcher contributed. "Other than in extreme cases, such as child abuse, almost every situation that gives rise to a conversation is the result of a joint contribution system."

## The costs of the blame frame

(The legal system rightly apportions blame. Most conversations aren't trials.)

- **When blame is the goal, understanding is the casualty.** The threat of punishment makes people less forthcoming. An automaker may resist safety improvements because they would look like an admission. Truth commissions, such as South Africa's after apartheid, offered clemency in return for honesty.
- **Focusing on blame hinders problem-solving.** When the dog has run off, do you argue about who opened the gate and who failed to grab the collar, or do you look for the dog? When the tub overflows, the bather, the spouse who called the bather downstairs, the manufacturer of the small drain, and the plumber all contributed.
- **Blame can leave a bad system undiscovered.** At Commodity Corp., a VP championed a new plant, and the added supply cut profits. Several people had privately predicted this and said nothing. The VP was fired and a new strategic planner was hired, which changed one "part" but not the system. Ask instead: Why did the people who saw it coming keep silent? What implicit incentives were at work? What structures, policies and processes allowed the poor decision?

## The benefits of understanding contribution

- **Contribution is easier to raise.** Joseph, who runs an overseas office, learned about headquarters' decisions from clients, and once from the newspaper. His manager pointed out that Joseph had installed an incompatible computer system and rarely asked questions. Caught in the blame frame, Joseph wondered whether it was all his fault and never raised the issue. "The blame frame creates a difficult burden. You have to feel confident that others are at fault, and that you aren't, to feel justified in raising an issue."
- **Contribution encourages learning and change.** After a wife's infidelity, a husband who stays wants to understand what happened. Does he listen? Does he stay late at work? Was she sad, lonely, feeling undesirable? And what does she do that contributes to his not listening? Does she work weekends, or withdraw? "The contribution system must be mapped."

## Three misconceptions about contribution

1. **"I should focus only on my contribution."** No. "Finding your contribution doesn't in any way negate the other person's contribution." The shares needn't be equal: whether you are "5 percent responsible or 95 percent responsible — there is still joint contribution." "Understanding is the goal, not assigning percentages."
2. **"Putting aside blame means putting aside my feelings."** No: sharing feelings is essential, and "the very impulse to blame is often stimulated by strong emotions that lie unexpressed." "You are responsible for ruining our marriage!" is blame standing in for feelings. Saying "I feel devastated by what you did" or "My ability to trust you has been shattered" reduces the impulse to blame. If you're mired in the urge to blame, or in wanting them to admit they were wrong, ask yourself: "What feelings am I failing to express?" and "Has the other person acknowledged my feelings?" What you want to hear is usually not "It was my fault" but "I understand that I hurt you and I'm sorry."
3. **"Exploring contribution means 'blaming the victim'."** No. Suppose you are mugged on a dark street at night. Every blame question gets a no: you did nothing wrong, broke no law, were not immoral, and deserve no punishment. The contribution question is "What did I do that helped cause the situation?" The answer is that you walked alone at night. "You may not be able to change other people's contributions, but you can often change your own." Nelson Mandela recalls Reverend Andre Scheffer, an Afrikaner minister, teasing that whenever blacks had a problem they could simply say "Ingabilungu," a Xhosa expression meaning "It is the whites." "His message was that we must also look within ourselves." Identifying what you do to perpetuate the situation gives you leverage.

## Finding your fair share: four hard-to-spot contributions

1. **Avoiding until now.** An ex-husband was late for every pickup for two years and it was never mentioned. A boss trampled someone's self-esteem for four years. A store manager got years of "Satisfactory" reviews because the managers colluded in a norm of avoiding hassle. A problematic form is **complaining to a third party** instead of to the person. It puts the third party in the middle, they can't speak for you, and they are left burdened with a partisan story. Asking a friend for advice is fine, but report back on any change in your feelings after the conversation, so they aren't left holding an unbalanced story.
2. **Being unapproachable.** Your style keeps people at bay if it comes across as uninterested, unpredictable, short-tempered, judgmental, punitive, hypersensitive, argumentative or unfriendly. Whether you really are like that, or intend it, is not the point. If others experience you that way, you are part of a system of avoidance.
3. **Intersections** "result from a simple difference between two people in background, preferences, communication style, or assumptions about relationships."
   - *Example:* Toby and Eng-An had been married about four months. Toby raised issues (housework, sticking up for him with her mother, saving versus spending a bonus). Eng-An said "I just don't want to talk about this right now" and walked out.
   - Their verdicts on each other: to Toby, she was "incapable of dealing with feelings... in denial." To Eng-An, Toby was "smothering me... three-dollar discrepancy in our checking account on the night before my big presentation to the board!"
   - Their backgrounds: Toby's mother was an alcoholic and his family lived in denial; he was the only one who spoke up, so he learned to raise problems immediately. Eng-An's family revolved around her intellectually challenged brother; she learned not to react quickly and to create distance.
   - The system: Toby talks, Eng-An withdraws, Toby talks more. "Treating an intersection as a question of right versus wrong leads to the death of a great many relationships." Each waits for the other to change, but "in intersections there is no one to blame. People are just different." The way through is to compromise preferences and meet in the middle.
4. **Problematic role assumptions** are unconscious assumptions about your role, and they are problematic even when everyone shares them. Seven-year-old George bangs his spoon on the dog dish. Mom says, "Can't you make him stop that?" Dad yells "Stop it!" George cries. Mom says, "Well you didn't have to yell at him." Then it repeats. The pattern helps the family connect emotionally ("fighting to show love"). Roles persist because they are familiar and comfortable and the group keeps each person in their role. Changing the system means finding another way to provide its benefits, which is work in the Feelings and Identity conversations. At work, the assumption "Leaders set strategy; subordinates implement it" changes only when there is an alternative model everyone thinks is better, *and* the skills to make it work at least as well.

## Two tools for spotting contribution

- **Role reversal**: ask "What would they say I'm contributing?" Pretend you are them and answer in the first person (I, me, my).
- **The observer's insight**: take the view of a disinterested observer. Imagine you're a consultant called in, and describe "in a neutral, nonjudgmental way, what each person is contributing." If that's hard, ask a friend. If what you hear surprises you, don't reject it. Imagine it is true, and ask how that could be and what it would mean.

## Moving from blame to contribution

This takes persistence. You'll slip back into blame, so stay vigilant.

*Example:* Sydney led a team of engineers on a two-week consulting project in Brazil.

- Miguel, a banker on the project, was dismissive at first. She paired herself with him on subprojects, and the work went well.
- Then at dinner he said, "You have such an easy charm... And we're so far from home." He began standing close, giving her extra attention and seeking her out, but never made a direct invitation.
- Sydney was stuck in the blame frame, plus doubts: was she overreacting? was it a cultural difference? She feared he would explode and disrupt the project.

"By continuing to think in terms of blame, Sydney kept the stakes of raising the issue unmanageably high."

**Map the contribution system:**

- **Know your predisposition.** **Shifters** see themselves as innocent victims. **Absorbers** feel responsible for everything. Know which you are so you can push against it.
- **What are they contributing?** Miguel expressed romantic affection without clarifying his intentions, stood close and hinted at longing. He ignored her nonverbal signals: she changed the subject, changed the staffing, and moved away, and he followed. He never asked how she felt. (Whether he is blameworthy, or should be punished, is a separate inquiry.)
- **What am I contributing?** Sydney was particularly attentive at first and went out of her way to work with him one-on-one, which he may have read as interest. And she hasn't told him directly that she's uncomfortable.
- **List each person's contribution**, in two lists side by side.
- **Who else is involved?** Families (as with Toby and Eng-An). Team members who encouraged Miguel, or passed up chances to help.

**Take responsibility for your contribution early.** This signals that you are leaving blame behind. Sydney: "I wasn't sure how to raise this, and I wish I'd raised it sooner. When I suggested we work together at the beginning of the project, you may have heard that differently from how I meant it. My intention was just to improve our professional relationship." She can also ask: "Are there other things I've done that were ambiguous or that suggested I might be interested in something else?" If you start with their contributions, they will deflect by pointing to yours. "Taking responsibility for your contribution up front prevents the other person from using it as a shield." If they then focus only on you: "It's not okay to look only at my contribution. That's not reality as I see it. I feel like I'm trying to look at both of us. Is there anything I'm doing to make it hard for you to look at yourself?"

**Help them understand their contribution:**

- **Make your observations and reasoning explicit.** Say specifically what they did or said that triggered your reaction, and jot down those triggers beforehand. Sydney: "When you said I had an 'easy charm' and asked if I wanted to head to the beach on Saturday, I was confused about what you wanted..." Toby: "When you left the house last night in the middle of our fight, I felt abandoned and angry. I think that's why I picked a fight with you this morning over the orange juice."
- **Clarify what you would have them do differently**, and how that would help you change your own part. A husband: "I want to do a better job of listening... One thing that would help me to listen is if you could first ask me how my day was, and whether this is a good time to talk... Is there anything that would make that difficult?" A specific request works "in the service of helping you change yours."

"The goal isn't to get an admission. The goal is to understand better what's happened between you."
