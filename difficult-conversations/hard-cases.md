# Hard Cases

## When they have bad intentions: lying, bullying, threats, derailing

You can't know their intentions. A lie that looks "obvious" and self-serving is often what they actually believe. But people do lie, manipulate, threaten, delay, obfuscate, bully and derail. Three pieces of advice:

1. **Be careful about rewarding bad behavior.** Giving in "to avoid the hassle" teaches them it pays. Giving in is reasonable when you've been persuaded they're right, when they care a lot and you care little, or when any solution now beats none. In the long run, though, "what gets rewarded gets repeated."
2. **Beware of reacting in kind or "playing their game."** Protect your reputation; don't lie back. "Being trustworthy is not the same as being trusting." If they ask "Don't you trust me?", answer: "Actually, I don't know you well enough to be sure, and if you are telling the truth I assume you have no problem offering verification or a guaranty." Stay focused on your objective. Mutual volatility can work when both people are comfortable with it and "make the same meaning out of it." Many people aren't.
3. **Seek to understand why they think their intentions and actions are justified.** They feel justified, usually by how they think you have treated them. There is a logic to their behavior, and that logic is what makes persuasion possible.

*Example:* Colin and Matt are 50-50 co-owners of a Web design firm. Matt exploded over branding: "I'm sick of you always playing it safe!" Then he threatened: "I'll pull the plug and sell my interest in the partnership if we don't do this my way." Colin gave in, then next time yelled louder himself, and it escalated. Imagine Matt's side: he may feel that Colin bullies by vetoing every change, passive-aggressively. Each is caught in a vicious cycle of act and react.

- **Name the dynamic.** Try this after persistent, good-faith listening and problem-solving have failed.
  - Make the other side's implied rule for decisions explicit: "When I get upset, I get my way."
  - Apply the **test of reciprocity**: "What if we were both using this strategy?"
  - Stay clear of attributing intent (not "You try to get your way by being angry").
  - Start from the Third Story and invite them in: "You feel strongly that you want to start over with our branding materials. I like them the way they are. When you and I strongly disagree, how should we decide what to do?"
  - If he answers "if we don't do it my way, I'm quitting": "So one way we can decide is based on who's more willing to quit. That doesn't seem like a sustainable decision-making process or one likely to produce the best decisions. I think a better approach is for us to step back and look again at our goals for these materials..."
  - Hold the stance: "I'm open to persuasion, but I'm not yet persuaded. And I'm the one who determines when I am. Your anger underscores how strongly you feel, but doesn't affect the legitimate data, reasoning, and principles that would help persuade me."
- **Clarify consequences.** An "enough is enough" speech fails. Matt's internal voice will answer "You're crazy, I don't get angry," and he has already given his own speech ("sick of playing it safe"). Settle the matter internally first, then focus on what you see, think, want and will do. The key points:
  1. Here's what I see.
  2. Here's the impact on me.
  3. You may disagree with my perceptions or feel that your behavior is justified.
  4. It doesn't matter which one of us is right about that. Our current way of interacting doesn't work for me.
  5. I am asking you to change this behavior.
  6. If it continues, here's what I'm going to do.

  The purpose is not to control them but to give them information for an informed choice. Think carefully about what the real consequences will be.

The same applies to people who keep changing the topic or attacking. First understand them and problem-solve in good faith. If the derailing or bullying persists, name the dynamic and clarify the consequences.

## Genuinely difficult people, and mental illness

Much difficulty comes from an intersection (see [contribution.md](contribution.md)), but some people really are more difficult. For depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, addiction, OCD, narcissism or ADD, communication helps, but support from family, colleagues, community and mental health professionals matters more. Two things to know:

1. **These syndromes have an internal logic.** OCD rituals are self-medication against anxiety. Addiction trades short-term pain for short-term pleasure (or reduced pain). Understanding the illness from the inside gives insight into how to help, and into what you do that worsens the symptoms. Seeing that the disease is compelling them dampens its impact on you, though it doesn't relieve them of responsibility.
2. **Less extreme difficulty** shows up as someone unpredictably emotional, quick to anger, brittle or self-absorbed. You say a coworker was "only gone for three days!" and they hear "Why were you on vacation for so long!?" Some people attribute negative intent more often and more certainly than others, which creates self-fulfilling loops.

There is no guarantee. "If you define success by what you can get others to do, you cede to them control of the outcome." Make your goal a productive interchange, and make sure your own actions aren't contributing. The approaches below flow from getting inside the other person's perspective.

### Addy's story

Addy's Aunt Robin feuds with Addy's mother and screams things like "Your mother's a goddamn liar!" Addy tried explaining her mother's view, sharing the impact on her, showing empathy, pointing out Robin's contribution, and losing her temper. Nothing helped. What did:

- **Distinguish helping from fixing.** Addy wanted not only to help Robin but to *fix* her. That is the illusion of control (Max in *Where the Wild Things Are* commanding "be still"), and giving it up is the first step.
- **Think about what might be going on for Robin.** She needs connection: "Negative interaction is better than no interaction at all." She wants to feel loved, to belong, and to have a valued role, and she feels unheard.
- **Consider the Big Reframe.** When the conversation is stuck, knock it off its center with big energy from a different direction: "Well, my mother sure can be a pain in the butt! Goodness knows, she's not perfect! Maybe everyone in our family is a pain in the butt. But one thing I know is that my mother loves you, and you love her, and I know that means a lot to both of you." It works through humor and lightness, a strong empathy move, and expressing love while giving Robin a role. (A patrolman who talked hundreds of people back from the Golden Gate Bridge used a Big Reframe. He didn't argue about jumping, but asked "What's your plan for tomorrow?", and if they had none, said "Well, let's make one.")

### Peter's story

Peter is the boss of Lucera, a brilliant pharmaceutical scientist. She rejects feedback ("Look at the source"), claims she has already toned down her criticism, and says her reports are jealous.

- **Recognize "blind spots."** These are signals outside the communicator's awareness. "The big three blind spots are tone of voice, facial expressions, and body language. The listener is very aware of these, the talker is not." Lucera sees a friendly "new Lucera," while her eye-rolling and tone leak disgust. Jealousy is the only explanation she can find for the feedback, which makes this a "self-sealing perceptual deficit."
- **Give Lucera the missing data.** For example, let her watch herself on videotape, if she is sensitive to such cues in others. It will be painful. Then she can entertain other explanations and rethink how she communicates.
- **Address the implications of the alternate view.** "Let's put aside for a minute the question of whether this complaint is true and instead ask what if it were true? What would it mean? What would be the implications for you?" This addresses the identity threat directly.

### Matamba's story

Matamba's new boss was hierarchical and rigid, with a sense of retaliation. When the group stood up to him, things escalated rapidly and destructively, and naming the dynamic didn't work. What helped:

- **Recognize that aggression can spring from fear.** A consultant saw that the boss was terrified. He was ambitious and concerned with appearing successful, and his outbursts came when subordinates proposed an untested course. He feared losing face and failing in public.
- **Clarify and validate the concern.** Ask: "What are you concerned might happen if we go in this direction?" A plausible scenario emerges. Then: "Wow, that would concern me, too! So let's make sure that doesn't happen. What if we agree to do this, so it can't happen?" The boss: "Oh. That would be fine. As long as that's clear, do whatever you want."

### Two final thoughts on difficult people

- **Remember joint contribution.** The danger is letting yourself off the hook. A challenging personality becomes more challenging when you are provocative, and "just because he's paranoid doesn't mean they're not out to get him." Enlist neutral observers to check your view.
- **Patience and persistence pay.** Resistance to acknowledging their own contribution is often an identity threat. Help them frame their contribution in a way that preserves their identity and allows a more nuanced view of themselves. These ideas are novel to most people, and "by the time someone starts to show a bit of openness to collaborative dialogue, we have so lost heart that we fail to recognize their subtle overtures."

## When the other person has all the power (the boss)

"People align their actions with implicit incentives, not official rhetoric." The question is not whether it would be righteous to get fired for speaking up, but what it costs and what it gains, and speaking up with skill may earn long-term respect. In surveys, most bosses believe their employees see them as effective and caring, while only a minority of employees do. Carrying bad news upward is like "reversing the direction of a waterfall."

- **Use the power of influence.** Control is the "unilateral ability to make something happen"; influence is the "ability to affect someone else's thinking." Acknowledge that they are the decision maker: "I know there are lots of factors you have to take into consideration, and at the end of the day, I'm onboard with whatever you decide. I just want to make sure that as you think about it, you are aware that...." Then tell the boss what matters to you and why. Bosses often don't know. ("Have you told your boss this is important to you?" "Well, I assume my boss must know.")
- **Use the language of request instead of the language of joint contribution.** Avoid "Part of the reason I wasn't able to get this in on time was that you waited until Friday afternoon." Say: "I'm totally committed to ensuring that this doesn't happen again. We've identified three things that I need to do differently: [x, y, and z]. Something else that would be really helpful to me is if I had more lead time on the more complicated projects. If I can get the assignments on Wednesday instead of Friday... I don't know to what extent that's possible on your end. What are your thoughts on that?"
- **Listen!** It quiets the boss's internal voice.
- **Say what's in it for the boss.** "I want to make this initiative a great success. To do that I need a little more help in making sure I understand the logic well enough to execute effectively." Be genuinely open to learning. If yours is the third new manager to recommend the approach, there may be some validity in it. To justify more talk: "I'm sure we'd all like to move on quickly. At the same time, I imagine it is even more important to all of us that this work..."
- **If the boss refuses to talk**, still avoid the hit-and-run. Put a time on the calendar with undivided attention and a defined length. Start from the Third Story and say why it concerns the boss: "I want to talk about how we raise things, as I think it's affecting morale and productivity." Or: "I want to talk about the best way to disagree with you in a meeting. What's your advice?" If the boss treats any such discussion as weakness, prepare, exhaust the other angles, and then approach the boss anyway, because the boss is the right person.
- **The abusive boss.** Robert Sutton's *The No Asshole Rule* says not to tolerate them.
  - *Why they bully:* mental illness, having learned it works, the culture, or a perk of success. Bullies are as likely to be women as men. Many are blind to it: "I wasn't angry; the numbers were bad." Anger justifies itself.
  - *Your identity:* neither take the feedback at face value nor reject it wholesale. Evaluate your work independently.
  - *Work around them:* deal with the boss only when required, cultivate other relationships, and avoid triggers. It isn't all-or-nothing; look for small wins (a three-day weekend, some input).
  - *Formal channels:* HR, a union complaint, banding together with colleagues, or a warning that you'll leave.
  - *Your alternatives:* ask "What are my alternatives?" and "Is this worth it?" Knowing there is some way out gives you confidence. "Nothing builds confidence quicker than the knowledge that you can survive the worst that can happen."
  - Sutton's advice: reframe the abuse as not your fault, and fight the little battles you can win.

## When you're the boss or the parent: why not just tell them?

"You can." Make decisions early and efficiently, explain them clearly, and own the implementation. But telling alone rarely succeeds. When a car stalls, pressing harder on the gas is like more "telling." You need to diagnose the cause (gas? transmission? battery?). When there is joint contribution, one-way commands are unlikely to solve the problem.

**Decision modes:**

- *Commanding*: I decide, and I tell you my decision.
- *Consulting*: I ask for your input, then I decide and tell you.
- *Collaborating or negotiating*: we decide together.
- *Delegating*: you decide.

Say which mode you're using. When people think they were collaborating and you were only consulting, they feel you "heard their advice, you didn't take their advice." "Role clarity up-front is the key."

**The And Stance applies.** Take a rule against teen drinking and driving. The teen is not consulted on the rule, *and* you listen to their thoughts, feelings and questions about how it will work:

- Is getting high the same as drinking? Do three blocks count as driving?
- How will they contact you? Will they be disciplined if they drink and then call for a ride?
- What pressures do they face around drinking, popularity and intimacy?

Probation script: "I understand that you don't think being put on probation is fair. Let's talk about that. I want to hear your view and share mine. Before we do, I want to clarify, though, that I'm not negotiating this or deciding in collaboration with you. I've already made the decision. The purpose of talking is to figure out why we've had a disconnect on this, so that we can try to avoid doing it again as we go forward, and so I can perhaps give you more effective coaching."

Mapping a contribution system "doesn't mandate a particular solution or change who owns the decision." Suppose an employee takes twice as long as expected. After mapping the contributions, they want to fix the things that slow them down, and you conclude the role doesn't play to their strengths and move them to a role with less responsibility. "If you are the decision maker, make the decision."

**Do I always have to start with listening and inquiry?** No. A boss who opens with leading questions sounds absurd ("How do you think you're doing...?" "Great." ... "But don't you think it was late?"). Assert instead: "Let's talk about the Vancouver Project. It was three days late. Let's figure out why, assess the impact, and decide how to avoid this going forward." Then move into inquiry, mixing inquiry and assertion. "The touchstone, as always, is purpose... Use questions when you want to learn and statements when you have something to convey." The book stresses listening only because failing to listen is the more common mistake. A self-reminder: "I may feel upset, I may feel like I already know their view, but there's always something I need to learn. In addition to asserting, I'm going to need to inquire, and then inquire some more."

## Across cultures

"Most Americans don't talk this way either": the book's language is stylized for conceptual clarity, so translate it. The underlying structure is the same worldwide: blame in South Africa and South Carolina, feelings in India and Iowa, identity quakes ("saving face") in Turkey and Tennessee. What differs is whether, when and how the internal voice gets expressed. Stereotypes (Americans are direct, the British are buttoned-up, Asia is hierarchical) are too simple. In Japan, for example, people signal through body language, word choice and silence. Directness is tied to assumptions about how to protect the relationship, but "you don't have to choose between protecting the relationship and having a good conversation." It is "genuine listening, empathy, and being open to persuasion that preserve and strengthen the relationship, not the degree of agreement." A useful internal stance: "You may be right, and I may well be missing something (it wouldn't be the first time), and yet so far I don't see it." "There's no way to throw a diplomatic hand grenade, whether you're speaking French, Arabic, or Mayan." In cross-cultural and virtual teams, talk about unintended impacts, and get curious about why colleagues in Uruguay or Uganda care about what they care about.

**The internal voice in each conversation:**

| | Difficult conversation | Learning conversation |
|---|---|---|
| What Happened? | Whose story is right (either/or). | "I wonder why we see things differently? What is each of our data and reasoning?" |
| | "They meant to have this impact on me." | "I don't like the impact they are having on me; I wonder what they were intending? I know my intent; I wonder whether that was the impact?" |
| | "This is their fault." | "We have each contributed to this result. Let's identify contributions and figure out how to fix this." |
| Feelings | "My feelings are their fault, and I should either let them have it or keep quiet (since it probably won't do any good)." | "My feelings say something about me and something about their actions. I can share my feelings without blame, and acknowledge theirs with empathy, without saying their story is right." |
| Identity | "They're attacking my identity unfairly! I am not...!" | "Realistically, some part of what they're saying may make painful sense. What am I really afraid of here? How can their story have validity without negating who I am, and vice versa for them?" |

## E-mail and phone

E-mail is efficient, allows reflection and leaves a record, but "E-mail isn't dialogue – it's serial monologue." It carries no tone, face or body language. "I was hoping to have your memo by now," all caps, or "You're such a loser! ☺" can each be read many ways. E-mail can be the most emotional channel of all: certain names in the inbox provoke dread, and the exchange happens onstage, with the boss cc'd or a message forwarded with "Can you believe this?" Texting reduces it to half sentences.

**When reading:**

1. **Question your attributions.** You don't know their intentions. A week without a reply may just mean they're busy, and their attack may be a defense against what they took as yours.
2. **Hit pause.** When you feel strong negative emotion, do nothing. "Because I'm really mad right now" is not a reason to reply. Wait at least an hour, ideally overnight.
3. **Pick up the phone or talk in person.** "You can't resolve an e-mail conflict with e-mail. For all practical purposes there are no exceptions to this rule." Anything written in a conflict can be taken the wrong way, even "all that 'I'm calm and rational' crap."

**When writing:**

1. **Be extra explicit about your intentions, reasoning, and (when appropriate) your emotions.** "I'm asking because my boss will ask, and I want to be sure we're aligned." "I get frustrated when this happens" is better than "FINE! WHATEVER YOU SAY."
2. **Let them know if there will be a delay.** "Let me check with Dan, and I'll get back to you in a few days. If you haven't heard from me by Tuesday, please send a reminder."
3. **Take it step-by-step.** Check the tone first: "I can't tell if you're annoyed. Was I supposed to respond earlier?"
4. **Ask for reactions, thoughts, and what you're missing.**

**On the phone** you have tone but no face. It's easy to slide into problem-solving ("You have to get help") or cheerleading ("Everything is going to be fine!"), for example with a mother coping with a father's dementia. Make an extra effort at empathy ("Mom, I can't even imagine how stressful this has been for you") and appreciation.

## When to give up

Some people ask for help with an "impossible" teenager, brother, boss or partner, then dismiss every suggestion ("tried that," "nope," "won't work"). They are really asking for permission to give up. The authors' answer: "Sounds like you've done your best and tried just about everything."

The work is slow, especially when your openness isn't reciprocated. After months of reaching out and feeling rebuffed, you may notice "a slight tremor": an appreciative note about the holidays, a less irritable voicemail, the usual opening to an argument passed by. If those gestures make a genuine difference, and you can negotiate with yourself to be patient, they may be enough to sustain you.

But don't beat your head against the wall or stay indefinitely in a relationship that damages your self-esteem. You may be making progress with your paranoid boss while your family suffers your deepening Monday dread. Your sister's addiction may keep wreaking havoc with your marriage despite all your empathy. **"You're allowed to give up."** "When you finally give up the idea that you have the power to change others, you are giving up something you never had anyway – control." "All you can do is take a hard look at yourself, be open to seeing things differently, change your own contribution, and be honest about what matters to you. You can extend an invitation to join you in working to improve things. It's up to them whether to RSVP." Giving up requires a difficult conversation with yourself about a healthy choice, and forgiving yourself. "That may be the toughest conversation of all, but one well worth having."
