# Difficult Conversations

A **difficult conversation** is "anything you find it hard to talk about." It is hard when you feel vulnerable, when your self-esteem is implicated, when the issues are important and the outcome uncertain, and when you care deeply about the topic or the people. Examples: asking for a raise, ending a relationship, giving a critical performance review, saying no to someone in need, confronting disrespectful behavior, disagreeing with the majority, apologizing. Everyday ones include returning merchandise without a receipt or telling the painters not to smoke in the house.

**The Dilemma: Avoid or Confront.** If you avoid it, you feel taken advantage of, your feelings fester, and you rob the other person of the chance to improve. If you confront it, things may get worse. "There Is No Such Thing as a Diplomatic Hand Grenade": a difficult message, "coated with sugar, thrown hard or soft," still does damage. And "choosing not to deliver a difficult message is like hanging on to a hand grenade once you've pulled the pin." Tact helps, but it is not the answer. The answer is to get out of the business of delivering messages altogether. Shift from the **message delivery stance** to the **learning stance**: hold a **learning conversation** instead of a **battle of messages**. "At heart, the problem isn't in your actions, it's in your thinking." Eliminating fear is unrealistic. The realistic goal is to reduce fear, manage what remains, and get better results at tolerable odds. "For every case that is truly hopeless, there are a thousand that appear hopeless but are not."

## The Three Conversations

The thoughts and feelings in every difficult conversation fall into three conversations. Each brings its own challenge and predictable errors.

**The "What Happened?" Conversation** is the disagreement over what happened or should happen: who said what, who did what, who's right, who meant what, who's to blame. It has three fronts, and each carries a crippling assumption:

- **The Truth Assumption**: "I am right, you are wrong." These conversations are almost never about getting the facts right. They are about conflicting perceptions, interpretations and values: "not about what a contract states," but "about what a contract means." "They are not about what is true, they are about what is important." The cure is to explore each other's stories ([stories.md](stories.md)).
- **The Intention Invention**: "we assume we know the intentions of others when we don't. Worse still, when we are unsure about someone's intentions, we too often decide they are bad." Intentions are invisible, so we make them up from behavior. The cure is to disentangle intent from impact ([intent-and-impact.md](intent-and-impact.md)).
- **The Blame Frame**: talking about fault "produces disagreement, denial, and little learning." What happened is usually "the result of things both people did — or failed to do." The cure is to map the **contribution system** ([contribution.md](contribution.md)).

**The Feelings Conversation** asks: Are my feelings valid? Appropriate? Should I acknowledge them or deny them, put them on the table or check them at the door? What about the other person's feelings? Difficult conversations "are at their very core about feelings": "Engaging in a difficult conversation without talking about feelings is like staging an opera without the music. You'll get the plot but miss the point." Unaddressed feelings leak in anyway ([feelings.md](feelings.md)).

**The Identity Conversation** is "the conversation we each have with ourselves about what this situation means to us." It turns on three questions: Am I competent? Am I a good person? Am I worthy of love? It decides whether you keep your **balance**. It is the most subtle conversation and the one with the most leverage: "anytime a conversation feels difficult, it is in part precisely because it is about You, with a capital Y" ([identity.md](identity.md)).

| | Battle of Messages | Learning Conversation |
|---|---|---|
| What Happened? (more complex than either side can see) | I know all I need to know. Goal: persuade them I'm right. I know what they intended. Goal: let them know what they did was wrong. It's all their fault (or all mine). Goal: get them to admit blame and make amends. | Each of us brings different information and perceptions. Goal: explore each other's stories. I know what I intended and the impact their actions had on me; I can't know what's in their head. Goal: share the impact on me, find out what they were thinking, and find out what impact I'm having on them. We have probably both contributed to this mess. Goal: understand the contribution system. |
| Feelings (emotionally charged) | Feelings are irrelevant (or my feelings are their fault and they need to hear about them). Goal: avoid feelings (or let 'em have it!). | Feelings are the heart of the situation; they are usually complex, and you may have to dig. Goal: address feelings (mine and theirs) without judgments or attributions. Acknowledge feelings before problem-solving. |
| Identity (threatens our identity) | I'm competent or incompetent, good or bad, lovable or unlovable; there's no in-between. Goal: protect my all-or-nothing self-image. | There may be a lot at stake psychologically for both of us; each of us is complex, neither is perfect. Goal: understand the identity issues for each of us, and build a more complex self-image to keep my balance. |

**The two-column view.** In the right column, write what was actually said. In the left, write what each person thought and felt but didn't say. "There's an awful lot going on ... that is not being spoken": the real action is in the left column. The gap between what you think and what you say is part of what makes the conversation difficult.

The learning stance moves "from certainty to curiosity, from debate to exploration, from simplicity to complexity, from 'either/or' to 'and.'" Once the three conversations are sorted out, "you may find that you no longer have a message to deliver, but rather some information to share and some questions to ask."

## Applying the book

- **Before a conversation**: run the five steps below with the user in order. The outputs are their preparation notes, a decision about whether to raise the issue, and an opening line from the Third Story.
- **After a conversation that went wrong**: draw the two-column view. Find where it derailed: an argument about who's right, an invented intention, blame, a feeling translated into a judgment, or an identity quake. Then plan the next conversation, often by talking about how to talk about it ([third-story.md](third-story.md)).
- **During a conversation**, or against someone who attacks, interrupts, blames or won't engage: use the moves for leading the conversation, which are listening, reframing and naming the dynamic ([taking-the-lead.md](taking-the-lead.md)).
- **On the receiving end** of criticism, an accusation or bad news: listen past the accusation for the feelings and acknowledge them. Ground your identity. Hold your own view alongside theirs with the And Stance.
- Write every script in the user's own situation, following the book's patterns. Speak only for the user: what they saw, how they feel, what they intended. Treat the other person's intentions and story as hypotheses to learn about.

## The five steps

### Step 1: Prepare by walking through the Three Conversations

1. **Sort out What Happened.** Where does your story come from? Where does theirs? What impact has this situation had on you? What might their intentions have been? What have you each contributed to the problem?
2. **Understand emotions.** Explore your **emotional footprint** and the **bundle** of emotions you are experiencing ([feelings.md](feelings.md)).
3. **Ground your identity.** What's at stake for you about you? What do you need to accept to be better grounded ([identity.md](identity.md))?

Lay out the preparation notes under these headings:

- **Multiple Stories**: "What's my story?" and what's their story?
- **Impact/Intent**: my intentions; the impact on me; their intentions?; the impact on them?
- **Contribution**: what did I contribute? what did they contribute?
- **Feelings**: what feelings underlie my attributions and judgments?
- **Identity Issues**: how does what happened threaten my identity?

[jack-and-michael.md](jack-and-michael.md) shows a full set of notes.

Step 1 is done when every heading has entries, and when:

- "their story" is told so that their conclusions make sense within it;
- their intentions are listed as guesses;
- both contribution lists have entries;
- each judgment of them has the feelings under it named;
- the identity at stake is named.

Expect the step to shake your confidence that you're right. That means it is working: you leave more curious and more grounded at once.

### Step 2: Check your purposes and decide whether to raise it

- **Purposes**: What do you hope to accomplish? Shift your stance to support learning, sharing and problem-solving.
- **Deciding**:
  - Is this the best way to address the issue and achieve your purposes?
  - Is the issue really embedded in your Identity Conversation?
  - Can you affect the problem by changing your contributions?
  - If you don't raise it, what can you do to help yourself let go?

The **three purposes that work** are learning their story, expressing your views and feelings, and problem-solving together. Purposes that fail are changing the other person, short-term relief at long-term cost, and the hit-and-run. If the user is letting go, use the four liberating assumptions ([purposes.md](purposes.md)).

Step 2 is done when the user has made a clear-headed choice: either raise it with purposes that work, or let go deliberately. Silence that is "running away rather than making a clear-headed choice to let go" does not count.

### Step 3: Start from the Third Story

1. Describe the problem as the difference between your stories. Include both viewpoints as legitimate.
2. Share your purposes.
3. Invite them to join you as a partner in figuring it out.

The **Third Story** is "the one a keen observer would tell, someone with no stake in your particular problem." For example: "Jill, you and I seem to have different preferences about when the dishes get done or beliefs about when they should be done. I wonder if that's something we could talk about?" The same file covers what to do when they open first, delivering bad news, making requests, and revisiting conversations gone wrong ([third-story.md](third-story.md)).

### Step 4: Explore their story and yours

- **Listen to understand**: ask questions, acknowledge the feelings behind arguments and accusations, paraphrase to check your understanding, and unravel how you got here. Inquiry, paraphrasing, acknowledgment, and the internal voice are covered in [listening.md](listening.md).
- **Share your own viewpoint**: your past experiences, intentions and feelings. Entitlement, the heart of the matter, subtext and easing in, the Me-Me And, and "always"/"never" are covered in [expression.md](expression.md).
- **Reframe, reframe, reframe**: from truth to perceptions, from blame to contribution, from accusations to feelings. The reframe table, the You-Me And, and naming the dynamic are in [taking-the-lead.md](taking-the-lead.md).

The order is never a tidy Third Story, then their story, then yours. It is interactive, and the conversation will "go up and down." To move from listening to your own story, use a **transition sentence**: "I'm beginning to get a sense for how you're seeing things, and that's helpful to me. I also want to try to give you a sense for how I was seeing things, and for how I was feeling." Keep asking two questions: "What else feels important for you to say? What else feels important for you to learn?"

### Step 5: Problem-solving

- Invent options that meet each side's most important concerns and interests.
- Look to standards for what should happen. Keep in mind the standard of **mutual caretaking**: "relationships that always go one way rarely last."
- Talk about how to keep communication open as you go forward.

Test perceptions jointly, and remember that it takes two to agree. If you still can't agree, consider your alternatives ([taking-the-lead.md](taking-the-lead.md)).

## Rules that hold at every step

- "You can't move the conversation in a more positive direction until the other person feels heard and understood." It is always a good time to listen.
- Acknowledge feelings before problem-solving. When accused, acknowledge their feelings first, and only then clarify your intentions.
- "You can't change other people"; mutual learning is what produces change ([purposes.md](purposes.md)). Measure success by how you conducted yourself, not by their reaction.
- Expect defensiveness, and expect to slip back into blame. Stay persistent and redirect.
- Most difficult conversations are "a series of exchanges and explorations that happen over time." Plan follow-up conversations to check in.
