# Getting Started: Begin from the Third Story

The beginning is the most stressful moment of a difficult conversation. It is also the moment with the greatest leverage over where the conversation goes.

## Why our typical openings don't help

- **We begin inside our own story.** We start from precisely the place the other person thinks is causing the problem: "Our story sends up flares."
- **We trigger their Identity Conversation from the start.** Each opening below carries an implicit message about the other person:

| Opening line | Implicit message about them |
|---|---|
| "If you contest Dad's will, it's going to tear the family apart." | "You're selfish, ungrateful, and don't care about the family." |
| "I was very upset by what you said in front of our supervisor." | "At worst, you betrayed me – at best, you were stupid." |
| "Your son Nathan can be difficult in class – disruptive and argumentative. You've said in the past that things at home are fine, but something must be troubling him." | "Your son is a troublemaker, probably because you're a bad parent who's created a lousy home environment. What are you hiding?" |

By leaving their story out, these openings set up a contest between the two versions.

## Step one: begin from the Third Story

"Every difficult conversation includes an invisible Third Story. The Third Story is the one a keen observer would tell, someone with no stake in your particular problem." Think of how a city planner would describe a conflict between cyclists and drivers, or a counselor a marriage, or a mutual friend two friends.

- **Think like a mediator.** Mediators can't impose an outcome the way judges and arbitrators can. Their tool is "describing the problem between the parties in a way that rings true for both sides simultaneously," which gets two people with different stories to sign on to the same description. The formula can be learned, and you don't need to be impartial to use it.
- **Not right or wrong, not better or worse — just different.** "Describe the gap – or difference – between your story and the other person's story."
  - *Jason's story:* "Do I have to do everything around here?... health risk." Even the tactful version is still his story: "Jill, we need to talk about your problem with getting the dishes done."
  - *Jill's story:* "You are so annoyingly anal about the dishes... You need to relax."
  - *The Third Story:* "Jason and Jill have different preferences around when the dishes are done, and different standards for what constitutes appropriate or obsessive cleanliness. Each is unhappy with the other's approach."
  - *The opening line:* "Jill, you and I seem to have different preferences about when the dishes get done or beliefs about when they should be done. I wonder if that's something we could talk about?"
- You don't need to know their story; just acknowledge that it exists: "My sense is that you and I see this situation differently. I'd like to share how I'm seeing it, and learn more about how you're seeing it."

**The three openings rewritten:**

- *The will:* "I wanted to talk about Dad's will. You and I obviously have different understandings of what Dad intended, and of what's fair to each of us. I wanted to understand why you see things the way you do, and to share with you my perspective and feelings. In addition, I have strong feelings and fears about what a court fight would mean for the family; I suspect you do too."
- *The supervisor:* "I wanted to talk to you about what happened in the meeting this morning. I was upset by something you said. I wanted to explain what was bothering me, and also hear your perspective on the situation."
- *Nathan:* "I wanted to share with you my concerns about Nathan's behavior in class, and hear more about your sense of what might be contributing to it. I know from our past conversation that you and I have different thinking on this. My sense is that if a child is having trouble at school, something is usually bothering him at home, and I know you've felt strongly that that's not true in this case. Maybe together we can figure out what's motivating Nathan and how to handle it."

Stepping out of your story doesn't mean giving up your point of view. Take the estate example: you think Dad meant his estate to go evenly to his three children; your brother thinks Dad meant it to go equally to the seven grandchildren, three of whom are his sons. Even if you never agree, you can express feelings, understand each other, and find a process (an arbitrator, probate court) that protects the relationship. "Separate the substantive disagreements from the importance of the relationships."

**If they start the conversation, you can still step to the Third Story.** "Take whatever the other person says and use it as their half of a description from the Third Story." Jill opens: "We need to talk about how you ruin all our meals by being so obsessive about the dishes." Jason: "It sounds like you're pretty unhappy with how I handle the dishes. I have trouble with how you deal with the dishes too, so I think we each have different preferences and assumptions around that. It seems like that would be a good thing for us to talk about..."

## Step two: extend an invitation

Describe the problem in a way both sides can accept, propose mutual understanding and problem-solving as your purposes, check whether that makes sense to them, and invite them in.

- **Describe your purposes.** Saying you want to understand their perspective, share your own, and talk about how to go forward makes the conversation less mysterious and less threatening. It shows "this isn't a campaign to change them."
- **Invite, don't impose.** An invitation can be turned down. You are not "setting *the* description" but "offering and discussing a possible description and purpose." It is a joint task, open to modification.
- **Make them your partner in figuring it out.** Offer them an appealing role.
  - In a salary negotiation, "I can see that we have different ideas about what salary makes sense here" is fine. Adding "and since you're new at this, I can tell you how it's usually done" casts them as a neophyte and will "sink the ship."
  - "Can you help me understand . . . ?" casts them as an advisor.
  - "Let's work on how we might . . . ." makes them a partner.
  - "I wonder whether it's possible to . . . ." poses a challenge and offers them the hero's role.
  - The role must be genuine. Casting them as the villain is not more genuine.
  - You can share your internal struggle: "The story I'm telling in my head about what is going on is that you are being inconsiderate. At some level I know that's unfair to you, and I need you to help me put things in better perspective. I need you to help me understand where you are coming from on this." Their role then becomes "someone who can help me get my perspective back."
- **Be persistent.** Ruth's ex-husband, Brian, kept missing his time with their daughter, Alexis (Lexi). Ruth: "It seems to me that we're having a hard time being clear with each other about how likely it is that you'll make it for your time with Lexi." Brian apologized, explaining a crisis on the shop floor, and braced to be yelled at. Ruth kept to the bigger picture. She had thought their plans were definite; he had thought they were tentative ("if I could get away"). "So you and I are misunderstanding each other. I'd like to sort this out, because it's awfully hard on Alexis when you and I get our signals crossed. Can we spend some time trying to figure this thing out?"

## Some specific kinds of conversations

### Delivering bad news

It should still be a conversation, and "it's usually best to put the bad news up front." Don't trick them into saying it for you ("So, what do you think of the relationship?"), and don't spend two hours discussing "issues" when you have already decided to break up. Script: "We've talked a lot about how important it is to you for us to come home for the holidays, and also how difficult it is financially and emotionally for us to do that. I'm calling because Juan and I have talked a lot about it, and have decided that we are going to spend this Christmas here with the kids. It was a really difficult decision, and I feel bad about disappointing you. I wanted to let you know as early as I could, and to talk a little bit, if you'd like, about your reactions and our thinking." With good news and bad news together, make clear you have both. You might discuss where to start, or share them in a logical order.

### Making requests: "I wonder if it would make sense . . . ?"

Don't make it a demand. Invite an exploration of whether, say, a raise is fair. Your boss has information you don't, and "you can't know that you deserve a raise until you've explored the issue." Script: "I'd like to explore whether a raise for me might make sense. From the information I have, I think I deserve one. [Here's my reasoning.] I wonder how you see it?" You may learn that you deserve none, or more.

### Revisiting conversations gone wrong: talk about how to talk about it

When a topic keeps going badly (a son's grades, a wife's finances, racism in the department met with eye-rolls), you get cast as the nag. Treat "the way things usually go when we try to have this conversation" as the problem, and describe it from the Third Story.

- On race and promotion: "I know that in the past when I've raised the question of who's getting promoted and what role race plays in that process, people have sometimes felt accused or exasperated. I don't mean to accuse anyone... At the same time, it feels important to me to discuss. I'm wondering whether we could talk about how we each react to that conversation, and whether there's a better way we could address these issues?"
- To an overbooked friend: "I definitely get the sense that you don't like discussing your schedule, at least not the way I bring it up. The problem for me is that I feel worried and I would like to share why in a way that's helpful. I don't seem to know how to do that, and I was wondering if you had any advice." It turned out the friend needed someone who would just listen and be supportive, without giving advice.

## A map for going forward: Third Story, their story, your story

The Third Story is the base of the mountain. From there, explore all Three Conversations from each side's perspective. **What to talk about:**

- **Explore where each story comes from.** "My reactions here probably have a lot to do with my experiences in a previous job...."
- **Share the impact on you.** "I don't know whether you intended this, but I felt extremely uncomfortable when...." / "When you didn't call, I was frantic with worry."
- **Take responsibility for your contribution.** "There are a number of things I've done that have made this situation harder...."
- **Describe feelings.** "I'm anxious about bringing this up, but at the same time, it's important to me that we talk about it...." Empathize: "If I were you, I'd be pretty frustrated at this point."
- **Reflect on the identity issues.** "I think the reason this subject hooks me is that I don't like thinking of myself as someone who...." / "Being fair is so important to me. It's upsetting to think that the way I handled this situation might not have been fair to you."

The order is never tidy; the conversation moves back and forth. The how comes next: listening ([listening.md](listening.md)), expression ([expression.md](expression.md)), and leading the conversation and problem-solving ([taking-the-lead.md](taking-the-lead.md)).
