# What's Your Purpose? When to Raise It and When to Let Go

Rules like "Never talk politics at the dinner table," "Don't raise anything before 8 a.m.," or "Never disagree with your boss" are "nonsense." Only you can decide whether to raise something. There is no single "right choice," and no way to know one in advance. Looking for one right answer is a "useless standard... crippling." The goal is to *think clearly* and make a considered choice.

**Work through the Three Conversations first.** Sort your feelings, your identity issues, and the distortions and gaps in your story. Separate what you know (your own feelings, experiences, story and identity) from what you don't (their intentions, perspective and feelings). The insight often answers the question for you, either "Raising this is important, and now I have some ideas..." or "having a conversation probably won't help."

## Three kinds of conversations that don't make sense

Ask three questions.

### 1. Is the real conflict inside you?

Carmen works full-time, and her husband, Tom, stays home. Yet she does all the scheduling (carpool, doctor, piano) and resents Tom for "dropping the ball." Her identity work showed that she was *keeping* control. She felt ambivalent about working, felt guilty, and was jealous that their daughter went to Tom first. The scheduling was her assurance that she was a good mother. She handed some of it to Tom. She also reframed the rest as "things I've chosen to take on to stay involved, rather than things he's let slide."

### 2. Is there a better way to address the issue than talking about it?

"Sometimes actions are better than words."

- *Walter*: a pediatrician in St. Louis.
  - **The pressure.** His mother, on the family farm in northern Missouri, kept asking when he was coming home, and suggested he take old Doc Denny's job.
  - **His Three Conversations.** He felt frustrated and resentful, *and* appreciative that she missed him. He was grateful for his roots, and sad that his kids were missing their grandmother and the farm.
  - **Her real message** was that she missed being part of their lives.
  - **His contribution.** He cut the conversations short and didn't call for weeks. She felt more disconnected, so she asked more.
  - **What he changed.** He phoned more, sent notes about the kids, and invited her to visit just for fun. When she asked about moving home, he shared both his satisfaction with his practice and his regret. His daughters spent a summer on the farm.
  - **The result.** The questions decreased, and they grew closer.
- *Fran*: a workers'-rights lobbyist.
  - **The exchange.** Fran asked a toll collector for quarters instead of dimes for the 50-cent toll. He exploded: "Where do rich people like you get off feeling so high-and-mighty entitled? Doesn't it even occur to you that I might be giving you dimes for a reason?" ... "You don't have the first idea what my job is like."
  - **Where her anger came from.** It came from denying unpleasant truths about herself, which conflicted with her self-image. She had felt entitled, hadn't wondered about his constraints, and appears wealthy.
  - **What she did.** Once she saw this, her anger faded. She wrote to the Turnpike Authority about the collectors' conditions: they are limited in the change they get and can't leave the booth. That led to a creative solution.

### 3. Do you have purposes that make sense?

We launch conversations the way NASA might "launch someone into space and figure things out." A purpose that is off base is a "destination that is impossible to reach."

- **Remember, you can't change other people.** Changes rarely come from arguments, facts or persuasion. "The paradox is that trying to change someone rarely results in change. On the other hand, engaging someone in a conversation where mutual learning is the goal often results in change." "They are more likely to change if they think we understand them and if they feel heard and respected. They are more likely to change if they feel free not to."
- **Don't focus on short-term relief at long-term cost.**
  - *What happened.* Janet had twenty years in nonprofit financial management. When a board member, Sylvie, questioned her competence, Janet "let her have it." She felt great "for about fifteen minutes," then realized "she could treat me this way, and I'd just made it more likely that she would." A purpose of changing, venting at, or telling someone off produces the very consequences you fear.
  - *A better purpose.* Janet can shift to curiosity about why Sylvie reacts as she does. She can ask: "Is it something Janet is doing? Is Sylvie aware of the impact...? Is this the way Sylvie has gotten results in the past? What advice can Janet offer Sylvie on how to get a better reaction from her?"
  - *Why it helps.* Shifting your purposes can "lower the threshold" of risk.
- **Don't hit-and-run.** Hit-and-run looks like this: "I'm sorry to hear that. Oh, by the way, you still owe me $500." Or "Late again, eh?" Or, at the sight of your son's beer bottles, "I see you're still drinking up a storm." It makes you feel better ("At least I've said something") and makes them defensive. The rule: "If you're going to talk, talk. Really talk." Plan a time, and say explicitly whether you want ten minutes or an hour. "If hit-and-run is all you can muster, it's better not to raise the issue at all."

## Letting go

Letting go is not magic. Your son may still not call, your boss may still lose his temper, and your mother may never understand how abandoned you felt. Sometimes you decide not to have the conversation and let go. Sometimes the situation "has you by the throat." There is no formula, and it takes time. It means telling the story differently: giving up the role of victim or villain for more complex roles. Don't believe you *should* have gotten over it by now, but it isn't only a matter of time either; there is plenty you can do.

**Adopt some liberating assumptions:**

1. **It's not my responsibility to make things better; it's my responsibility to do my best.** Karenna decided her relationship with Paul *had* to work, and that making it work was her job. Accept your limits: "The best you can do is try."
2. **They have limitations too.** They agree to change and then repeat the behavior. They "are as imperfect as you are," and may not have the capacity to be different, at least not now. Alison is a bossy big sister. Her brother can accept her as she is, and work on the identity issues that let her get to him.
3. **This conflict is not who I am.** People integrate a conflict into their identity: the least favorite son, the long-suffering wife, the oppressed group. When the merger handed control to Rob's losing faction at his firm, he felt uneasy rather than relieved. In ethnic conflicts, identity can be defined by who we're against, so reconciliation itself threatens. "Remember why you are fighting... for what is right and fair, not because you need the conflict to survive."
4. **Letting go doesn't mean I no longer care.** David's brother was murdered over a drink in a poker game. David was angry at his brother too, and worked toward forgiving both men. "Letting go of my rage and indignation doesn't mean I have to let go of my love for my brother or my sense of loss... I can miss him terribly without the clutter." "Letting go, at heart, is about how to handle with skill and grace not having a difficult conversation."

## If you raise it: three purposes that work

The gold standard is "working for mutual understanding. Not mutual agreement, necessarily."

1. **Learning their story.** Ask about their side across all three conversations:
   - What information do they see that we missed?
   - What past experiences influence them? What is their reasoning?
   - What were their intentions? How did our actions affect them?
   - What do they think we're contributing?
   - What are they feeling? What does this mean to them, to their identity? What's at stake?
2. **Expressing your views and feelings.** Do this "to your own satisfaction." You can't count on them being moved. Say what is important about your views, intentions, contributions, feelings and identity issues.
3. **Problem-solving together.** What would improve the situation? Brainstorm creative ways to satisfy both of you. Where your needs conflict, use equitable standards.

**Stance and purpose go hand in hand.** Purposes that work need the learning stance, and holding that stance is what makes them reachable.

## Who has time for all this?

"Nobody." But the choice we imagine is a **false choice**: (a) spend time tackling the conversation, or (b) save the time and have the problem magically disappear. A more realistic assessment:

- **"We're already spending time and energy dwelling on it."** We fume, vent to colleagues, complain to our spouses, design workarounds, lie awake rehearsing what we *should* have said, and look up their personality disorders on the Internet.
- **"How we're spending our time is actually making it worse."** Triangulating to a colleague drags them into the conflict, and it relieves us of the need to address it directly. Complaining to a friend reinforces our one-sided story, and since we rarely give them permission to challenge us, we leave more righteous than before.
- **"Instead, aim energy in useful and efficient directions."** Ask friends for genuine advice and coaching, not agreement. They can help you see the other side's perspective, your own contribution, the other side's intentions, the identity issues making you overreact, and the heart of the matter.

With practice, you can run through the checklist on your commute, or with the conference call muted for a minute or two. **"Spend seven minutes now and save seven hours later."** Not every conversation with your name on it needs tackling, and many can be quick. The earlier you raise an issue, catch a misunderstanding, or ask a question to clarify intentions, the sooner it's cleared up. For example, seven minutes on why you and a client seem to have different expectations about a project's scope saves seven hours (or seven months) later. Asking a supplier why your request poses a problem, rather than insisting, puts you on the same side. Difficult conversations run in "dog years": they feel seven times longer than they are. People who check the phone display after a call that "lasted forever" often find it took four minutes, "and addressed the real issues."
