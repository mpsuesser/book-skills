# Stop Arguing About Who's Right: Explore Each Other's Stories

This file is the cure for the **Truth Assumption** ("I am right, you are wrong"). Disagreement is not bad in itself, and arguing feels natural. "But it's not helpful."

## Why arguing fails

- **We think they are the problem.** We label them as *selfish* (a girlfriend who won't go to couples' counseling), *naive* (a daughter moving to New York for the theater), *controlling* (a boss who insists on his way) or *irrational* (Great Aunt Bertha, who won't replace her sagging mattress). Then we assert harder, try to educate them, or call them on it. The argument goes nowhere, and everyone feels unheard and powerless. "The first thing we should do is hear from Aunt Bertha." For Bertha, the mattress was the one she shared with her husband for forty years. It felt safe, a haven amid all the changes and her one source of control. And she complained to connect, not to get answers.
- **"We Each Make Sense in Our Story of What Happened."** "We don't see ourselves as the problem because, in fact, we aren't. What we are saying does make sense," and what the other person is saying also makes sense. Each person is a visiting character in the other's story. Difficult conversations arise where stories collide. We blame the collision on what the other person is like, but really the stories are just different: "It's as if Princess Leia were trying to talk to Huck Finn."
- **Arguing blocks us from exploring each other's stories.** We trade conclusions, "the 'bottom line'." Neither conclusion makes sense inside the other person's story, so each is dismissed, and the result is a battle of messages.
- **Arguing without understanding is unpersuasive.** "Telling someone to change makes it less rather than more likely that they will." "People almost never change without first feeling understood." *Example:* Trevor, a financial administrator, kept pushing a social worker, Karen, about late paperwork. Then he shifted to understanding her first: she was overwhelmed, putting her energy into needy clients, and felt unappreciated. Once he understood, he explained in detail the extra work her lateness caused him. She promised to prioritize the paperwork, and she has.

"To get anywhere in a disagreement, we need to understand the other person's story well enough to see how their conclusions make sense within it. And we need to help them understand the story in which our conclusions make sense." Understanding is the essential first step, though not always the solution.

## Where our stories come from

Stories are built "in often unconscious but systematic ways," up a ladder from the **Available Information**: **1. Our Observations** (we take in information), **2. Our Interpretations** (we give it meaning), **3. Our Conclusions**. "At each step, there is an opportunity for different people's stories to diverge." We usually trade only the conclusions, "without stepping down to where most of the real action is: the information and interpretations." In any disagreement, step down the ladder on both sides.

1. **We have different information.**
   - **We notice different things.** Four-year-old Andrew called it "the best truck parade I've ever seen!" His Uncle Doug noticed no trucks at all. What we notice is tied to who we are: whether we attend to feelings and relationships, status and power, or facts and logic. Randy (white) and Daniel (Korean American) worked on the same assembly line and described the company's racial record so differently that "it's as if Randy and Daniel work at different companies." Each assumes he has "the facts."
   - **We each know ourselves better than anyone else can.** Only you know your own constraints, hopes and fears. Jack knows the heat was off at midnight and his wife was angry about the canceled dinner. He doesn't know that Michael's client blew up that morning, that the revenue figures were a hot topic, or that Michael's designer had taken unscheduled leave. The rule: "rather than assuming we already know everything we need to, we should assume that there is important information we don't have access to."
2. **We have different interpretations.** (In *Annie Hall*, one partner says they "never" have sex and the other says "constantly," and both mean three times a week.)
   - **We are influenced by past experiences.** Bonnie and her coworkers treated their supervisor, Caroline, to dinner, and Caroline complained about the prices. Bonnie decided she was a cheapskate, then asked her. Caroline grew up in the Depression with "a nickel on the counter" for her lunch. By age eight or nine it was no longer enough, and she never told her mother.
   - **We apply different implicit rules.** "We get into trouble when our rules collide." Thelma's rule: "It is unprofessional and inconsiderate to be late." Ollie, who arrived at 7:10 for a 7:00 meeting, has a different rule: "It is unprofessional to obsess about small things so much that you can't focus on what's important." Rules take the form of "should" and "shouldn't." Make your rules explicit and encourage the other person to do the same, which avoids an "accidental duel of conflicting rules."
3. **Our conclusions reflect self-interest.** In Howard Raiffa's study, sellers valued a company about 30 percent above its independent fair market value and buyers about 30 percent below it. Roger Fisher: "I sometimes failed to persuade the court that I was right, but I never failed to persuade myself!" This calls for humility.

## Move from certainty to curiosity

- Instead of "How can they think that?!" ask "I wonder what information they have that I don't?"
- Instead of "How can they be so irrational?" ask "How might they see the world such that their view makes sense?"

"Certainty locks us out of their story; curiosity lets us in." *Example:* Keiko caught her own certainty when Tony watched football instead of visiting his sister's newborn, and "negotiates herself to a place of curiosity." For Tony, the game was the one relaxing time in a week of ten-hour days, kids, and listening to her day, and he believed his sister wouldn't care whether he came today or tomorrow. Then Keiko shared her story, and they worked it out together.

**What's your story?** Get curious about what you don't know about yourself: your own information, your interpretations, and your implicit rules. Jack's rule was "You should always show appreciation to others no matter what," which is "not a truth, just a rule." Michael's rule was "Good friends can get angry with each other and not take it personally." Andrew is "truck obsessed" only compared with Doug's baseline, and Doug might be "cheerleader obsessed."

## Embrace both stories: adopt the "And Stance"

"Don't choose between the stories; embrace both." This doesn't mean pretending both are right. "First work to understand it. The mere act of understanding someone else's story doesn't require you to give up your own." The world is complex:

- you can feel hurt *and* they can too;
- they're doing their best *and* you think it's not good enough;
- you did something stupid *and* they contributed;
- you are furious *and* you love them.

The And Stance lets you assert the full strength of your views without diminishing theirs. When one person wants the window open and the other wants it closed, asking "who's right" makes no sense. The most useful question is not "Who's right?" but "Now that we really understand each other, what's a good way to manage this problem?"

### Two exceptions that aren't

- **"I really am right."** Two clerics: "You can do God's work your way, and I'll do God's work His way." You are right that your daughter's smoking is bad, but "that's not what the conversation is really about." The real conversation is about your fear, sadness and rage at feeling powerless; her need for independence and to break out of the "good girl" mold; her ambivalence; and what she should do and what your role is. With a friend's alcoholism, being right won't help either. Tell him the impact his drinking has on you, and try to understand his story: what keeps him in denial, what admitting it would mean, what gets in the way. "Merely being right doesn't do you much good."
- **Giving bad news.** Firing someone, breaking up, cutting a supplier's orders by 80 percent: here you can impose the outcome, and most of the difficulty lies in the Feelings and Identity conversations. Understanding is not agreeing. It doesn't diminish your power to implement the decision and to "be clear that your decision is final." "The And Stance is probably the most powerful place to stand when ... deliver[ing] or enforc[ing] bad news." A breakup script: "I'm breaking up with you because it's the right thing for me [here's why], and I understand how hurt you are... and I'm not changing my mind... and I don't think that makes me a bad person... and I know I might regret this decision, and I'm still making it.... And, and, and." **"'And' helps you to be curious and clear."**

Understanding doesn't mean the differences disappear or that all views are equally valid. It helps you judge whether your own strong views make sense. "Before you can figure out how to move forward, you need to understand where you are."

## Facts, stories and truth

- **Facts aren't relative, but they can be hard to pin down.** A 15% tip on a $30 bill is $4.50, not $6.00: that's a fact. Whether 20% is the "correct tip" is a judgment, even if a survey says it is customary. "Distinguish clearly between facts on the one hand, and opinions, assumptions, values, interests, predictions, and judgments on the other." That a five-year-old threw his dinner is a fact; how to discipline him is a judgment. When you arrived is a fact; your boss's view that lateness means a poor work ethic is an assumption. Memory is unreliable, and each act of recollecting rewrites it ("version 18"). When facts clash, ask: "Is it simple error, a lack of information, misinformation, or selective and revised memories, or are the facts themselves more ambiguous than you had appreciated?"
- **Not all stories are equal, but it takes a learning conversation to find out.** More reasonable stories take account of more of the available information. They rest on fewer or less extreme assumptions, or assumptions closer in time and place. They make fewer logical leaps and contain fewer internal contradictions. To compare stories, you must first explore each one. When the other person seems "just plain wrong," re-examine your own assumptions and "inquire into their view looking for the sense rather than the nonsense in it." Paraphrase it, share where and why you differ, and ask for their reaction. Look for different information, different interpretations of ambiguous information, and different assumptions about missing information. "Most of us give up too soon." Before you do, ask what they would need to learn to reconsider; if they say "nothing," that tells you further persuasion is a waste. Ask yourself the same question.
- **Absolute truth.** Whether or not some truths are absolute, our ability to perceive them is limited: "We are all thousand-watt souls with forty-watt bulbs." "The critical question is less whether there is absolute truth than whether and how well we can perceive it." Argue with passion but with humility. "We have no power to make other people see something as we do, and our urgent attempts at one-way persuasion usually result in increased resistance."
- **Passionate divides** (government, health care, abortion, immigration, climate): "The more passionate we are about the issues that matter most to us, the more likely we are to have a cartoonish view of those who see things differently." They hold a cartoon of you too. "With a passionate viewpoint comes the responsibility to be informed about the issues involved and to listen to how people with a different point of view see them," at least so you understand how your neighbor sees it.
