# Ground Your Identity: Ask Yourself What's at Stake

Much of the anxiety in a difficult conversation comes from facing ourselves. Identity is "the story we tell ourselves about ourselves." *Example:* Ben, a software VP, accepted a job elsewhere and was terrified to tell his boss, who was also his mentor. Ben's father had been loyal to one company all his life, and Ben asked himself: "Am I really the loyal soldier I like to think I am, or just another greedy jerk willing to betray someone for the right price?"

## Three core identities

Three identity questions come up most often in difficult conversations:

- **Am I competent?** A chemist asks about salary. The supervisor replies: "I'm surprised you want to discuss this. The truth is, I've been disappointed by your performance this year." The chemist thinks: "Maybe I'm not the talented chemist I thought I was."
- **Am I a good person?** A man breaks up with Sandra, and she cries. "The hardest thing for me in life is hurting people I care about." He ends up telling her he loves her and everything will work out.
- **Am I worthy of love?** You confront your brother about how he treats his wife. He says: "Who are you to tell me how to act?! You've never had a real relationship in your whole life!" You can hardly breathe.

**An identity quake can knock us off balance.** "Maybe I am mediocre." The reaction is physical, because our images of ourselves and our future are hardwired to the adrenal response. We feel a rush of anxiety or anger and want to get away. "Your supervisor is explaining why you're not being promoted; you're busy having your own private identity quake."

Mild loss of balance looks like losing confidence or concentration, or forgetting what you meant to say. Extreme loss of balance looks like paralysis, panic, the urge to flee, or trouble breathing. For example, the head of Creative looks betrayed when you turn down her proposal, and you stammer that it might be reconsidered, with no reason to believe that.

**There's no quick fix.** You can't "quake-proof" your sense of self. A husband cries when you say you want no more children. A coach tells you to "Grow up" when you raise discriminatory treatment. Grounding may require mourning a cherished self-image.

## Vulnerable identities: the all-or-nothing syndrome

The biggest source of vulnerability is all-or-nothing thinking. It makes identity unstable and hypersensitive to feedback, and it leaves only two choices: deny or exaggerate. "All-or-nothing identities are about as sturdy as a two-legged stool."

- **Denial** tries to keep incoming information consistent with the self-image. The chemist tells himself: "My boss knows business, but not chemistry..." It is "like trying to swim without getting wet," and it costs huge psychic energy. "The bigger the gap between what we hope is true and what we fear is true, the easier it is for us to lose our balance."
- **Exaggeration** flips the self-image: "I'll probably never amount to anything. Maybe I'll even get fired." **We let their feedback define who we are.** You deliver a hundred memos on time and the 101st late, and you conclude "I can never do anything right." The feedback fills the "whole identity screen." Other examples: a waitress gives you a funny look, so you're cheap. You don't help your friends paint, so you're selfish. Your brother says not to visit the kids, so you're an uncaring aunt.

## Ground your identity: two steps

**Step one: become aware of your identity issues.**

- **Watch for the symptoms.** A usually articulate person stumbles. An empathetic person interrupts. A calm person boils over.
- **Find the pattern and ask why.** Notice what knocks you off balance, then ask: "What about your identity feels at risk? What does this mean to you? How would it feel if what you fear were true?"
- *Example:* Jimmy kept an emotional distance, "rational to a fault," to protect himself from his family's emotional shrapnel. Later he changed, but he was afraid to show the change to his family. A friend asked, "What are you really afraid of? What's the downside?" His first answer was about obligation: "Someone in my family has to be the rational one." The deeper answer: "What if they reject me? What if they laugh?" He persisted. It was awkward at first, and his family relationships became more genuine.

**Step two: complexify your identity (adopt the And Stance).** Move away from the false choice between "I am perfect" and "I am worthless." Everyone is a mix of good and bad behavior, noble and less noble intentions, wise and unwise choices. "No one is always anything."

- *Example:* Ben made sacrifices, worked weekends, turned down offers and recruited talent, *and* he is leaving for higher pay.
- It is reasonable for his boss to feel abandoned. That doesn't make Ben bad or greedy: he has kids' college to pay for, and he was under-compensated for years.
- "The bottom line is that there is no bottom line." A complex self-image is "healthy and robust," a sturdy foundation.

## Three things to accept about yourself

The more easily you can admit these, the better your balance:

1. **You will make mistakes.** Rita told a mutual friend about Isaiah's alcoholism, which she had promised to keep confidential, to ask for advice. She argued that she was helping, until she realized she couldn't admit she had violated his trust. Once she admitted it, they got somewhere. Competent people who take mistakes in stride are seen as confident and "big enough." People who resist admitting mistakes are seen as insecure. "No one is fooled."
2. **Your intentions are complex.** Sally was breaking up with Evan and feared he would accuse her of using him during a lonely period. There was some truth in it. Her answer: "As I think about it, some of what you're saying makes sense. I was lonely, and being with you helped. I don't think that was my only reason... There are lots of pieces to what was going on for me."
3. **You have contributed to the problem.** Walker's daughter, Annie Mae, checked into the university health clinic with an eating disorder. Their exchanges stayed on the surface: "How are you doing, kiddo?" / "I'll be okay, Dad." He feared she thought he hadn't been a good father: he was away a lot, wasn't supportive, and broke promises. He could tiptoe around it, or work through his identity issues in advance and accept his contribution. He chose the second, and could say: "I wish I'd been there for you more often. I'm so sorry and sad that I wasn't." He met her with compassion instead of fear.

## During the conversation: learn to regain your balance

O Sensei, the founder of Aikido: "I am constantly losing my balance. My skill lies in my ability to regain it."

- **Let go of trying to control their reaction.** If one of your purposes is that they not react badly, you are in trouble: "you can't control their reaction — and you shouldn't try."
  - *Telling the kids about a divorce:* the urge to soothe them is partly self-protection ("Maybe I'm a rotten dad, as well as a lousy husband"). Smoothing over their feelings makes things worse. "Everything will be all right" is heard as "I don't understand how you feel" or "You're not allowed to be upset by this." Adopt the And Stance instead. Your purposes are to tell them the news, show your love, say that you honestly believe things will be okay, *and* give them space to feel.
  - *Firing someone:* "Don't measure the success of the conversation by whether or not they get upset." Your purposes are to give the news, take responsibility for your part (but not more), show you care how they feel, and be helpful going forward.
- **Prepare for their response.** Imagine the conversation. Might they cry, sulk, withdraw, pretend they're fine, attack, or reject you? For an identity check, imagine their most difficult response and ask: "What do I think this says about me?" Then: "Is it okay for me to make someone cry? How will I respond? What if they attack my character or motivations?"
- **Imagine that it's three months or ten years from now.** Look back from the future. What will you have learned? How will you feel about how you handled it? What advice would your future self give you?
- **Take a break.** "I'm surprised by your reaction to this and would like some time to think about what you've said." Even ten minutes, a walk, or some air helps. Use the time to check yourself:
  - Check for distortions.
  - Check for denial: "In what ways is what they are saying true?"
  - Check for exaggeration: "What is the worst that could happen here?"
  - Ask what you might do to turn it around.

## Their identity is also implicated

Annie Mae feared she would never be good enough to please her father. Walker can lead her away from all-or-nothing thinking. He can tell her that everyone needs help, and "I'm proud of you for getting help." He can tell her he loves her not because of her A's but because she is his daughter.

**Raising identity issues explicitly.** Some identity issues are irrelevant to the other person, for example a colleague who reminds you of a former boyfriend; work those out on your own. Others are helpful to name:

- "What I'm sensing this is all about is whether I'm a good spouse or not. Is that how you're feeling too?"
- "I've always regretted not saying something at Dad's funeral. That's why it's so important for me to speak at Mom's."
- "I'm sensitive to criticism of my writing style. I know I need the feedback, but it's something for both of us to be aware of..."

The authors are "astounded how often difficult conversations are wrapped up in both people reacting to what the conversation seems to be saying about them."

**Find the courage to ask for help.** After rape, war, illness, addiction or loss, suffering in silence for a long time may be "more reckless than brave." Test: if someone you loved were in your situation, would it be okay for them to ask for help? Asking also gives the other person an opportunity.

## When the identity conversation stays stuck in either-or

*Antonio's story:* when criticized, at work or at home, Antonio criticizes back, even when he knows the feedback is right. He resolves to say "That's helpful, I'm going to work on that," but in the moment the old pattern takes over. His root belief is that a good and valued person doesn't disappoint people: "If I know anything about myself, it's that I don't hurt others, I don't let others down." To avoid intolerable guilt or shame, he decides the criticism *must* be wrong or ill-intended, and he points to others' mistakes. He sees only two choices: "preserve the pillar (denial), or let things collapse (exaggerate)."

**Where identity comes from.** "Identities develop from the complex interplay of our hardwiring, life experiences, and what we *choose* to make of those experiences (in other words, the story we tell)." We can't change our wiring or our past experiences, "but we can change our story about those experiences."

- Children assume they deserve whatever they get, and build a story to explain why: "I got spanked because I am bad."
- We form images of ourselves in *relationship* to others. A middle child in a conflict-ridden family is rewarded as the peacemaker, "the rational one, who never loses her temper." As an adult, she has trouble sticking up for what she wants.
- Antonio's parents divorced when he was five, and he concluded: "My dad left because I was bad. I will never permit myself to be bad again." Someone else might take a different lesson from a divorce: "People leave, so don't rely on them."
- New feedback threatens the **pillar**, so he lashes out: "It wasn't me! It couldn't be!"

Bringing the dilemma to light, recognizing all-or-nothing assumptions, and resetting your expectations may not "stick." These help:

- **Explore the roots of the theme, and re-evaluate them.**
  - *Antonio:* he remembered his father labeling his brother Hector a "failure," and his own fifth-grade prize meeting only "That's nice, Antonio." He concluded that love was contingent on success. Years later his mother told him they had been proud, but a school psychologist had warned them against over-praising gifted children. His father regretted being harsh with Hector. "Love was not as contingent as Antonio thought, it was just being expressed in ways he didn't understand."
  - *Hector:* he learned his school trouble came partly from dyslexia and a big-picture learning style. Even so, he kept declining leadership roles, until a friend asked: "What would *you* say to a fifteen-year-old in that situation?... You are nothing like the kid you were then."
  - *Limits:* trauma can bring physiological changes, and "there is not infinite play in the joints." If you had few friends in high school, you can't decide you were Mr. Popular. "Coming to grips with your experience is not about making things up; it's about looking under the simplistic identity labels we give ourselves, putting events in context, and, if necessary, grieving the fact that things happened as they did." Positive labels can hurt too: being labeled "smart" makes some people risk-averse. "We are all fast and slow, strong and weak, motivated and lazy in a thousand tiny ways throughout our days that the generalizations simply don't capture."
- **Create positive experiences.** Act *as if* something is true, as an experiment. Antonio might seek out negative feedback with the mindset of "someone who is great at learning from feedback." If he listens and works at it, then at least this once it is true, which gives him an experience "manufactured but real."
- **Enlist help to stimulate and reinforce new behaviors.** Under stress, old pathways take over, so ask peers to help: "I'm working on taking feedback. I don't find it easy or natural, but you can help me by pointing out when I get defensive. I'm giving you explicit permission to coach me on this, to be direct. At the same time, I'm asking you to be patient. I'll surely mess up, and when I do, please remember that I'm trying hard."
- **Give yourself some empathy.** "Accepting our *whole* selves – our mistakes, failures, and shortcomings, our moments of weakness, selfishness, and stupidity – and forgiving ourselves these, are essential steps toward finding balance now and growth in the future." "It's not about making excuses or shifting responsibility toward others. It's the simple intention to accept and care for what *is*." If we're not satisfied, "we can apologize and grieve, and we can try for better starting right now."
