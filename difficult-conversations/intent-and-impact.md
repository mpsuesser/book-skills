# Don't Assume They Meant It: Disentangle Intent from Impact

This file is the cure for the **Intention Invention**. Intentions shape our judgments: a car blocking your driveway makes you react one way if it is an ambulance and another if it is a BMW.

**The battle over intentions.** At a party, Leo said, "Lori, why don't you lay off the ice cream?" Lori, who struggles with her weight, replied: "You act like you're my father... You have this need to control me or put me down." Leo answered, "I wasn't trying to hurt you... You're so defensive." The same fight recurs because each of them makes one of the **two key mistakes**:

- **Lori's mistake**: she assumes she knows Leo's intentions.
- **Leo's mistake**: he assumes that once he has clarified his good intentions, Lori has no reason to be upset, so he never learns what she feels.

## First mistake: our assumptions about intentions are often wrong

Intentions "exist only in their hearts and minds. They are invisible to us."

- **We assume intentions from the impact on us.** "We feel hurt; therefore they intended to hurt us." This is automatic, and we don't notice it is an assumption.
- **We assume the worst.** A friend is late to the movie, so he's a jerk. Margaret was annoyed that her gruff hip surgeon had extended his vacation, and imagined him in the Caribbean. In fact he was on a "working vacation... helping set up a hospital in Bosnia." That didn't erase the inconvenience, but she felt substantially better. E-mail and voicemail invite the worst reading. A customer writes "I don't suppose you've gotten to my order yet...": sarcastic? angry? or knowing you're busy?
- **We treat ourselves more charitably.** When your husband forgets the dry cleaning, he is irresponsible. When you forget the airline tickets, you are overworked.
- **Are there never bad intentions?** Sometimes there are, but they are "rarer than we imagine, and without hearing from the other person, we can't really know."

**Getting their intentions wrong is costly:**

- **We assume bad intentions mean bad character.** We jump from "bad intentions" to "bad person," see everything they do through that lens, and feel justified in avoiding them or gossiping. To test a label like "That traffic cop is a control freak," "My boss is manipulative" or "My neighbor is impossible," ask what it is based on. If the answer is that you feel powerless, fear manipulation, or are frustrated, the label rests solely on the impact on you, which is "not a sufficient basis."
- **Accusing them of bad intentions creates defensiveness.** Accusatory questions ("How come you wanted to hurt me?", "Why do you ignore me like this?") leave them feeling falsely accused, so they defend or attack. This is the cycle of accusation, with "the two classic characteristics of the cycle: both parties think they are the victim, and both think they are acting only to defend themselves."
- **Attributions can become self-fulfilling.** Your boss gives you little responsibility. You assume she distrusts you, you get demotivated, your work suffers, and she gives you even less.

## Second mistake: good intentions don't sanitize bad impact

Leo's logic is "I have now clarified that I didn't. So you should now feel fine, and if you don't, that's your problem."

- **We don't hear what they are really trying to say.** "Why were you trying to hurt me?" carries two messages: "I know what you intended" and "I got hurt." The accused person answers the first and misses the second. Often "You intended to hurt me" really means "You don't care enough about me." When a father is too busy for his son's basketball game, the son's real point is "You may not have intended to hurt me, but you knew you were hurting me, and you did it anyway." Clarifying intentions is useful; the question is *when*. Do it after you understand their feelings, not at the start.
- **We ignore the complexity of human motivations.** Leo may have been embarrassed, or may want Lori thin for his own sake. Intentions are often mixed. Your willingness to ask the question matters more than the answer. "No, I had good intentions" is a barrier that says "I'm more interested in defending myself." Thinking hard about your own intentions tells them the relationship matters.
- **We aggravate hostility, especially between groups**: union and management, neighborhood organizations and developers, administrative staff and professionals, and issues of difference such as race, gender and sexual orientation. Minority reporters at a newspaper complained, and the editors met behind closed doors without any minority staff. A white editor said, "I can see why you felt excluded. But that wasn't our intention..." and thought the issue was over. But "whether or not the intention was to exclude, people felt excluded."

## Avoiding the first mistake: disentangle impact and intent

Ask yourself three questions:

1. **Actions**: "What did the other person actually say or do?"
2. **Impact**: "What was the impact of this on me?"
3. **Assumption**: "Based on this impact, what assumption am I making about what the other person intended?"

**Hold your view as a hypothesis.** It is a guess, and your reaction may say as much about you as about them. For example, teasing feels hostile to people whose siblings were cruel and affectionate to others. You "can't afford to level an accusation based on tenuous data."

In every exchange, each side sees only half the picture:

- **I'm aware of** my intentions and the other person's impact on me.
- **I'm unaware of** the other person's intentions and my impact on the other person.

**Share the impact on you; inquire about their intentions.** Say what they did, its impact on you, and the assumption you're making, labeled as a hypothesis you are checking. Lori's redo: "You know when you said, 'Why don't you lay off the ice cream'? Well, I felt hurt by that... I felt embarrassed that you said it in front of our friends. Then what I wonder is whether you said it on purpose to embarrass or hurt me. I don't know why you'd want to do that, but that's what I'm thinking when it happens."

**Don't pretend you don't have a hypothesis.** Hiding your view, or saying "I know you didn't mean to hurt me" when you suspect otherwise, is inauthentic. Share your assumptions as guesses to be tested.

**Some defensiveness is inevitable.** Anticipate it, and be ready to clarify what you are and aren't saying. Relieve their need to defend themselves where it's true: "I was surprised that you made that comment. It seemed uncharacteristic of you...." That balance makes it easier for them to own up to any malice.

## Avoiding the second mistake: listen for feelings, and reflect on your intentions

When you are the one accused:

- **Listen past the accusation for the feelings.** An accusation says two things: you had bad intentions, and they were frustrated, hurt or embarrassed. Start by listening to and acknowledging the feelings, then return to intentions.
- **Be open to reflecting on the complexity of your intentions**, rather than insisting "My intentions were pure."

Leo's redo: "Wow. It sounds like what I said really hurt." ... "Well, at the time I was thinking that you'd said you were on a diet, and that maybe I could help you stick to it. But I can see how saying something in front of everyone would be embarrassing. I wonder why I didn't see that?" Lori: "Maybe you were embarrassed to have to say something." Leo: "I could have seen you as out of control, which is a big issue for me." ... "I'm sorry. I don't like hurting you. Let's think about what I should do or say, if anything, in situations like that."
