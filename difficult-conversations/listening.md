# Learning: Listen from the Inside Out

Four-year-old Andrew kept tugging at Uncle Doug, who was on the phone: "I want to go outside." After several rounds, Doug said, "Hey, Andrew. You really want to go outside, don't you?" Andrew said "Yes" and walked off. "We have a deep desire to feel heard." Listening "helps you understand the other person. And, importantly, it helps them understand you."

## Listening transforms the conversation

Greta kept urging her mother to stick to her diabetes regimen: "Mom, you need to stay on the exercise plan..." Her mother: "Please stop hounding me... You don't understand." Greta: "I know it's hard... after a week or two it gets easier." Her mother shut down. "Assertiveness isn't Greta's problem. What's missing from her stance is curiosity."

Greta shifted from persuading to learning, and limited herself to listening, asking questions and acknowledging feelings: "When you say it's upsetting, what do you mean? In what ways?" "I just don't know much about what you think about it, what it means to you, what you feel about it." Then her mother opened up:

- She missed her late husband, who would have handled the rules, and she was lonely.
- She didn't want to be a burden.
- Her own mother had died of diabetes.
- Following the rules made her feel like "some sick old lady."
- She didn't understand the regimen, and her doctor was no help.

Greta acknowledged what she heard: "It sounds like you've been feeling really lonely without Dad." "So keeping to the regimen would feel like accepting something that you don't really totally accept yet?" "Maybe that's something I could help you with." "I want to help. It would actually make me feel better. Not so powerless."

**Listening to them helps them listen to you.** When the other person "won't listen," it's usually because "they don't feel heard," and they think *you* are the stubborn one. Remove the block by "demonstrating that you understand what they are saying and how they are feeling." Experiment: pick the most stubborn person you know. Listen, especially for feelings (frustration, pride, fear), acknowledge them, and see whether they become a better listener.

## The stance of curiosity: listen from the inside out

"The single most important thing Greta has done is to shift her internal stance from 'I understand' to 'Help me understand.' Everything else follows from that."

**Forget the words, focus on authenticity.** People who learned "active listening" from a course often hear "Don't use that active listening stuff on me." "The heart of good listening is authenticity": people read what is going on inside you. "If your intentions are false, no amount of careful wording or good posture will help. If your intentions are good, even clumsy language won't hinder you." So the real questions are: Are you curious? Do you care?

## The commentator in your head: become more aware of your internal voice

The **internal voice** is what you are thinking but not saying, and it is the biggest barrier to listening. (If you're thinking "What are they talking about? I don't have an internal voice," that's the voice.)

- **Don't turn it off, turn it up.** Get to know it. It chatters in all three conversations: "I'm right," "I did not intend to hurt you," "This isn't my fault"; "I'm so furious!"; "Was I really that thoughtless?" It also daydreams ("there's enough meatloaf for the in-laws") and rehearses your reply ("When it's my turn to talk, there are four points I'm going to make").

There are two ways to manage it:

1. **Negotiate your way to curiosity.**
   - **Remind yourself that understanding is harder than it seems.** "If you think you already understand how someone else feels or what they are trying to say, it is a delusion." Recall a time you were sure and one fact changed everything.
   - *Example:* after a movie in which a puppy's mother ran away, six-year-old Jocie was scared. Her mother, Audrey, reassured her: "I would never run away and leave you." In fact Jocie was worried that her new turtle might be someone's mother. (The turtle was itself a baby.) She felt both fear and guilt. Audrey's internal voice, "I know what this is all about," had ended her curiosity.
   - **Keep the purpose in view.** If you are trying to persuade, your internal voice says "Why don't you just do this." If you are trying to understand, it asks "What else do I need to know for that to make more sense?" or "I wonder how I can understand the world in such a way that that would make sense?"
2. **Don't listen: talk.** If you are sitting on pain, outrage or betrayal (or joy or love), listening may be hopeless. "At times, to be able to listen they'll first need to speak," and the same goes for you.
   - *Example:* Dalila's roommate of six months, Heather, lost her job two weeks ago and is moving out tomorrow. Dalila: "I'm sorry you lost your job, and we can talk more about that. Right now, though, I'm feeling worried about how I'll make rent without you. And I'm surprised and frustrated that you waited until now to tell me."
   - **Give a headline.** "I'm surprised to hear you say that. I think I disagree, but say more about how you see it," or "I have to admit that as much as I want to hear what you have to say, I'm feeling a little defensive right now."
   - **If you can neither listen nor talk**, say so: "This is important to me, I want to find a time to talk about it, and right now I'm not able to."

## Three skills: inquiry, paraphrasing, and acknowledgment

### Inquire to learn

"And only to learn." The test is why you asked the question: "The only good answer is 'To learn.'"

- **Don't make statements disguised as questions.** "Are we there yet?" really means "I'm feeling restless." The rule: "If you don't have a question, don't ask a question. Never dress up an assertion as a question." Disguised questions are heard as sarcasm. They usually carry a feeling or a request, which feels vulnerable to say outright. Sarcasm seems safer, but that's an illusion: they reply in kind ("Well, sure, I can focus on you just once"). Say the feeling or the request directly:

| Disguised question | Said directly |
|---|---|
| "Are you going to leave the refrigerator door open like that?" | "Please close the refrigerator door." / "I feel frustrated when..." |
| "Is it impossible for you to focus on me just once?" | "I feel ignored." / "I'd like you to pay more attention to me." |
| "Do you have to drive so fast?" | "I'm feeling nervous." / "It's hard for me to relax when I'm not in control." |

- **Don't use questions to cross-examine.** Questions like "Surely you'd agree that you made more mistakes than I did, wouldn't you?" or "How do you explain the fact that Kate was able to make the sale so soon after you gave up?" are cross-examination. Pull out the embedded statement, share it as your perception, ask for their reaction, and assume they've thought about it: "I understand that you feel you did everything you could to make the sale. To me, that seems inconsistent with the fact that Kate made the sale right after you gave up. What's your thinking about that?"
- **Ask open-ended questions.** Avoid yes/no questions and menus ("Were you trying to do A or B?"). Ask "What were you trying to do?" Use variations on "Tell me more" and "Help me understand better...."
- **Ask for more concrete information.** "What leads you to say that?" "Can you give me an example?" "What would that look like?" "How would that work?" "How would we test that hypothesis?"
  - *Example:* Ross's boss told him she needed evidence of long-term dedication, because "you view this job as a stepping stone." It became "Am so!" "Are not!" The redo: "Say more about how you judge dedication, and what you've observed in me that suggests to you that I'm not as dedicated as you'd like me to be." It turned out she read social events as the sign of dedication, and suspected that working late might be a way of building a record in order to move on. Ross had assumed dedication meant working late and taking on assignments.
- **Ask questions about the Three Conversations.**
  - "Can you say a little more about how you see things?"
  - "What information might you have that I don't?"
  - "How do you see it differently?"
  - "What impact have my actions had on you?"
  - "Can you say a little more about why you think this is my fault?"
  - "Were you reacting to something I did?"
  - "How are you feeling about all of this?"
  - "Say more about why this is important to you."
  - "What would it mean to you if that happened?"
- **Keep digging, and say what's still unclear.** "Okay, so your view is that Kate made the sale because she could offer a reduced price on the service contract. I can see how that would make a difference. What I'm still not clear on, though, is why you couldn't offer that or get permission to offer that. Can you say more about that?"
- **Make it safe for them not to answer.** Telling them there is "no need to be defensive" and pressing on feels like an attempt to control them. Make each question an invitation they can decline without penalty. That builds safety and trust, and they may answer later.

### Paraphrase for clarity

To paraphrase is to "express to the other person, in your own words, your understanding of what they are saying." It does two jobs:

- **Check your understanding.** They can say "No, that's not quite what I meant."
- **Show that you've heard.** If they keep repeating themselves, that's a signal to paraphrase more. Once they feel heard, they listen to you.

*Example:* Ron told his friend Chris he would come over on Saturday. Rachel's response: "Oh, so his religious practices are more important than ours?" because Saturday is temple. In the redo, Ron paraphrases his way down:

- Ron: "It sounds like my making plans is frustrating to you."
- Ron: "So part of the problem is that I made plans without consulting you?" Rachel says no: she hates being the one who nags.
- Ron: "You feel like I make you the one responsible for our religious life." Rachel says she has become the "Shabbat police," and she worries about the message it sends the kids.
- Ron: "So you're afraid that if the kids see me breaking the Sabbath they won't take it seriously?" She also feels lonely going alone, and wants him to want to go.

Then Ron shared his side. He resists pressure. He sees himself as following the spirit of the law: helping Chris through a rough marriage is a way of connecting with community. And he can talk with the kids about caring. "But that might not meet your interest in going to services together... Can you say more about that?"

### Acknowledge their feelings

Ron responded to what Rachel didn't say outright, which was that she felt frustrated. "It is a fundamental rule: feelings crave acknowledgment. Like free radicals, feelings wander around the conversation looking for some acknowledgment to hook onto." Like a child desperate for attention, they keep getting louder until someone notices them.

- **Answer the invisible questions.** Every expression of feeling carries the questions "Are my feelings okay?" "Do you understand them?" "Do you care about them?" "Do you care about me?" Acknowledging answers yes to each one.
- **How to acknowledge.** Acknowledgment is "any indication that you are struggling to understand the emotional content of what the other person is saying." Take the statement "I'm confused by the fact that you lied to me."
  - *These are not acknowledgments:* "Well, it won't happen again." "I should explain that I did not lie." "It sounds like you're overreacting a bit here." The first two address substance; the third judges the feeling.
  - *These are:* "It sounds like you're really upset about this." "This seems really important to you." "If I were in your shoes I'd probably feel confused too."
  - A nod or a look can also do it.
- **Order matters: acknowledge before problem-solving.** "You're working too hard," says your husband. "I never see you anymore." Answering "I'll make a real effort to be home every night by six o'clock" won't satisfy him, because it's "not a math problem." Better: "It's been a tough time, these last few months, hasn't it?" or "It sounds like you're feeling abandoned."
- **Acknowledging is not agreeing.** Keep the Feelings Conversation separate from the What Happened? Conversation. A supervisor transferring a subordinate says: "Sounds like you feel really hurt and betrayed. I can see why that would be upsetting." The subordinate: "So you agree with me that this is unfair?" The supervisor: "What I'm saying is that I can see how upset you're feeling, and it hurts me to see you so upset. I also think I understand why you think this transfer is unfair... I fought hard to make this work. I feel badly about how it's turned out, but I do think it's the right decision, and overall I don't think it's unfair. We should talk about why."

## Empathy is a journey, not a destination

Empathy means imagining what it feels like to be the other person on the inside, and you never fully arrive. We care more that the other person is *trying* to empathize than that they have succeeded: "struggling to understand communicates the most positive message of all."
