# Problem-Solving: Take the Lead

The other person probably hasn't read this book. They will argue about who's right and who's to blame. They may attack and interrupt, worry about being blamed, and distrust your new behavior. **Skills for leading the conversation** are "moves" that work whether or not the other person cooperates:

- **Reframing** puts the conversation back on course.
- **Listening** is "the single most powerful move you can make to keep the conversation constructive."
- **Naming the dynamic** deals with a troubling pattern, especially when the other person dominates.

## Reframe, reframe, reframe

Reframing means "taking the essence of what the other person says and 'translating it' into concepts that are more helpful – specifically, concepts from the Three Conversations framework."

| They say | Reframe to | Example: they say → you say |
|---|---|---|
| Truth | Different stories | "I'm right, and there are no two ways about it!" → "I want to make sure I understand your perspective. You obviously feel very strongly about it. I'd also like to share my perspective on the situation." |
| Accusations | Intentions and impact | "You hurt me on purpose!" → "I can see that you're feeling really angry about what I did, which is upsetting to me. It wasn't my intention. Can you say more about how you felt?" |
| Blame | Contribution | "This is all your fault!" → "I'm sure I've contributed to the problem; I think we both have. Rather than focus on whose fault this is, I'd like just to look at how we got here – at what we each contributed to the situation." |
| Judgments, characterizations | Feelings | "You are the nastiest person I've ever met." → "It sounds like you're feeling really badly." |
| What's wrong with you | What's going on for them | "I am not a bad neighbor!" → "Heavens, I don't think you are either. And I certainly hope you don't think I'm a lousy neighbor. I do think that we disagree about how this should be handled, and I think that's pretty normal between good neighbors. The question is whether we can work together to figure out how to address both of our concerns." |

*Example:* Sydney raised the issue with Miguel.

| Miguel | Sydney's reframe |
|---|---|
| "A team leader should know how to handle this better." | "Rather than focusing on which one of us is to blame, I'm trying to figure out how we got into this spot in the first place. I think we each did – or didn't do – some things that made the situation worse." |
| "You're an engineer, and engineers want certainty." | "I don't know that I agree with that generalization about engineers. But either way, you and I are coming from different perspectives and assumptions. It sounds like you see our interactions as being okay in the context of a working relationship. While to me, your comments felt suggestive and uncomfortable." |
| Their interactions were "normal." | "When you say 'normal,' do you mean normal for two people in just a professional relationship? Or...pursue something further?" |
| "You are overreacting, and you should have brought this up sooner." | "As I said at the beginning, I agree... I think I felt frustrated that I was trying to ignore it, and you persisted. Like when I kept turning down your invitations to have a drink in the bar or take a walk on the beach." |

Eventually Miguel said: "There were times I could tell something was wrong. I suppose I could have also asked you... And maybe we should have just talked about our expectations of each other up front...." Expect to reframe constantly, and to persist.

**The "You-Me" And.** Reframe "either/or" into "and," this time between the two of you: "I can listen and understand what you have to say, and you can listen and understand what I have to say."

*Example:* Stacy is searching for her birth mother. Her adoptive mother, Joyce, says the search will be fruitless and painful.

- Stacy: "You might be right... She might not want to see me at all. And it's still important to me to try. Here's why...."
- Joyce: "After all we've done to love you and raise you, what could you possibly need that your birth mother could provide?"
- Stacy: "It sounds like my search is really hard for you. You're the best mother in the world, and the only mother I'll ever have. That's not going to change. This is hard for me too... sometimes I think I'm just being selfish or ungrateful. At the same time, I have questions that I really want to answer. I hope we can keep talking about what this means to each of us as I begin to pursue this."

## It's always the right time to listen

This is "the single most important rule about managing the interaction": no forward movement until they feel heard and understood.

- If they are highly emotional, listen and acknowledge.
- If they insist theirs is the only version, paraphrase and ask why.
- If they accuse you, understand before you defend.

**Be persistent about listening.** The listener directs the conversation. "Persistence in a difficult conversation means remaining as stubbornly interested in hearing the other person's views as you are in asserting your own." Persistence is not repeating yourself.

*Example:* Harpreet says "I love you." Monisha, a pharmaceutical sales rep who travels a lot, replies "Good night. See you Thursday." Her line is "You know I love you, so why do I need to say it all the time?"

- Harpreet: "When I say that I love you, what are you thinking?" She feels pressured.
- Harpreet: "When you say that I know, how are you thinking I would know?"
- Monisha: "I'm still with you, right?"
- Harpreet: "That's a pretty low standard!" His parents stayed together for years after they stopped loving each other.
- Her side: her parents were sappy to the point of embarrassment. She shows love through kindness; she flew to Phoenix when his mother was sick.

## Name the dynamic: make the trouble explicit

Use this when they keep interrupting, attacking, dismissing you, or acting upset while saying "I'm fine." Put what is happening in the conversation itself on the table, like a "conversation doctor":

- "I've noticed that we keep running out of time whenever we start talking about this. Maybe we should designate an hour..."
- "I've tried to say what I was thinking three times now, and each time you've started talking over me. I don't know whether you're aware that it's happening, but I'm finding it frustrating. If there's something important about what you're saying that I'm not understanding, please share it. And then I want to be able to finish what I'm saying."
- "Here's what I'm noticing. I ask you if you are feeling hurt... and you say, 'No, no, no, of course not...' But then you keep acting toward me in ways that people act when they're hurt or mad at me..." Then invite them to help you figure out what you're doing that might be upsetting them.
- "Hang on a second. Several times now, when I've said the things that are important to me, you've gotten very angry to the point where I feel threatened... If you're trying to intimidate me into changing my mind, it won't work. I really do want to know what's upsetting you, and I want us to find a way to talk about it that doesn't feel intimidating to me."

Naming the dynamic takes the conversation off the substance and may escalate it. It is "best thought of as something to try when nothing else has worked."

## Now what? Begin to problem-solve

Problem-solving means "gathering information and testing your perceptions, creating options that would meet both sides' primary concerns, and, where you can't, trying to find fair ways to resolve the difference."

### It takes two to agree

If you focus on persuading them, you are like a "fish on a hook," and you hand them total control. "You need to persuade them no more and no less than they need to persuade you." Turn the tables: invite them to persuade you, and insist on it. If you are open to persuasion and prepared to live with not agreeing, you can be firm: "I understand that you are determined to have your article reviewed this week, and I'm still not persuaded that I should spend my vacation doing it." This is liberating.

### Gather information and test your perceptions

*Example:* Henry (Hank) planned a weekend away months ago. On Friday his boss, Rosario, says there's a supplier problem, the holiday rush is a month away, and "I need you to stay."

- **Propose crafting a test.** "Divergent views are often rooted in one or more conflicting assumptions or hypotheses." Henry assumes the supplier would rush an order overnight. Rosario has had bad experiences and wants to get it right the first time. Discuss what would be a fair test. Here: call the supplier about availability and willingness. Rosario wants to ask what-if questions and to have a personal relationship with the person responsible. The test must satisfy both of you as fair and adequate.
- **Say what is still missing.** "I think I understand now why stock problems caused us to lose money last year... Yet right now we've got a thirty-day head start on the problem, so I'm not understanding why this weekend is going to make the difference."
- **Say what would persuade you.** "My assistant manager, Bill, has the training to do the inventory this weekend... Maybe you've got concerns about Bill that would be persuasive to me."
- **Ask what (if anything) would persuade them.** "I have offered a number of what seem to me good reasons... Yet you remain adamant... Is there a reason I haven't heard? If not, I'm wondering if there is anything I could say that would persuade you otherwise and, if so, what it would be?"
- **Ask their advice.** "Help me understand how you would feel and how you might think about the situation, if you were in my shoes. What would you do? Why? Could you imagine a way of staying that would not end up making it more likely that something like this would happen again?"

Being known as someone who is not a pushover earns respect, and people give you a wider berth.

### Invent options

*Example:* the neighbors' dog barks all night. It turns out they keep it out for security, and outside at night because they fear it might hurt their new baby, whom it adores. Your answer is to get rid of the dog; theirs is earplugs or closing your windows. Instead, try "determined joint brainstorming": "I wonder if we can work to find a creative way to meet both interests here. What do you think? Are you willing to try?" Ideas: your son, who wants a dog, spends time with theirs; a second dog for company; bring the dog in after 10 p.m. and close the baby's door; you call them when the barking bothers you. The aim is to satisfy "you, them, and the dog."

### Ask what standards should apply

Instead of haggling or intimidating, look for standards and fair principles: the local noise ordinance, how other dog owners handle it, industry or local practice, legal precedents, ethical principles. These can settle a dispute without anyone backing down or losing face. Standards differ in how persuasive they are (how on point, how widely accepted, how relevant), so discuss them.

**The principle of mutual caretaking.** We tend to think our own way is "right": "If you would just change, there wouldn't be a problem." That is not persuasive. "No relationship will endure if one party always gives in." Each person accommodates somewhat, or you take turns: one way on some issues, the other way on others.

### If you still can't agree, consider your alternatives

Weigh accepting less against the consequences of not agreeing. Walking away takes two things:

1. **Explain why**, in terms of the interests and concerns that aren't being met. Henry: "Rosario, I really am sorry. I want very much to be a good employee... Normally, I'm happy to work weekends and nights... It's simply a matter of notice. I feel badly about leaving you in the lurch; at the same time, these plans are really important to me, and I gave you plenty of notice and worked hard all week... So I don't like the choice, but given the choice, I'm going to go."
2. **Be willing to accept the consequences.** Henry may lose his job. He may also find Rosario unhappy *and* more respectful. If he can't live with the consequences, he works the weekend, knowing he handled it skillfully.
