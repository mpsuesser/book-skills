# Worked Example: Jack and Michael, All Five Steps

## What happened

Michael needed a financial brochure by the next day, and his regular designer was out. Jack, a friend and freelance designer, dropped everything and worked late, breaking a dinner date with his wife. Michael approved the mock-up. Then he left a voicemail: "You really screwed this one up! ... The earnings chart isn't presented clearly enough, and it's slightly off. It's just a disaster."

Jack argued that the chart was clearly labeled, and asked, "Why didn't you say something about this when you looked at it this morning?" Michael: "I'm not the one who's supposed to be proofreading... Either you're on the team or you're not. I need a yes or a no." Jack: "Alright, alright. I'll do it." Months later, the relationship was still strained.

**The two-column view.**

- *What Jack thought but didn't say:* "That's the thanks I get?!" "A total overreaction." He was angry with himself for a stupid mistake. He thought "Michael tries to intimidate colleagues into getting his way" and "I feel stuck." He wondered, "Is that how you see me? As a proofreader?" And he resolved, "I'm going to be bigger... The best way out is for me just to be generous and redo it."
- *What Michael thought but didn't say:* he wondered whether he should have hired Jack at all. He had gone out on a limb with his partners to give a friend another chance, and he felt frustrated and confused about whether that had been a good decision.

**Michael's story.** He had gone out of his way to help Jack. "Instead of assuming that the client is always right, he argues with me!" He was angry that Jack made excuses instead of fixing the problem, and the revenue graphs were a critical part of the brochure.

## Step one: prepare by walking through the Three Conversations

**Jack's preparation notes:**

- **Multiple stories.**
  - *My story:* I did a favor and he overreacted. I felt bullied, and got no thank-you. Michael took no responsibility for signing off on the mock-up.
  - *His story:* Michael was counting on me, I let him down, and I argued instead of making it right. ("Hmm. This has some truth to it.")
- **Impact/intent.**
  - *My intentions:* to help a friend, do a good job, and persuade Michael that the mistake was no big deal(!).
  - *Impact on me:* bullied, unappreciated, frustrated.
  - *Michael's intentions?* To get the brochure fast. To make sure it was right? To intimidate me?
  - *Impact on Michael?* Frustrated? Disappointed? In a tight spot with his client?
- **Contribution.**
  - *What did I contribute?* I didn't tell Michael I was upset. I did make the mistake. I didn't ask about his predicament.
  - *What did he contribute?* He also didn't catch the mistake. He didn't call earlier, which made it a rush job. "Are you going to redo it? Yes or no?" felt like bullying.
- **Feelings** (what feelings underlie my attributions and judgments?): angry, frustrated, disappointed (Michael has since hired someone else), hurt, guilty, embarrassed and ashamed. Also appreciative of Michael's past support, and sad that the friendship has fallen by the wayside.
- **Identity issues** (how does what happened threaten my identity?): I'm a perfectionist, and I'm usually good at managing client problems. I got "the worst of both worlds. I didn't stick up for myself, and I still lost Michael as a client and as a friend anyway."

Jack's discoveries:

- Michael probably didn't know Jack had worked through the night.
- Jack can't know whether Michael meant to intimidate him.
- His own contribution included not raising his feelings at the time.

Rethinking his assumptions "succeeded in shaking my confidence that I was right... I didn't really understand this thing at all from Michael's perspective." That left him more open and curious, *and* more confident and grounded. He was now absolutely certain that each of their stories matters.

## Step two: check purposes and decide

Jack feared Michael would brush the conversation off. But not raising it "would have been running away rather than making a clear-headed choice to let go." He reminded himself that he couldn't control Michael's reaction: "I'm raising it because I think it's important... at least I tried."

## Step three: start from the Third Story

- **First try**, from inside his own story: "The problem... was that after all the work I did, you treated me badly, and you know it!" Michael: it was "poor judgment to use you in the first place."
- **Second try**: "Michael, I've been thinking a lot about what happened between us on the financial brochure. I found the experience frustrating, and I suspect you did as well. What's most worrisome to me is that it feels like it has affected our friendship. I wonder whether we could talk about that? I'd like to understand better what was happening for you, and how you felt about working together, and I'd also like to share what I found upsetting."
- **Michael still attacked** ("not careful enough... making excuses"), though less confrontationally. The lesson is to expect defensiveness, persist, listen with curiosity, and attend to the feelings behind his words.

## Step four: explore their story and yours

**Listen first.**

- Jack invited more: "You felt I was making excuses? Say more about that."
- He paraphrased: "So your thinking was that since the graph was off, it was up to me to correct it... And it sounds like when I questioned this, that was frustrating for you."
- Michael explained. His client was breathing down his neck after a dispute over a photo, and "the customer is always right." The revenue chart was the first thing she'd notice, and her investors were unhappy.
- Jack acknowledged it: "I didn't realize some of this background. It sounds like there was a lot going on for you."

**Make the transition to your own story.** A judgment like "You acted badly" won't work. Use a transition sentence instead: "I'm beginning to get a sense for how you're seeing things, and that's helpful to me. I also want to try to give you a sense for how I was seeing things, and for how I was feeling."

**When interrupted** ("Sometimes I think you're too sensitive"), be persistent and assertive. You can interrupt to make space, and say explicitly that you're still explaining: "Before we get into how you feel about how I feel, I just want to tell you a little more about how I'm seeing things." If he interrupts again, the options are to give up (too early), listen more (always good), reassert yourself, or share your frustration at being interrupted. Jack chose: "Michael, I understand you think I'm taking things too personally. We can come to that. Before we do, I want to give you a better sense of where I'm at." Paraphrasing Michael's point took away his need to keep repeating it.

**Jack's story.** He had the Anders materials due the next day and a dinner with Charlotte, and he canceled it. "After making these sacrifices, the first feedback I heard was, 'Gee, Jack, you really screwed this one up!' Can you see why I would feel upset by that?" Michael: "I shouldn't have said that... I meant to say thank you... I was also doing you a favor... giving you the business."

**The conversation goes up and down.**

- Michael: "I get really upset when you try to deny that you've done something wrong." Jack: "I wasn't denying anything. I didn't do anything wrong!"
- Michael was assuming he knew Jack's intentions, and Jack was making the second mistake: defending his intentions before acknowledging Michael's feelings. "First, you have to acknowledge the other person's feelings, and only then should you try to clarify what your intentions were."
- The repair: "I hear that my response was frustrating for you." Then: "Let me try to explain my response. I wasn't trying to pretend nothing was wrong... I genuinely felt that the graph was fine... I see that my reaction wasn't based on all the information."

**Identity.** Jack's reflex about his defensiveness was "That's not true." But in his heart of hearts, he hates mistakes. So he shared his Identity Conversation: "Actually, Michael, as I think about it, admitting mistakes is something I do sometimes have trouble with. Even that is hard for me to say." Then he used the And Stance: "I did make a mistake with the graph, and it was my strong judgment... that the problem was so inconsequential that it didn't need to be redone."

Keep asking: "What else feels important for you to say? What else feels important for you to learn?"

**Joint contribution.** "Your reaction was that since I did the work, the graph problem was my fault. And my initial reaction was that since you looked it over and gave the go-ahead it was also your fault –" Michael explained that when he approved the mock-up, he assumed there were no mistakes. Jack: "We both had a hand in the problem. We misunderstood each other... I should have asked you point-blank whether you had read the brochure carefully, and you might have said more clearly that you had not." Talking about contribution "focuses you naturally on problem-solving."

## Step five: problem-solving

**For future differences of judgment:**

- The client makes the final decision.
- Jack gives his judgment beforehand.
- They make clear what each conversation is for: an opinion, not a decision.
- When Michael lacks time, he says "I don't have time to talk about it" and why (a noon deadline, touchy revenue figures, they'll talk later). "It'll only take five seconds."

**On the friendship.** Jack's first draft was "Making sure that none of this hurt our friendship." That puts words in Michael's mouth and is "a little controlling." An open question works better: "I guess I wonder whether you think this has affected our friendship." Michael saw work and friendship as separate, and had thought the friendship was fine.

Assume there will be more problems. Now it's OK to talk about them. "Life is just one damn thing after another."
