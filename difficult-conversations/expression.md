# Expression: Speak for Yourself with Clarity and Power

**Orators need not apply.** The oratory of a Churchill or a King is no help here. Your task "is not to persuade, impress, trick, outwit, convert, or win over" the other person. "It is to express what you see and why you see it that way, how you feel, and maybe who you are." What counts is knowing yourself and believing that what you share is important, not eloquence.

## You're entitled (yes, you)

First, negotiate with yourself until you "truly believe that what you want to express is worthy of expression – a belief that your views and feelings are as important as anyone else's. Period." John, a second-year law student, was dreading a clerkship meeting with a prickly federal judge. His professor told him, "we are all equal in the eyes of God."

**No more, but no less.** "My views and feelings are as legitimate, valuable, and important as yours – no more, but no less."

Audre Lorde wrote in *Sister Outsider*, after her breast cancer diagnosis: "what is most important to me must be spoken, made verbal and shared, even at the risk of having it bruised or misunderstood... what I most regretted were my silences.... My silences had not protected me. Your silence will not protect you.... We can learn to work and speak when we are afraid in the same way we have learned to work and speak when we are tired..."

**Beware self-sabotage.** Some people are caught between believing they should stand up for themselves and secretly feeling they aren't entitled to. They go through the motions badly: they wait until there's no time left, forget their materials, or watch their points vanish. Then they feel good about having tried and secretly relieved that they failed. This is "the art of self-sabotage." When you notice ambivalence (a vaguely sick or confused feeling), imagine an enormous STOP sign. Then take up the Identity Conversation: "Why aren't you entitled? Whose voice from your past do you hear in your head telling you you're not? What would you need to feel fully entitled to speak up?"

**Failure to express yourself keeps you out of the relationship.**

- *Angela* broke off her engagement because her fiancé was "too nice": "something was missing: him." Hide parts of yourself and you end up hiding all of yourself behind a lifeless front.
- *Callie*, a Native American running a tutoring program with white coworkers, shared stories of being teased and of longing to be "normal." Her colleagues admired her for it and shared their own stories. That overturned her own stereotype that "white people don't understand and don't care."
- Authentic relationships are comfortable and nourishing: "My boss knows some of my vulnerabilities and still thinks I'm okay."

**Feel entitled, feel encouraged, but don't feel obligated.** Entitlement is not obligation. Don't beat yourself up when you hold back; learning this is a lifelong process.

## Speak the heart of the matter

**Start with what matters most.** "For me, what this is really about is . . . . What I'm feeling is . . . . What is important to me is . . . ."

*Example:* Charlie is the oldest of four brothers. Gage, sixteen, is dyslexic, struggling in school, and drinking. Charlie's advice ("do the debate team," "don't overdo the drinking thing") left Gage feeling criticized and patronized. Its implicit message was "You're a screw-up who needs my help and is too dumb to ask for it." The heart of the matter for Charlie was this: he admired Gage's effort, felt bad about how he had treated him growing up, and needed to feel like a good brother who loves and is loved. When Charlie said this, he cried, and Gage was riveted. It was a turning point.

"We say the least important things, sometimes over and over again." Ask yourself: "Have I said what is at the heart of the matter for me? Have I shared what is at stake?"

## Say what you mean: don't make them guess

- **Don't rely on subtext.** Jokes, questions, offhand comments and body language try to say something while not saying it: "doing neither and doing both... doing both badly." *Example:* a husband's new golf game takes every Saturday morning, and the couple's Saturday routine is gone.
  - "Honey, there's really a lot to be done around the house this weekend." This is the wrong subject, offered as "truth."
  - "Is golf so important that you need to play it this often?" This is a statement masquerading as a question, and it leaves the real cause unclear.
  - "Honey, you are simply playing too much golf!" This is an opinion couched as fact. Too much golf in relation to what?
  - Direct: "I'd like to spend more time with you, and Saturday morning was one of the few times we had to spend together. As a result, I'm finding your interest in golf irritating."

  "Being disappointed that someone isn't reading our mind is one of our contributions to the problem."
- **Avoid easing in.** Chris Argyris named the habit of softening a message with hints and leading questions. Performance-review examples:
  - "So, how do you think you've done?"
  - "Do you think you've really done as much as you could have?"
  - "I have the same problem, but it probably would have been a little better to.... Wouldn't you agree?"

  Easing in sends three messages: "I have a view," "This is too embarrassing to discuss directly," and "I'm not going to be straight with you." The recipient imagines something worse. Better: "Based on what I know, it seems to me that you might have gotten more done. However, you know more about what happened. In what ways would you see it differently?"

## Don't make your story simplistic: use the "Me-Me" And

Simple stories are incomplete. Your real view of a memo is "incredible creativity, and at the same time is so badly organized that it makes me crazy," but what comes out is "Your memo makes me crazy." The **Me-Me And** connects two aspects of what you think or feel:

- "I do think you are bright and talented, and I think you're not working hard enough."
- "I feel badly for how rough things have been for you, and I'm feeling disappointed in you."
- "I'm upset with myself for not noticing that you were so lonely. And I also was having problems during that time."
- "I feel relieved and happy that I finally went through with the divorce — it was the right decision. And I do miss him sometimes."

If you fear being misunderstood, say so:

- "I have a view on this that I want to share, and I have to say that I'm nervous about doing so because I'm afraid it may sound self-serving. So if you see anything in what I say that doesn't seem legitimate, please say so and let's discuss it."
- "I'm having a strong reaction here that I'd like to share, and I'm worried about feeling embarrassed if I'm not able to be clear or unemotional at first..."

## Telling your story with clarity: three guidelines

"Clarity is the key."

1. **Don't present your conclusions as The Truth.** "We often experience our beliefs, opinions, and judgments as facts." "Facts are facts. Everything else is everything else." "Spanking children is just plain wrong" invites "Who are you to proclaim...?!" Better versions: "I believe spanking children is wrong." "I've read several books that say..." "I was spanked as a child and I feel sad and frightened..." "I'm not sure why I feel this way, but I just feel so strongly..." Watch for words that hide judgments, such as "inappropriate," "should" and "professional." Preface them with "My view is that...." or drop them. (The point is not that there is no truth.)
2. **Share where your conclusions come from**: the information and interpretation beneath them. Suppose your wife wants to send your daughter Carol to private school ("It's an important age and I know we can come up with the money"), and you think "she's doing fine in public school." Share your own private-school experience "with all the vividness and detail": the fear of the first months, never fitting in, the guilt that your parents couldn't buy a car because of the tuition.
3. **Don't exaggerate with "always" and "never": give them room to change.** "Why do you always criticize my clothes?" "You never give one word of appreciation..." These words are rarely accurate, so they start an argument about frequency ("I said several nice things to you last year when you won the interoffice new idea competition"). And they imply change is impossible. Proceed as if they don't know their impact and would want to change: "When you tell me my suit reminds you of wrinkled old curtains, I feel hurt. Criticizing my clothes feels like an attack on my judgment and makes me feel incompetent." Then say what you would like to hear: "I wish I could feel more often like you believed in me... 'I think that color looks good on you.'"

## Help them understand you

- **Translate into their terms.** A wife's anxiety about leaving the kids with a new babysitter is like her husband's fear of flying: "when I try to tell you to relax during take-off it has no impact." Fit how people take things in: some want pictures and charts, some the whole problem first, some the details up front.
- **Ask them to paraphrase back.** "Let me check to see if I'm being clear. Would you mind just playing back what you've heard me say so far?"
- **Ask how they see it differently — and why.** If they seem puzzled or unpersuaded, ask how they see it *differently* instead of repeating yourself more forcefully. Asking for agreement ("Does that make sense?" "Wouldn't you agree?") hides their doubts ("Yes, I suppose so").

"The secret of powerful expression is recognizing that you are the ultimate authority on you. You are an expert on what you think, how you feel, and why you've come to this place... You only get in trouble if you try to assert what you are not the final authority on — who is right, who intended what, what happened. Speak fully the range of your experience and you will be clear. Speak for yourself and you can speak with power."
