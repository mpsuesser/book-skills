# Have Your Feelings (Or They Will Have You)

A four-year-old stands next to a shattered vase holding a bat, and when asked what happened says "Nothing." Feelings "will be heard one way or another, whether in leaks or bursts. And if handled indirectly or without honesty, they contaminate communication."

## Why feelings matter

- **Feelings can be the whole problem.** Max and his daughter Julie were negotiating wedding costs: $2000 for the ballroom, $1500 for the band, $7200 for food. Under the numbers, Max felt both sadness and joy. He would have less of Julie's attention, and the planning was his final chance for her to be just his daughter. He wanted her to ask his advice. "This conversation will not go well unless these feelings are surfaced."
- **We try to frame feelings out of the problem.** Max framed it as "trouble deciding how much we should spend." "Solving problems seems easier than talking about emotions." But "when feelings are at the heart of what's going on, they are the business at hand." Sometimes you should let sleeping dogs lie, but "a lack of skill in discussing feelings may cause you to avoid not only sleeping dogs, but all dogs – even those that won't let *you* sleep." Talking about feelings is a skill you can learn.
- **Unexpressed feelings can leak into the conversation.** Emma's mentor, Kathy, told the Executive Committee that Emma wasn't mature enough for promotion. Emma felt betrayed, hurt, furious, and full of self-doubt ("What if I'm not ready?"). Instead of saying so, she argued about professional etiquette: "You have an obligation to talk to me first." She never said "I feel hurt," "I'm angry," or "I'm terrified that you might be right." Feelings leak through affect, tone, body language, facial expression, long pauses and detachment. They make us sarcastic, aggressive, impatient, unpredictable or defensive. Few people can detect factual lies, but most can tell when emotion is being distorted or withheld: "if clogged, your emotional pipes will leak."
- **Unexpressed feelings can burst into the conversation.** We cry or explode because we express our feelings "too rarely," like a shaken carbonated drink.
- **Unexpressed feelings make it difficult to listen.** "The two hardest (and most important) communication tasks in difficult conversations are expressing feelings and listening." Buried emotions keep pulling the spotlight back to us. "Our listening ability often increases remarkably once we have expressed our own strong feelings."
- **Unexpressed feelings take a toll on our self-esteem and relationships.** We lose self-esteem, and we deprive others of the chance to learn and change. "By keeping your feelings out of the relationship you are keeping an important part of yourself out of the relationship."

"The drawbacks of avoiding feelings are inevitable, the drawbacks of sharing feelings are not." **A way out of the feelings bind** has three parts: sort out what your feelings are; negotiate with your feelings; and share your actual feelings, not attributions or judgments about the other person.

## Finding your feelings: learn where feelings hide

We often don't know how we feel. Feelings disguise themselves. Uncomfortable ones pose as more manageable emotions. A bundle of feelings masquerades as a single emotion. "Most important, feelings transform themselves into judgments, accusations, and attributions."

### Explore your emotional footprint

Everyone has a characteristic **emotional footprint**, "whose shape is determined by which feelings we believe are okay to have and express and which are not." Questions to ask:

- How did your family handle emotions? Which feelings were discussed easily, and which were pretended away?
- What was your role in your family's emotional life?
- Which emotions are easy for you now, and with whom? Which are harder?

The footprint differs by relationship: mother, best friend, boss, the stranger in the next seat on a plane. It isn't only about negative feelings. Some people find disappointment easy to express and affection, pride or gratitude hard.

- **Accept that feelings are normal and natural.** Rick, a retired judge, said: "In my family we were taught not to talk about our problems, or the feelings that accompany them." Feelings "just are," like arms and legs. The trouble comes only from hitting and kicking.
- **Recognize that good people can have bad feelings.** Some believe good people don't get angry at loved ones, don't cry, don't fail, and are never a burden. But "everyone feels anger, everyone experiences the urge to cry, everyone fails, and everyone needs other people." People feel rage at a brother's funeral, or weepy at landing a dream job.
- Denial can serve a psychological function, especially after trauma: "Don't knock down a wall until you know why it was put up." Unacknowledged feelings still affect communication, so work toward understanding them, perhaps with a therapist or trusted friend.
- **Learn that your feelings are as important as theirs.** Your father moves in, with his demands and crankiness, and your brother isn't doing his share: "It's hard, but it's not that hard... I don't want to rock the boat." Your girlfriend moves dinner to Saturday, so you give away your baseball ticket. The implicit rule is to put others' happiness before your own. But "their anger is no better or worse than yours," and "when you are more concerned about others' feelings than your own, you teach others to ignore your feelings too." Staying silent to protect the relationship lets resentment grow until it erodes the relationship anyway.

### Find the bundle of feelings behind the simple labels

Brad's mother kept prodding him about his job search. "All I'm feeling is angry," he said. A friend pushed him to look further, and Brad found more. About the search: hopeless, confused, afraid. About his mother: annoyed, but also feeling her love and caring. About himself: shame at letting her down and wasting his college education, and pride that he was holding out for the right fit and supporting himself with odd jobs without ever asking for a penny. One strong feeling can trump all the others and blind you to them.

**A landscape of sometimes hard-to-find feelings:**

| Family | Some of its members |
|---|---|
| Love | affectionate, caring, close, proud, passionate |
| Anger | frustrated, exasperated, enraged, indignant |
| Hurt | let down, betrayed, disappointed, needy |
| Shame | embarrassed, guilty, regretful, humiliated, self-loathing |
| Fear | anxious, terrified, worried, obsessed, suspicious |
| Self-doubt | inadequate, unworthy, inept, unmotivated |
| Joy | happy, enthusiastic, full, elated, content |
| Sadness | bereft, wistful, joyless, depressed |
| Jealousy | envious, selfish, covetous, anguished, yearning |
| Gratitude | appreciative, thankful, relieved, admiring |
| Loneliness | desolate, abandoned, empty, longing |

**Don't let hidden feelings block other emotions.** Jamila couldn't express love to her husband. She blamed herself and denied her anger: "If anyone has the right to be angry, it's my husband, for having to put up with me all the time." A coaching question unlocked it: "What would you say to your husband if you could let your guard down completely, if you could vent at him... with absolutely no consequences attached?" Out came: "I'm sick of you playing the victim all the time... Do you ever stop to think of the impact your constant sniping has on me?!" "It doesn't matter if it's fair or reasonable or rational. What matters is that it is there." Afterward, Jamila said: "If I could just share some of that, it would be easy to balance it with the love I feel."

### Find the feelings lurking under attributions, judgments, and accusations

"Peanuts aren't nuts. Whales aren't fish. Tomatoes aren't vegetables. And attributions, judgments, and accusations aren't feelings." Emily had helped her friend Roz through a divorce and got no thanks. She "told Roz exactly how I felt... that at times she can be self-absorbed and thoughtless," and Roz attacked back ("oversensitive"). Those were judgments. Emily's real feelings were hurt, confusion about the friendship, anger, and embarrassment ("How stupid can I be?").

**We translate our feelings into:**

- **Judgments**: "If you were a good friend you would have been there for me."
- **Attributions**: "Why were you trying to hurt me?"
- **Characterizations**: "You're just so inconsiderate."
- **Problem-solving**: "The answer is for you to call me more often."

Judgments *feel* like feelings as we say them. The receiver can tell we feel something but not what, so they focus on being judged. There is a "vast difference between 'You are thoughtless and self-absorbed' and 'I feel hurt, confused, and embarrassed.'"

**Use the urge to blame as a clue to find important feelings.** Talking about contribution when you want to blame feels like "fat-free yogurt when they're craving real ice cream." "The urge to blame arises when the contribution system is explored in a feelings vacuum." If you feel the need to say "Admit it! This was your fault!", you are sitting on unexpressed emotion. Say instead: "Here's what I've contributed, here's what I think you've contributed, and, more important, I ended up feeling abandoned." The urge recedes.

## Don't treat feelings as gospel: negotiate with them

There are **two rules for expressing feelings**. Rule #2: try to get everything you're feeling into the conversation. Rule #1: before you say what you feel, *negotiate* with your feelings. Feelings rest on perceptions, and perceptions are negotiable. A scuba diver spots a shark and feels terror; once her training lets her identify it as a Reef Shark, which doesn't prey on anything as large as her, she feels excited and curious. "It isn't the shark that's changed; it's the story you tell yourself... our feelings follow our thoughts." The route to changing your feelings is changing your thinking:

1. **Examine your own story.** What story am I telling that gives rise to how I feel? What is it missing? What might their story be?
2. **Explore your assumptions about their intentions.** Is the feeling based on an untested assumption? Might they have acted unintentionally, or with mixed intentions? And what about my intentions: what motivated me, and how did my actions affect them?
3. **Consider the contribution system.** Can I see my own contribution? Can I describe theirs without blaming? Is there a reinforcing pattern?

You don't need definitive answers. "Walk around the sculpture of [your] feelings and observe it from different angles." The anger loses its edge and the hurt becomes less deep. Jamila, after negotiating: "I know I've contributed to the problems we're having... the anger and frustration I've been feeling in reaction to your contributions has made me focus more on our problems than on our strengths. But when I step back... I love you very much, and I'd like for things to get better."

## Don't vent: describe feelings carefully

"We confuse being emotional with expressing emotions clearly... You can express emotion well without being emotional, and you can be extremely emotional without expressing much of anything at all."

1. **Frame feelings back into the problem.** It is always possible to define a problem without its feelings, but then you aren't truly solving it. "Your feelings need not be rational to be expressed." You can preface them by saying you're uncomfortable, or unsure they make sense. Decide what to do about them later.
2. **Express the full spectrum of your feelings.** Brad, instead of "Mom, you're driving me crazy!": "When you ask me how the job search is going, I feel a couple of things. One thing I feel is angry... because I've asked you not to bring it up... But at the same time, part of me is appreciative, and reassured..." And instead of "Stop bugging me": "It's hard for me to talk with you about this. Whenever I think about it, I end up feeling ashamed, like maybe I'm wasting my potential or letting you down."
3. **Don't evaluate — just share.** "I felt hurt" answered with "You're overreacting" short-circuits the conversation. Create an **evaluation-free zone**: share pure feelings (no judgments, attributions or blame), save problem-solving for later, and don't monopolize.
   - **Express your feelings without judging, attributing, or blaming.** Emily's redo: "I just explained that I felt hurt. And confused about the state of our friendship." Roz became contrite. "You are so damn undependable!" invites "I am not undependable!", whereas "I feel frustrated. You didn't send the letter out" does not. Untangle mixed messages too: turn "You didn't call me like you said. It's your fault that I felt hurt" into "When you didn't call, I felt hurt," and explore joint contribution later.
   - **Don't monopolize: both sides can have strong feelings at the same time.** Get both parties' emotions "into the conversational cart before you head for the checkout." You may be angry at your boss while your boss is annoyed about your memo.
   - **An easy reminder: say "I feel . . . ."** It keeps the focus on feelings and on your own perspective, and it avoids the translation trap. Instead of "Why do you insist on undermining me in front of the kids?!" say "When you disagree with me about child-rearing in front of the kids, I feel betrayed, and also worried about the message it sends to them." Your spouse can't argue with how you feel.

## The importance of acknowledgment

You can't leap from describing feelings to problem-solving: "Acknowledgment is a step that simply cannot be skipped." Acknowledging means "letting the other person know that what they have said has made an impression on you, that their feelings matter to you, and that you are working to understand them."

- Ways to acknowledge: "Wow, ... I never knew you felt that way." "I kind of assumed you were feeling that, and I'm glad you felt comfortable enough with me to share it." "It sounds like this is really important to you." "Before I give you a sense of what's going on with me, tell me more about your feeling that I talk down to you."
- **The temptation to "fix" the feeling comes too soon.** "If you're feeling lonely, I guess I'll try to spend more time with you" and "You're right. What can I say?" both jump past acknowledgment.
- **When they skip acknowledging yours, redirect them**: "I'm not saying you intended to hurt me. I don't know whether you did or not. What's important to me is that you understand how I felt when you criticized my work in front of the department." It is your responsibility to make sure they appreciate how important this is: "If they aren't getting how important something is to you and you don't flag it, then you are letting yourself down."
- **Acknowledgment can matter more than agreement.** In community dialogues on racial tension between police and minority residents, a Black teenager in tears said: "I don't want to change their minds. I just wanted to share my story... they care enough about me to just listen."

**Sometimes feelings are all that matter.** Once Max shared his sense of loss and his pride, the money was easy. Other times they aren't all that matters: there is still a job to do or kids to raise, and you need to talk about both the feelings and the problem.

## Feelings in the workplace

- **Organizations already have feelings.** Every organization has accepted feelings (stress, frustration, pride, loyalty, enthusiasm) and hidden ones (disappointment, self-doubt, jealousy, hurt). The official line is "check your feelings at the door." But the workplace is "already suffused with feelings," and determination, pride and commitment are why people persist. "It is not negative feelings in themselves that distract us from productivity, but the failure to acknowledge them, and to deal with them directly, efficiently, and honestly."
- **Two problems at once.** By the time an issue becomes a difficult conversation, there are usually two problems. One is the substantive business issue. The other is "how people have felt treated in the conversations so far (ignored, publicly embarrassed, left out of the loop, or unfairly blamed)." You need to address both. Sometimes a single line does it: "I'm frustrated. It seems like we're going in circles," or "I was disappointed not to be chosen for that assignment."
- **Is it risky?** It depends on what you share, when, and how.
  - *Language*: in cultures that frown on feelings, use acceptable language and tie the discussion to a business goal. Instead of "I feel hurt and taken for granted," say: "I'd like to find a way to get this done earlier each quarter. I know I often leave these meetings frustrated, and I imagine you also periodically feel frustrated. Can we talk about why that is and how we might design a better process?"
  - *When*: not right before a deadline, and not in a hallway; a quarterly coaching session might work.
  - *How*: "Talking about emotions by *being* emotional – crying, screaming, pouting, eye-rolling, or foot stomping – will often make you appear weak, out-of-control, or unprofessional." Context matters, though: tears can be authentic, as when a football player announces his retirement.
  - *What*: lower-risk feelings include appreciation, enthusiasm, pride, confusion about your role, anxiety about how a decision will affect teammates, and curiosity. These are heard as invitations to solve problems together. A corporate culture "is actually made up of many individual relationships," so changing yours changes it.
- **Deciding on the merits.** In 1986, a NASA leader said he was "appalled" at Morton Thiokol's reading of the *Challenger* data, and Thiokol reversed its no-launch recommendation. Yet "the description of one person's emotional state has no relationship to whether a launch is safe." Not talking about feelings also produces poor decisions. "Acknowledging that feelings are in play – making them discussable – positions them appropriately as another factor to be weighed on their merits." A feeling may not itself be a reason for a decision, but "it may be a clue pointing to a legitimate business reason." Thiokol could have answered: "As to the timing of our informing you, it *is* late in the game... As to our interpretation of the data, tell us more about what seems problematic to you or why you read the data differently." And NASA: "I hear your concern, and I am aware that you are closest to the data, so it troubles me that the data seems so much less clear to me. I'm wondering what you see that we don't." "The emotion is part of the discussion, but the mere presence of strong emotion shouldn't drive the decision."
- **Separate acknowledgment from decision making.** An employee who isn't promoted will feel disappointed and hurt no matter what. If they can't talk about it, they will also feel betrayed and ignored, and they'll start making attributions about you. If you draw out their feelings, they can take in your reasoning. "The key to managing feelings well is to separate acknowledgment from decision making and to give up the responsibility of fixing them." Be explicit: "I'd like to talk about how we're each feeling about this situation, and what we each think, so I have as much information as possible in making this decision. Of course, in the end, I'll have to decide based on my assessment of your performance, the criteria for the position, and my sense of what's best for the company."
