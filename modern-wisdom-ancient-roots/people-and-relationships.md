# People and relationships

Practices for when other people inflame you, disappoint you, judge you, or slip away from you. The common thread is that what you see in others, and what you need from them, is your own mental model at work.

## Feed the dog, not the wolf

In Rao's version of a Native American parable, a medicine man tells a boy that a dog ("intelligent, loving, kind, and trustworthy") and a wolf ("malevolent, vicious, cruel, and ready to kill") are fighting inside him. Which one wins? "Whichever one you feed." Inside each of us are "let's help one another and make the world a better place" impulses and "let me grab whatever I can for myself, and the Devil take the hindmost" impulses, warring endlessly. Your job is to feed the dog in yourself, and to pick out and feed the dog in everyone you meet. "Magic happens in your life when the dog in you becomes friends with the dog in another person."

We feed the wolf without noticing. A friend rails against feckless politicians and crooked businesspeople, you join in about corrupt legislators, and you part warm with camaraderie, having fed the wolf in both of you. Feeding the dog would sound like: "I entirely agree with you. But is there any sign that things are moving toward greater amity and cooperation? And is there anything we can do to foster movement in that direction?" In every interaction, with friends, spouse, children, colleagues or clients, ask: **"Am I feeding the dog, or am I feeding the wolf?"** Are you leaving them energized with possibility, or more desolate? Indulging an easy, comfortable habit, such as junk food instead of a healthy salad, also feeds the wolf in you.

## The person you dislike

Lincoln supposedly said: "I don't like that person very much. I must get to know him better." Adopt the model **"'I don't like him' means that I don't know him well enough."** Practice it conscientiously for a month:

1. Pick someone you dislike, whether for their politics, values you abhor, slovenliness, rudeness or selfishness.
2. *Look* for traits you find likable.
3. Find out what makes them tick as a human being.
4. Note the ways you are similar, such as the same Netflix series, the same tennis player, or a love of advanced Sudoku.
5. Notice how you now feel about them, and about yourself.

For the person giving you the most trouble (a spouse, a boss), list the specific things about them that bother you. For each one, ask whether it is easier to let go of an attribute you cannot change or to keep struggling against it, and picture how much easier life would be if you accepted them. For three things you habitually complain about (a job, a spouse, a child's lack of respect), give each its own page, list five positive attributes, focus only on those, and see whether your attitude shifts.

## Is he really such a jerk?

Julie, a dedicated teacher still afraid after a student hit her the week before, woke a dozing Jason confrontationally. He snarled "Leave me alone," and she pressed for indefinite suspension. An older teacher asked for twenty-four hours to look into it. Jason's father was in jail and his mother was battling addiction and an abusive boyfriend. Jason had spent the night nursing his feverish sister and his mother, who was comatose after a binge. "We should give him a medal... but we were about to throw him out and further wreck his life." After the three of them met, Julie became his advocate until he graduated. "Each of them viewed the other through the prism of their own experience, and neither recognized that this is what they were doing."

When behavior enrages you, think of **plausible scenarios in which it could actually be reasonable**. "It does not matter in the slightest" whether the scenario is true. The act of imagining it opens you up, shows you that your take is not the only one, and moves you to a different emotional domain. The interaction improves markedly. It doesn't work every time, but it works often enough to make your life significantly better. The banker in `SKILL.md`, who heard his wife's tirade as loneliness, is the same move.

## The dirt is on your face

Dr. Kamla Chowdhry questioned Rao gently until he saw that the trait he railed against in his paper was his own. "We never see the world as it is—we see the world as we are." The persistent bore complains that everyone is long-winded, and the self-centered lout laments others' selfishness. Patrick, a tailor turned multimillionaire, had a private audience with the pope, and all he could report was "Size 38 long... but he's tough to outfit because his trousers are size 42." A surgeon in Rao's program summed it up: **"If you can spot it, you got it!"** Ask what you notice in people: her shabby clothes, or the genuine affection in her greeting? His obscure college, or his deep curiosity? A cat burglar, primed for a rival in the house, fired at a shadow and shattered a mirror. "We fight with mirrors as we try to defend ourselves. Our enemy is within us": envy, hatred, sloth, and the need to be more, get more, and be seen as more. "Why do we clean the mirror when the dirt is on our face?" When you criticize someone, ask whether they are the problem, or you are.

## Let go of your grudges

At London Business School, with students from twenty or more countries, Charlotte objected to being grouped with Shelley as "thoughtless and rude." It emerged that in second or third grade Shelley had been among the girls who laughed at Charlotte's pinafore, and they had not spoken since. Charlotte saw she was being guided by an ancient memory. We are all Charlotte. Each grudge, however small, is a silken thread, and many of them bind you down. To let them go:

1. **Be aware of them.**
2. When you meet someone with whom you have a troubled history, see that the past is coloring your expectation, and that "it is not what happened in the past but what you *think* happened in the past" that does it.
3. **Play a game.** Imagine this is the first time you are meeting the person. Be open, and attend only to this fresh moment. "Put your suitcases down! The train can carry you *and* your baggage."
4. Consciously decide to feed the dog.
5. Be prepared to be pleasantly surprised.

On paper, draw a suitcase that nearly fills the page. Inside it, write the people and events from your past that still drag you down, along with the preconceptions and expectations that bring you grief, and draw lines between the connected ones. Are these people still active in your life? Has anything improved? Where is lightening the suitcase your responsibility, not theirs? Then crumple the drawing and begin fresh.

## If I could just get them to change

Examples: the attorney whose son is experimenting with drugs, the entrepreneur with a brilliant employee who is brusque with clients, the executive whose wife wants dinner out every night with people he can't stand, the proper woman whose well-trained dog bites visitors' trousers. Everyone is trying to fix someone: children, spouses, parents, colleagues, bosses, vendors, even pets. You hold your well-being hostage to their behavior, and when they misbehave "you punish yourself by becoming miserable." "They are who they are." You can try to change them, but failure is likely, so accept it gracefully and work on yourself: "the hunchback is oblivious to his own crook as he notes those of others." Do keep trying to bring about positive change, but when you fail, as you often will, keep your equanimity. People differ for a reason, so accept and celebrate the difference.

## The hungry ghost

Joe, a CPM student going through a divorce, played a game with his eight-year-old son Adam. Together they listed all the ways moving between two countries could be fun, and Adam's eyes shone. When Joe asked what else he could do, Adam deflated: "I wish you would talk to me like you just did... You are never there most of the time." Joe did take him to movies and came home for dinner, but he was always preoccupied. With your partner, children and loved ones, ask: **"Are you really there? Or are you a hungry ghost going through the motions, obsessed with your mental chatter and driven by your needs?"** Don't beat yourself up. Bring yourself to the present and to the person you are with, and pour your full, undivided attention into the interaction, especially with the important people in your life. Practice mindfulness to weed out the chatter.

**Exercise.** In conversations, notice whether you track tone and emotional undertones or are busy composing your next comment. Try a whole conversation without the words "I," "me," or "my," and see how hard it is.

## What will they think of me?

You think everyone is watching the movie of your life, but you are the only one watching it closely. Carmen declined a hilarious company skit in which she would look scruffy, take a fall, and crush the department bully with a riposte, because her bosses had only ever seen her polished. People turn down unusual assignments, lateral moves, speaking invitations and improv groups, and keep quiet when an expert is in the room, all from "What will they think of me?" Rao knows exactly what they think of you: "The truth is they don't think of you at all. And they won't suddenly start doing so either." They are watching the movie of *their* life, where you have a bit part at most.

**Test it.** Recall a gaffe from years ago that still makes your ears burn. Today, contact the people who were there and gently ask what they remember. Most won't recall it, and those who do will think it trivial. Hundreds of students have confirmed this. The fear keeps you in a job you hate when you could start a company, and stops you approaching the person you find attractive. "What will they think of me?" is a prison of your own making, and it does not really exist, because *they* are not thinking of you. Break out today.

## Needing recognition

In *My Fair Lady*, Eliza sobs because Higgins did not congratulate her after her triumph at the ball. Higgins says he treats a duchess as a flower girl. Eliza wanted to feel important, and so does everyone. The trouble starts when we need that recognition from a specific person or group: "Every time our emotional well-being is affected by whether someone else acknowledges us, we construct a prison around ourselves and hand that person the key." Do you want your happiness controlled by "the spigot of other people's attention and acclaim"? "Your job as a flower is to bloom." A wild rose is not a whit less than one in a show garden. You free yourself by seeing, really seeing, how the quest robs you of your birthright: serenity and happiness. Recall your greatest fulfillment that had nothing to do with applause or external validation, and find ways to bring that feeling back.

## Invisible threads

A friend's in-laws gave her an expensive outfit, then expected her to wear it at every visit, remembered every gift, and asked their son whether she was upset with them. "They just put another rope around me." We all do this. We remember the neighbor who never reciprocated the coffee, keep track of who picked up the tab, keep reminding a friend that we have "forgiven" her, lay guilt trips on children who don't call, and expect profuse thanks for gifts. These are **invisible threads** attached to things we think we have given away, and they weave a net that traps giver and receiver alike. "Don't hang on to what you have given away." Whether the gift is a thing, a service, or something intangible like forgiveness, let it go. "Even the expectation of a thank-you can become a burden." What nourishes you is being able to serve, so help because you are able to.

## The people we ignore

Rao barked at a telemarketer. His daughter: "You had the opportunity to make the world a little nicer for a fellow human being, and you totally blew it... Isn't that what you teach in your course?" Think of how many people we cavalierly ignore: janitors, waitresses, cab drivers, bellhops, lawn care workers. Each has their own cares and dreams, and each provides a service we need. His wife sets out ice-cold water for the lawn workers and the mailman on hot days. You need not buy from the next caller, but acknowledge for an instant that fate has intertwined you for some unfathomable purpose, and seek to make their life a little better through genuine interaction.

## Don't make the mistake I made

A friend of three decades used to drive more than two hours each way to lunch with Rao and supported him through a turbulent time. Rao canceled, rescheduled, let an email get lost, and cut short a call from Florida. More than two years passed, and the friend died of pancreatic cancer before Rao could say goodbye. "The 'urgent' rides roughshod over us and sidelines the 'important.'" There is someone you love and mean to call: "Sometimes tomorrow never comes." Reach out *today*. Send the email or make the call.

## Giving that is personal

After the magnitude 7.8 Nepal earthquake of April 25, 2015 (more than ten thousand dead, nineteen climbers killed on Everest), donations flooded in and attention soon faded, just as help was most needed. Rao's suggestion is to make it *personal*. Find a specific affected person or family and help them directly. His family gave to a Nepali waiter at their local restaurant whose home had collapsed and crushed his mother. Seek out people in low-wage jobs, or ask aid workers on the ground for names. Give time, sweat and care, not just fungible money: "There is a difference between donating to a hospital and personally caring for a quadriplegic and wiping the drool from his lips." Much helping is done for acknowledgment, fame and ego-gratification, like the person who holds a door and bristles when no one says thanks. American philanthropy is "an intricate charade" of named buildings, even though much good comes of it.

**Exercise.** Recall times you benefited someone with no thought of thanks: moving a stone off the road, returning a stray shopping cart, giving a street musician a dollar while looking him in the eye and thanking him. "These are the bricks on which a fulfilled life is built." You do it for you. Spend one hour researching a local organization that could use your time, then go and offer your help.
