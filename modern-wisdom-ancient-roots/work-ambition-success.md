# Work, ambition and success

Practices for people who feel stuck in their work, get passed over, chase status, or wonder whether to keep going. Rao is not against achievement. He says the joy and the success both come from changing the mental models and the intent behind the striving.

## Two ways out of a job you dislike

Ted quit as a managing director and senior partner at a consulting firm to start a small, selective consultancy. He earned less, the papers stopped quoting him, and the invitations stopped. His mother-in-law, at his funeral: "He certainly wasted this one." His wife: they had a wonderful decade, he was cheerful, he saw the children, and "If I could go back and do it all over again, I'd have made him quit earlier." Paul Graham's warning: "Prestige is a distorting force that makes you want to like something that you really do not."

There are two ways out. One is to walk away, as Ted did. The other is to "genuinely like, not 'pretend like,' where you are and what you do." Ask: do you define success by markers others celebrate (position, power, money) or by internal markers (a deep feeling of well-being, joy, a sense of purpose)?

## Passion is in you

The usual complaint: people would follow their "passion", but "they need the income". "Passion does not exist in the job. It exists in *you.* And if you cannot ignite it within yourself right where you are now, you will not find it outside yourself." Boring, meaningless, too hard, too easy, difficult colleagues, time demands, bland cafeteria food: "Each of these is a thought in our head." Thoughts are clouds, and they have no power unless we give it to them.

**Experiment.** The next time you resent something at work, measure how much of the resistance is chatter: "I don't want to do this. Why do I have to do this? It's a waste of time. It's beneath me." See how strong your preferences are and how they drag you into dark places. Treat the situation as a **training ground** for untangling yourself from your desires. Pick one boring or dispiriting part of your job and change your perspective on it until you are enthusiastic. One student hated organizing meetings and taking minutes until she saw that she could shape the agenda and get her priorities airtime.

A Harvard MBA could only get a job covering entertainment, then an unprestigious "industry", as a brokerage analyst. He made peace with it, learned the field, and liked the people. When Steve Ross, Lew Wasserman and Charles Bluhdorn built their conglomerates, he was the premier analyst covering them, the one the moguls would always call back and the one the press loved. The job he hated became the one he loved, and then his calling. He later became president of the world's largest entertainment conglomerate. "Letting go of resistance is a part of that luck."

**Work with love.** Rao resented watering his wife's potted plants, doused them from above, and killed many. She watered them "with love, with a gentle trickle to the roots." Kahlil Gibran: "Work is love made visible." We race after work we love and forget to put love into what we are doing. Seek betterment, but pour love into your present work meanwhile. "Putting love into what you do is better than searching for what you love." Your perfect station may be right where you are. As a daily practice, imbue every action with love deliberately: tip the waiter and wish her family well, and send a blessing rather than a curse to the driver who cuts you off. You will forget, so go back to it when you remember.

**Break rocks or build a cathedral.** Three friends became kindergarten teachers. Mary saw herself as temporary custodian of rich, uncaring parents' children and resented wiping noses. The children sensed it and learned poorly, and no one came to her funeral. Joan taught the three Rs and kept order, and a few nearby former students came to say goodbye. Eleanor began like Mary, then saw what her resentment was doing to her: snapping at her daughter, withdrawn from her husband, always tired. She decided to love each child: "They were the clay, and she was the potter—and each of her pots would be a work of art." She told them stories of heroes and urged them to dream great dreams and lay foundations under their castles in the air. The board kept extending her past retirement, and the prime minister, a former pupil, came to her funeral. Ask before work tomorrow: Mary, Joan, or Eleanor? "It matters what you do. It matters much more who you are being as you do it!"

## Where are you living? Strive from gratitude

John, a Harvard MBA, was passed over in favor of a younger outsider, regretted turning down a venture firm that now has unicorns, fretted that his son's 3.3 GPA wouldn't get him into the Ivies, decided his teacher wife wasn't "right" for his new circles next to his boss's dealmaking spouse, and found the house he would have been thrilled with ten years ago drab. He built that world with his chatter and believes he had bad breaks. You are privileged (lunch tomorrow, a bed, a roof) yet feel stressed about where you *could* and *should* have been. Bettering yourself is "a capital idea"; comparing yourself to the lives you imagine others lead and feeling miserable about it is "a terrible idea." Name the unexamined model (John's: his wife must be accepted by his aspirational group for him to be proud of her) and ask whether he truly wants what it demands.

Keep the dreams, but pursue them **from a place of gratitude**, not from deep dissatisfaction. Striving from dissatisfaction is a choice: "Probably it was an unconscious choice, but it was still a choice and still *his* choice." Observe the chatter and turn it "much as you would a horse you are riding. When envy raises its green head, direct your thoughts to how much you have to be grateful for." Then, from that place, try to do better. "Where are *you* living?"

## Making a quantum leap

Susan was interim CMO, the internal candidate, with stellar reviews and degrees from Harvard and Columbia. The permanent job went to David, four years younger, and she considered quitting. Someone younger and seemingly less qualified will zoom past you too. Rao answers on two levels:

1. "It is extraordinarily foolish—and toxic—to measure yourself against someone else and let this measurement determine your well-being."
2. You can "jump ahead to the next queue and then move to the head of the one after that." Working harder won't get you there, and may set you back. Working smarter won't, because everyone has the same information. Managing your time better won't either: "you will be dismayed at how much time you spend in an effort to manage that time." The only way is "*thinking differently*—by thinking radically differently," which means changing your mental models.

Susan cried for a week, then made David her ally and advocate. They had the same school, professors and friends. Eighteen months later they quit together to start a company. "Fate does not give you a lemon... Fate gives you the opportunity to make lemonade, and if you are smart, you will streamline the process and create a global empire of lemonade stands." Ray Kroc did this with burgers.

**Exercise: bad thing to good thing.** Pick whatever is soaking up your emotional energy that you see as a "bad thing" or "very bad thing".

1. Is there any scenario in which it turns out to be a "good thing" in a few months or years?
2. Is there anything you can proactively do to *make* it a good thing?

Asking these and acting on them "moves you from the realm of despair to the realm of possibility" and makes you so resilient that others wonder how you do it. First recall past events you classified as disasters that now look not so bad, or even good. A student was laid off from a tech company famous for never firing anyone. His severance funded a business that doubled his income within two years and reached five times his old salary in five.

**Outgrow your need.** As a boy in Delhi, Rao desperately wanted a picture book called *Just Like Daddy*, and he cried his frugal mother out of buying a purse so he could have it. Decades later he saw the book at a flea market: "I did not want it. I did not *not* want it." He had outgrown it. You have outgrown countless things you once craved, whether or not you got them. For whatever you want badly now (the credit line, a daughter's grades, the "Best Place to Work" list, in-laws moving to Australia), ask: **"Would you rather get what you want? Or would you rather outgrow your need for it"**? Imagine you have vaulted so far higher that you no longer need the experience you seek. What would your life be like?

## Serve, don't seek

Five hundred executives roared with laughter at Rao's mispronunciation. A veteran speaker told him: "the audience takes its cue from you... If you get rattled, they get rattled." Those executives were anxious about a restructuring, and "You made them laugh uproariously and forget their concerns for a while. Be grateful you could do that." Rao stopped caring what audiences think of him and focused entirely on helping them, and his fear of public speaking disappeared. Audiences now laugh with him.

**Before a speech or a serious conversation**, be clear what you want to accomplish, but do not put your emotional energy into "What will they think of me?" or "What do I want to get out of this?" Put it into **"What can I do to help this person—or this audience—achieve their goals?"** Your tension drops and you succeed more often. This is not "a sophisticated method of self-serving". Thinking "If I help him get what he wants, he will give me what I want" turns everything into a transaction and impoverishes you. Serve only because you can serve. What you need comes to you organically.

## Intent: same action, different space

A newly freelance consultant's first big prospect accepted her proposal, but at 10 percent below her quote and with extra work thrown in. Rao advised her to decline: she would have loved to work with him, but could not accept his terms. The client came back, paid the full quote, and paid for the extra work. She called it scarcity creating perceived value. People want what they cannot have. Lazy marketers say "supplies are limited", and smarter ones say "only two seats left." Rao looks deeper, at the space the action comes from:

- **Manipulation**: "I think he is just negotiating... if I hold firm... the scarcity effect will kick in, and I will get my price." This is legal, ethical and smart.
- **Service**: "I want to serve this client, but I cannot give my best if he dictates the terms... I will resent this, and it will affect the quality of my work... If I lose the project, then so be it. I only want to work with clients who understand what I stand for." This is also legal and ethical, and it leaves you in a far better emotional domain.

The two overlap, and most people act from a mixture, which is fine. Recognize your intent and get as close to the second as you can. "Don't be a sophisticated manipulator of people—be an authentic helper." Don't be so enamored of the outcome that you compromise too fast. Take your time and chart a win-win in your head. You decide what win-win is, you face the consequences if the other side sees it differently, and you accept whatever happens with equanimity. The action is the same, the outcome is usually better, and you feel better about yourself. Fred Smith was told no one would pay many dollars for what the Postal Service did for a quarter, and he built Federal Express anyway. Thinking differently often leads to acting differently, but intent decides whether you grow.

## Ambition

"Would you drink corrosive acid? Then why harbor ambition?" Ambition is "an earnest desire for some type of achievement or distinction, as power, honor, fame, or wealth and the willingness to strive for its attainment." The rub: "When you are ambitious, you live for a future that may or may not come," and when it comes it never brings the hoped-for joy. By definition ambition makes you unhappy with your lot. Abd-ar-Rahman III, caliph of Cordoba, reigned more than fifty years with every earthly blessing, counted his days of pure and genuine happiness, and found fourteen. Gabriel rose from poverty to great wealth by packaging accident-victims' lawsuits into securities. He missed his children's performances and dinners, his wife left quietly, and after two strokes he sat paralyzed in his mansion, alone on his birthday, with a voice telling him he had wasted his life.

When people object "Why should I get up in the morning if I don't have ambition?" and ask how anything will improve without ambitious people, the answer is: do create and maintain a **grand vision** and work toward it, because that is your path and it feels right. It stops being ambition once you see that the reward is the effort and the growth it produces, not the achievement. You cannot drop ambition by an act of will. Persist, and one day it is no longer "the cat-o'-nine-tails driving you on."

## You are not special

"The big lie": from childhood you are told you are special, unique, significant, that you *matter*. It helps when you are small and becomes toxic later, when you chase the feeling by trying to be significant in some small arena, such as the club tennis championship. "You are *not* special." Millions like you came before and more will follow, and even Caesar, Genghis Khan and Alexander shriveled and were forgotten. You matter "in the same way that an individual bee in a hive matters, or a grain of sand on a beach." The world would get along fine without you, and relaxing into that is liberating. There is no façade to maintain, and you can flower where you are. Your accomplishments and possessions "are all in time and space. And whatever is in time and space will be wrested from you and scattered to the winds." The British Empire and the Mughal Empire are both gone. Keep your goals, but pursue them as a rose blooms. Rao calls putting this into play "a lifetime's work."

**Paths of glory.** Shah Jahan, builder of the Taj Mahal, ruled more than 150 million people and nearly a quarter of the world's economy. He killed his brothers for the throne, was imprisoned by his own son Aurangzeb, and his darbar throne now sits in a sorry corner where tourists take selfies. Hamlet: "Imperious Caesar, dead and turn'd to clay, / Might stop a hole to keep the wind away." Keep striving, but be "exceedingly careful about *what* you are striving for and *why*." The true value lies in "the changes these efforts produce in you."

## Something better than hope

Hope assumes that tomorrow will be better, so we ignore and even denigrate today. "But today is all you have. It is all you will ever have." Chatter says the present is terrible and demands more: money, a bigger house, a better spouse, more accomplished children. "Hope is the handmaiden of this urge." You *should* strive to grow, materially and spiritually, and it is "your *obligation*", but do it with joy and gratitude, knowing that planning for tomorrow happens today. "You work and strive because the working and striving itself are providing you with the nourishment you need for your growth." Tomorrow turns out as it turns out, and you are fine with that.

## Effortless striving

Kodo Sawaki Roshi described Manchurian carts pulled by giant dogs lunging at meat dangled before their noses: "Even so do you live your lives." Spending everything on "ordinary-person hopes" wastes a golden opportunity. He did not mean stop striving. He meant that the striving should be **transmuted** to serve a higher purpose. A mother nursing a child's cold acts on several levels at once. She helps him blow his nose and wants a better night's sleep herself. She shapes a future citizen who will carry the family name. She fulfills her own path in life. "When you act with awareness, you are mindfully conscious of all the different levels from which you are acting." You are still the dog pulling for the meat, but the sense of effort drops away. You see that you allowed the good things to appear. You allowed the misfortunes too, and you can withdraw your permission.

**The surrender experiment.** Michael Singer, practicing surrender, found himself registered for a doctoral public finance exam he had not studied for. He read three random sections the day before and the morning of the exam, fully expecting to fail and at peace with it. Three of the six questions (he had to answer three) were on those sections, and he got an A and a commendation from the dean. Ask, slowly and without forcing an answer: "Do you really have to impose your will... on the universe to make things happen the way you want them to? Or can you learn to set aside your oh-so-strong preferences and let a greater wisdom guide you effortlessly through life?"

**Exercise: two kinds of striving.** Make two lists. On one, put where you merely strive toward a goal or rigid expectation, focused only on the outcome. On the other, put where you strive for a purpose. Rebuild your days around the second list. When you miss a goal, do you pause to rethink the purpose, or just work harder?

**The hamster wheel.** "Hamster on a wheel" usually stands for a busy, meaningless life. A CPM alumnus objected: "the hamster isn't trying to go somewhere. He is simply exercising and having a fun time." Do the activity as well as you can, give up attachment to any particular outcome, and be the hamster having a rip-roaring time. Tarthang Tulku: "Every kind of work can be a pleasure."

## Keep struggling or throw in the towel?

For the suffocating job, the stifling marriage or the business that is running out of money, proverbs contradict each other and persistence is overpraised. Rao holds two truths together. An "intolerable" situation is mostly so because we defined it that way and reinforced the label with chatter and models, and many find it becomes bearable, even enjoyable, once their thinking changes. Also, "one of the ways the universe signals to us that it is time to make a change is by making us miserable where we are." To decide, use **"Who am I being?"** from `SKILL.md`. Be the calm, grateful person you want to be, ask what that person would do, and accept that staying or going matters less than who you are being.

## The secret of incredible success

Rao's youthful mistake was ignoring his mother's urging to live by the Great Masters, because he wanted "success" now. The great truth he found later is that their teachings "also lead to vast success in the material world." So deliberately following a spiritual path is the way to material gain: "You *can* have your cake and eat it too!" The catch is that if you set foot on the path expecting material success, it disappears. It comes only as a by-product. Use your little time to break free from "the great illusion", and the same steps benefit you within the illusion. "You don't have to make trade-offs. Both joy and success can be your lot in life."
