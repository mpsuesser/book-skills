# Chatter, emotions and adversity

Practices for when mental chatter, a strong emotion or a run of bad events has taken over. Each one rests on the witness stance in `SKILL.md`: see that the thought is a thought, and you stop being dragged by it.

## The inner critic

Pedro runs late to a presentation while his inner voice berates him: *Why do I always screw up... they think I'm the 'token' minority.* We all talk to ourselves like this. We compare ourselves unfavorably, criticize our looks, our performance and our station, and condemn the second slice of pizza as proof that "I'll never amount to anything." "We are mentally slashing our wrists every day." This is our single worst enemy.

You cannot slay it, but you can tie it up, because "the mistake you make is that you *believe* it." Count how often the voice has been wrong. One mother of a hearing-impaired son still pictures plane crashes whenever he fails to call after a trip, despite decades of safe journeys. When the voice heads somewhere dark, pause immediately and recognize "It is *not* an oracle, and it is not necessarily truthful. Most of all, it is *not* you. It is just a voice in your head." Watch it like a movie. Its intensity drops, and soon you stop going to those dark places.

## Mental chatter is fake news

Paul, an executive vice president, was left out of a board meeting and saw "CEO succession" on his rival's agenda. His chatter built a plot in which the rival had bad-mouthed him, so he resolved to resign. He walked into the boardroom to cheers: he was the new CEO, and the rival was about to marry the CEO's daughter and leave. "We are all Paul": the child who has not called to say they got home safely, the talking heads on soaring crime. Rao's own forecast at Data Resources, that computers and AI would bring a thirty-hour work week, shows how confidently a mind spins tales. Treat every message from chatter as unverified, and take it under advisement like a fuel warning light. See the two levels in `SKILL.md`. When circumstances are beyond your control, your emotions are what they are, but you can reduce the suffering fear causes by tuning within and observing your reactions. "Be an observer of fear—and remember, you are connected to everything."

**Exercise: anti-mantras.** List the **anti-mantras** you repeat to yourself, for example that you are not aging gracefully, that your colleague will take your job, or that your son will get hurt out late at night. How much control do you have over each? Sort them into internal and external problems, and make peace with them from the inside. Then review every list you have made and see that "the overwhelming majority of the problems that plague you are the result of your mental chatter running amok."

## Anger and fear: the rosogolla

Steve's boss blamed him for requirements the boss himself had changed, HR refused his expenses, and at home he stepped on his son's new Game Boy and charged upstairs. Those are surface explanations. "Steve got angry because there was anger in him." The same holds for fear, jealousy and insecurity. Recognize this without beating yourself up, which only adds to the anger. Then transmute it with gratitude:

1. Think of something in your life that is a true blessing, such as the peace of spiritual reading, a partner who genuinely cares, or your infant's smile in the crib.
2. Savor it, and let the gratitude well up and pour out of you in waves.
3. If you are a type A person who lives in your head, persist until "thinking" gratitude becomes experienced gratitude.

You cannot be grateful and angry at the same time. When gratitude becomes your default emotional domain, whatever happens draws out care, compassion and reasoned action.

## The *pause* and the disaster day

Peter's day: his three-year-old cried all night with an ear infection, his mother made pointed comments, and he arrived late to the staff meeting just after lecturing others about lateness. A big customer canceled after he had told analysts sales were over projection, ahead of an IPO, and the furnace technician set off the alarm at home. For an emergency, the routine takes under five minutes:

1. Sit upright, both feet on the ground, spine straight.
2. Breathe deeply and slowly. Most of us breathe with the top third of the chest. Breathe until the stomach expands gently, and feel it collapse as you exhale.
3. Observe the breath for two or three minutes: going in, going out.
4. *Observe* the chatter running amok (worry colliding with frustration, rage, anxiety, fear). Don't react from the emotion. *Pause* and keep breathing.
5. Look at the angry comment or the blistering email you were about to send, and ask whether it serves your longer-term interests.
6. From the pile clamoring for attention, pick the one most important item and deal with it calmly. Your calm rubs off on others who are frantic. This is how you develop **presence**: "Not by appearing unruffled, but by actually *being* unruffled."
7. Take the next most important item, calmly, and keep breathing.

No matter how bad the day is, it will pass, and you will tell your grandson about the storms you weathered. The *pause* can be used many times a day. A shorter drill: fast, shallow breathing marks anger, overwhelm, sadness, fear, lust and anxiety. So pause, straighten your spine, take ten very deep, very slow breaths, and then go back to what you were doing.

## When the world turns bleak

On the day Rao describes, icy rain fell, his wife's wrist was in a cast, CNN carried talk of escalation in Ukraine, and the dinner-party talk was of revenge on ISIS. External stimuli capture thoughts and emotions, and we spiral down before we notice. *Pause*, breathe, and notice that what troubles you is a thought, so that you become its observer. Dwelling on how terrible events are "feeds the demonic energy that has been unleashed." Don't turn your back on them, and don't obsess over them either. In your own small world, help someone elevate their consciousness and practice random acts of kindness. Tyrants appear invincible and then fall. Rao showed students old *Fortune* covers of CEOs who once shook industries, and the students recognized almost none of them ("Ozymandias"). "Life is a passing show." Resolve that you alone control how you feel, and that no news anchor will hijack your well-being.

**Draw the bullseye.** Arjuna, the greatest archer, found a village where every painted target had an arrow dead center, even a tiny bullseye bent around the corner of a shed. The marksman turned out to be a scrawny lad: "He shot the arrow first and then drew the target around it." When your arrow misses, embrace what happened and draw the bullseye around where it landed. Your experience of life becomes immeasurably better.

## When you feel really terrible

Feverish, alone and congested, Rao was about to wallow in self-pity. He remembered the Buddha's teaching that the mind determines well-being, and watched himself feeling sorry for himself. The self-pity "vanished like dew in the tropical sun." He felt empathy for his laboring body and knew "this, too, shall pass." The masters say this detachment holds even in great pain or near death. Practice it on small miseries to prepare for large ones.

## Sleepless nights

Insomnia comes from chatter that paints dark futures and dwells on the two or three things wrong in your life. (At least seven hours of sleep is a good benchmark.) Starting tonight:

1. Five minutes before bed, shine the flashlight of awareness on the ways you are blessed, and think of how your life is unfolding exactly as you would wish in many areas.
2. *Feel* the gratitude rise from the soles of your feet, well up through your body, and gush out through your face. "This is not a 'thinking' exercise." A checklist ("Food to eat, check. Roof over head, check") does not count.
3. To move from thinking gratitude to feeling it, think it mindfully and constantly. Keep registering that you are fortunate to have the very problems you complain about. "Don't wait for good weather—learn to dance in the rain."
4. Persist until there is a lightness in you, then go to bed. Many students report that they fell asleep before finishing.

Include the good in *yourself*, not only in your life. Repeat it on waking, instead of rushing straight into *there-is-too-much-to-do*. Set alarms and reminders through the day. One day an inner glow tells you the thought has become a felt experience. (John Kralik, a failing attorney at fifty-two, wrote one handwritten, heartfelt thank-you note a day for a year. Within the year he had gained financially, lost weight, found true friendship and found inner peace. "When you start focusing on appreciating what others have done for you, there is a sharp decrease in lamentation about your own sorry state." The universe "responds automatically and quickly to accommodate the new person you are becoming.")

## The *beep* and the worst case

At a red light you glance at your phone, and the car behind you beeps. You fume, glare at the driver at the next light, and the trivial beep colors your day. If a beep can do that, what will your ex-husband, your son or your boss do? There is a moment when you hear the beep and can decide: **"Am I going to let this disturb me?"** Be the observer who hears it, and let it go. This does not mean you skip what you must do. It means you do it knowing you are doing what you can in the best way you can, at peace with whatever the outcome. "It is ridiculously easy. It is unbelievably hard." (This is from Michael Singer, *The Untethered Soul*, which a CPM alumnus called "spiritual TNT".)

Wanting to let go is not the same as being able to. For disturbances that recur, use this:

1. **List** everything that disturbs you with some predictability: a daughter who won't take college seriously and has an unsuitable boyfriend, a career stalled in a dead-end job, doubts about your marriage.
2. **Imagine the worst case** for each: she drops out, gets pregnant and is abandoned; you are fired without notice; your spouse leaves before you decide.
3. **Recognize that it is just a thought.** It may happen or not. Imagine that it does happen, and work out what you will do. "When you have consciously and explicitly recognized that this could happen and what you will do if it does, it loses its power to terrify you." Make your peace with it.
4. **Go about your business.** Take steps to prevent it, and focus relentlessly on your intent and on what you are doing. Don't think about what could happen, since you have no control over the outcome. Energy poured into the doing brings peace instead of turbulence.

Repeat for every situation that disturbs you.

## Put down the suitcase

Rao's guru told him before he left for America: "Srikumar, the train is powerful enough to carry you and your suitcases. You do not have to carry the suitcases on your head. Put them down." The suitcases are fear, anxiety, self-loathing, insecurity, the fear that you are not as good as people think and will be found out, the fear of losing what you have accumulated, and the dread of global forces in the headlines. Ask: when were you last completely okay? We try to fix internal turbulence with external action. Lonely, we rush to find "the one". Afraid of being fired, we hold back and avoid rocking the boat.

To put them down, first be aware that you are carrying them. Watch the stories you tell yourself, how fast they arise, and how quickly they push you into emotional domains you do not want. A sure-thing client goes silent, and you spin what-ifs about the proposal, then your company, then your future. These are "no more than stories" with no power except what you give them. "Stuff happens. Watch it happen and enjoy it." Tell yourself stories you can enjoy even when things go wrong. When an expensive restaurant switches your order, refuse to give any server the power to decide how you feel, and enjoy the strange dish. Do this with everything that bothers you each day.

## How to let go

Felicity, nearly forty, had just ended a three-year relationship with a man who would not commit, and everyone told her to "let it go." "Just how the \*\*\*\* do I 'let it go'?" Tennis players relive the double fault, and divorcing partners remember every unpleasant remark. Lugged-around history makes you dread the boss who slighted you, and colors how you see your spouse or child. Let *all* of it go, this way:

1. Separate yourself from the persistent, painful thoughts. Become aware of the chatter and recognize it as just chatter. Felicity had built a whole future life, fallen in love with it, and lost it, and all of it was a thought in her head.
2. Skip distractions such as movies, calling friends or alcohol.
3. Sit and watch your thoughts as if in a theater. Feel the pain, then move to the position of observing yourself feeling the pain.
4. You will slip. Pick yourself up each time and return to **witness mode**.

**Sandcastles**: there is nothing wrong with building them, but don't get so attached that one crumbling tosses you for a loop. Enjoy the building and don't obsess over the outcome. Within a month Felicity could talk about him without breaking down, and then he became "a bittersweet memory with no emotional drain."

**What a baby knows.** Take a baby's bottle and he wails in full fury. Give it back and in seconds he is gurgling again. "The baby experiences anger and frustration to its fullest extent, and then he lets it go!" We are good at the experiencing and lousy at the letting go. Hurts, resentments and slights are "a rock on our heads." Sasha snubbed her old high-school rival Denise as a "traitor" at a party, then found the next day that Denise was her new boss. Holding on imprisons the other person, but harms you most. "Try to put your rock down."

## Rewriting your past

Clients blame the present on some past tragedy, and they are all sure nothing can be done about the past. They are wrong. "The events that happened in your life don't matter all that much. The stories you tell yourself about those events matter a great deal." Joe, a poor scholarship boy, decided that his idol Frank was ashamed of him, and turned down Frank's invitation to a weekend for just the two of them as charity. Years later Frank, now the client's attorney, explained himself. He had been ashamed of his *shallow friends*, had arranged the weekend to learn from Joe, and was still hurt by the refusal. Joe's decades-old "molten ball of lead" disappeared. "What happened in your past matters a little. The stories you tell yourself about what happened to you in your past matter a great deal. Choose wisely."

## Manufactured suffering

In the film *Lion*, Saroo finds his birth family decades after he was lost and learns that his brother Guddu died the night he disappeared. He collapses in grief. The grief is today's, though the death came nearly three decades earlier: "He took a memory, infused it with emotion, mixed it with recent knowledge of past events, and felt deep pain." It was socially appropriate and harmless if released in good time, but he made it up. You do the same every time you wince at an ex or an old mistake, or worry about what "could happen." Think about this until you see clearly that you are doing it. That recognition is the first step on "the long journey to rehabilitation."

## A terrific day, every day

On waking, make a conscious, mindful decision that you will have a fantastic day. It fails only because of a false belief that a terrific day requires (1) that what you want happens and (2) that what you don't want doesn't. The car won't start, someone sideswipes you, the deal is postponed. The day is lousy only if you let it be. "Feces will drop from the sky. It always does." That just means some unexpected cleanup within your terrific day. When unwanted things happen, address them as if they were people: **"Am I going to let you steal my fantastic day from me?"** Tell a flat tire on the way to an important meeting that it will not ruin your perfect day, and mean it. Then reschedule and do what is needed "from a space of tranquil ease." Each evening for two to three weeks, with an alarm reminder, journal whether it was a fantastic day.

## Life as a game (the WD-40)

Shanna was a fourth-year associate on partner track, with a key partner making passes at her, a broken engagement, a father whose dementia was speeding up, and a new BMW that kept breaking down. Finding the faulty model takes help, so Rao offers a general-purpose fix, like WD-40: **convert whatever situation you resent into a game.** You decide the rules. The fun is in the playing, and winning is nice but not mandatory. Step outside yourself and view life as a movie you star in, with a script you keep rewriting. As a game, Shanna can weigh her moves with the partner dispassionately. She can go to HR, send a stronger signal, or mention his advances to others who could later corroborate. With her father she can say how much he means to her, have deeper conversations, and say goodbye to him in her head.

To "This is *real life*... there are *consequences*": life *can* be a game, and only played as one is it truly enjoyed. Whether it is a game "is a *decision* you make. It happens between your ears." Games have consequences too, and you accept them. An adverse event means you lost that game, and it is time for the next one.
