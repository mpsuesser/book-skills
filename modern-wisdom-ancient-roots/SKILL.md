# Modern Wisdom, Ancient Roots

Srikumar Rao teaches ancient wisdom (Vedanta, Zen, the Buddha, Rumi, the sages) to high achievers, as he does in his course **Creativity and Personal Mastery (CPM)**. He opens with this claim: "We are sick!" The mind that will not let us be is a deep sickness, and we label it *normal*. The **left little finger** test shows it: in the past twenty-four hours you gave your little finger no thought, because a healthy part works silently in the background. Your mind constantly demands attention (*Am I too fat? Why doesn't he call?*), so it is unwell. The cure is not easy or fast. It is "a lifelong pursuit."

The book is a lens and a toolkit, taught through parables. Apply it the way Rao coaches:

1. **Find the chatter.** Pick out the story the person's mind is telling about the situation, and separate it from what actually happened.
2. **Name the mental model** under the chatter, the notion of how the world works that produces it (for example the if-then model, *I need X to be successful or happy*, or the belief that other people must change).
3. **Prescribe the practice** for that situation from the reference files: its exact steps, its question, and the parable that carries it. Give one practice at a time.
4. **Turn it toward growth.** Whatever happens, the question is how the person grows. Rao does not tell people to stop striving, and he does not decide for them whether to stay or quit. He changes the place they act from (gratitude rather than dissatisfaction, service rather than wanting) and who they are being while they act.

Speak as Rao does: plainly, with gentle provocation, and asserting what he asserts at full strength.

## Mental models

A **mental model** is "a notion we have about the way the world works." We hold dozens, perhaps hundreds: how to get a job, how to get ahead, how to find a partner. "The problem is not that we have mental models. The problem is that we do not recognize that we have mental models. We think the model is reality and not just a model." Most of them serve you well, which is why you have achieved what you have. Some do not, "and those models are responsible for *all* the problems in your life." He means *all*, not some.

- **Change can be overnight.** Sri Ramakrishna's sealed cave: when the rocks are removed, darkness that lasted thousands of years vanishes at once. Behavior that has lasted decades can change in an instant. It does not change by force of will. It changes when you change the models, because "as you make changes in them, you literally become a different person."
- "A small tweak in a mental model can make a huge difference in your experience of life." An investment banker's wife attacked him for coming home late. His meditation teacher reminded him of the mentored child who pounded on his chest when he arrived late, and whom he had soothed easily: "When your wife attacks you, she is really saying, 'I love you. I want to be with you. Why are you not there?'" He answered her with compassion, and the marriage healed.
- **"You cannot think differently by trying to think differently."** Platitudes such as "see the glass as half full" fail. The only way to think differently consistently is to examine the models that make you think as you do and change them, with small tweaks for some and major restructuring for others. Then the new thinking feels natural to you and radically different to everyone else.
- **Willpower does violence to yourself.** Change forced by an act of will usually fails, and when it does work it brings side effects (quit smoking cold turkey, gain weight). Change the model instead, and the new behavior "happens effortlessly as a by-product." A nursery teacher seated a hungry boy at a table of four boys who would not drink their milk. Once he began grabbing their glasses, their model changed from "I can take my time" to "If I don't drink it fast, I will lose it."
- **Exercise: identify your models.** Write down three to five mental models you act on, each of great significance to you. Include at least one about your job or professional development (how to get a good job), one about your personal life (how to raise children), and one broad "this is the way the world works" model. Put them aside for a couple of weeks, then study them. Are they serving you well? Would a tweak or a replacement make life better? Make the changes, working on only one model at a time.
- **Exercise: changed minds.** Recall every time you changed your mind about someone after new information arrived. One example: the elderly neighbor who seemed to be ignoring your call, when his daughter had deleted his voicemail.

## Mental chatter and the witness

**Mental chatter** is "that internal monologue you have going on in your head—*all* the time." It paints dark scenarios and drags you along with it. Rao's images for it: Sinbad's **Old Man of the Sea**, who locks his legs around your neck and will not let you rest; a wedding crasher who plants worry and dissension and then slips away; "an untrained stallion that takes you where it wants to go." The mind "is simply an unceasing flow of thoughts, and they are all directed outward."

- **The terrible mistake** is that you *believe* it. You think the voice is you and that it is true. Audit its record: the client who missed a call had a dental emergency, and he was not about to defect. "Mental chatter is fake news." Double- and triple-check everything it tells you, and do not let it set your mood.
- **The first exercise** Rao gives every client is this: watch your mental chatter, and observe it. Become "a witness of [your] mental chatter rather than someone mixed up with and carried away by it." Watch it "as if it were a movie and you are the audience." Your chatter "does not like being viewed": its intensity drops and its tone changes. When Rao had a 103-degree fever, "Instead of feeling sorry for myself, I watched myself feeling sorry for myself," as if from a camera a foot above his head, and the self-pity vanished.
- Thoughts are clouds. They come and go, and they can drag you down only if you lose awareness. "Thoughts in your head are both supremely powerful and utterly inconsequential": powerful because you identify with them, inconsequential because you can refuse to.
- **Level one** applies when chatter seems useful, as a warning to plan ahead. Chatter "just is. It is neither 'good' nor 'bad.'" Remain the witness and take what it says **under advisement**, as "simply a notification, like the warning light that comes on in your car when you run low on gas." The trouble comes only from identifying with it.
- **Level two** is for people ready for it. The witness of level one is itself fake news. What you call "me" is a **body-mind-intellect (BMI)**: fictitious, arising from thought, like a dream. "You are pure awareness contemplating it," beyond space and time. When people told Ramana Maharshi they were anxious, he asked them to contemplate *who* is anxious. Rao calls this the most direct path to enlightenment, or moksha, and "really the only thing that matters."
- **Many selves.** The illusion is "that you are one single permanent self that is present all the time." There is the resolute morning self and the snack-eating evening self. One CPM student wrote of his Nigerian me, UK-born me, Worry me, Indulgent me and Creative me all fighting over dinner. These selves come and go and are all equally unreal. "You are none of these selves but rather the observer of these selves. Rest in that state." Exercise: list all the people you are and the roles you play, then decide which ones you could be more genuine in and which you could let go of altogether.

## The garden and the laundry

"Your mind is the most fertile garden that you will ever see. It *will* bring forth." Envy at the *Forbes* 400 issue, lust, mentally consigning your boss to Gitmo: these are weeds. Thousands spring up each day unnoticed, take root, and grow into addictions and destructive thought patterns, so be "ruthlessly diligent about the weeding." The work never ends, just as the laundry hamper fills again. Clarity after meditation fades, and you revert to "an ego-driven automaton, jerked about by fickle desires." So keep meditating and reflecting, and surround yourself with aids such as like-minded friends, books, audio and video, retreats and apps. Make it permanent, like laundry.

**Where you live**: "We don't live in a bungalow or a duplex or a mansion or an apartment. We live in our minds." In that mind, regrets are piled in a corner, expectations stuffed in a closet, comparisons spilled on the table and grudges stinking in a box. "We cannot outsource the housekeeping."

## Emotional domains and deliberate intent

An **emotional domain** is the inner place you occupy: gratitude, appreciation, possibility, or despair, self-pity, anger. Rao's tools move people between domains.

- **Deliberate intent**: "Consciously and deliberately decide what emotional domain you would like to occupy. Then examine the tools at your disposal that will help you get to that space and select an appropriate one." Without it you get **hijacked**: inertia, a Netflix teaser, or a ranting politician in the **attention economy** chooses your domain for you.
- **Gratitude** is the master domain. "You cannot be depressed when you are being appreciative," and "it is impossible to be both grateful and angry at the same time." It must be *felt*, not thought or ticked off as a checklist.
- **The rosogolla**: however you mistreat this Indian sweet, only syrup comes out, because that is what is inside it. "You get angry because there is anger in you." Life is a skillful teacher that presents triggers. When gratitude becomes your default domain, care, compassion and reasoned action come out of you, whatever happens.
- **Replace "have to" with "get to."** You don't *have to* feed your son breakfast and walk him to the bus, go to work, or cook the healthy dinner. You *get to*. It is a simple swap, and it changes how you experience your days.
- **Awareness is like a flashlight.** It lights whatever you shine it on. We spend our energy on "the two or three things that you have arbitrarily decided are wrong with your life" and ignore "the fifty to five hundred things that are pretty darn good."

## The if-then model and growth

The **if-then life**: "*If I get X, then I will be happy.*" (If I get promoted, if my partner behaves.) Well-wishers egg us on with "go for it," so we chase one goal after another, get a rush, and return to where we started. We "tinker with what we put on the 'if' side" and never see that the model itself is faulty. It runs on fear: "You don't have control. You've never had control. You never will have control."

- The replacement is **growth**. "The only point of life is to grow." "Our work is not about our work": it is "the ultimate vehicle for our personal growth." Whether the business fails or becomes a billion-dollar company, "the question is, how did you *grow*?"
- Pain and success alike are occasions to grow: "Pain can lead to growth. Success can lead to growth."
- **The benevolent universe**: adopt the model that the world has your back and everything that happens is for your good. Rumi: if you stop running after what you think you want and sit "in my own place of patience, what I need flows to me, and without pain"; "what I want also wants me, is looking for me and attracting me." When you think differently, "the universe becomes more friendly." It works even better when you let go of strong wants and leave it to the universe to decide what comes your way.
- **Misery is resistance.** "All of our misery, all of our pain, is caused by our resistance to the way life is unfolding... Joy comes from total acceptance of what is." Everyone is a **control freak** wedded to preferences, and everyone denies it by pointing at someone worse. You can train your desires. Swami Dayananda forced down a bitter karela curry first to get it over with, and every hostess then served him heaps of it: "So I decided to like karela curry, and it is now one of my favorite dishes!"
- **Try your level best, and accept either result.** "If you succeed, fantastic. If you do not succeed, fantastic." Do not let success or failure touch your equanimity.
- **"You cannot solve an internal problem with external action."** Strivers try to patch an inner hole with attainment and acquisitions, and the hole remains or grows. Joseph Heller, told that a billionaire host made more in a day than *Catch-22* ever earned, replied: "Yes, but I have something he will never have—enough."

## Process, not outcome

Actions are within your control, but outcomes are not. "Invest in the process and not the outcome." John Wooden never once talked about winning in twenty-seven years at UCLA, and believed his teams won more because of it. Set the goal, then stop obsessing over it, and pour your attention into what you must do. **Enlightened detachment** means your well-being does not depend on reaching the goal, and that makes reaching it "far more likely."

- **Striving as a rose blooms.** A rose does not try to be the best rose, and it is neither diminished by a better one nor superior to a fading one. Keep setting goals and seeking promotion (you cannot stop anyway), but do it "not because you *want* anything but because this activity is part of the natural unfolding of life for you."
- **The reward is the change in you.** "Your gain comes from the *effort* you put into attaining it." "It will be wonderful if you achieve your goal—no problem if you do not. On this road you will always win."
- **Success is a by-product.** Following the spiritual path brings material success too. But "if you try to make it a direct goal, it will disappear." Mr. Miyagi's sanding and waxing built the karate.
- **Meaning discovers you.** "You *do not* discover meaning. It discovers you. You help it discover you by creating it." Meditating to calm the mind, or to become enlightened, keeps you stuck. "Meditate because you meditate." "The purpose of washing dishes is not to get them clean. The purpose of washing dishes is to wash the dishes."

## Who am I being?

At a fork, people ask "What should I do?" and weigh pros and cons. Rao asks instead: **"Who am I being?"** Stuck in a toxic job, you are being a victim of external circumstances: self-pitying, me-centered, outside appreciation and gratitude. Decide who you want to be (for example calm, serene, grateful, and willing to work hard to improve what falls short of your preferences). Pour your emotional energy into being that person. At first it feels like kidding yourself, and that passes. Then ask what that person would do, and "the answer will pop out easily." Staying or quitting matters less than this: "Who you are being is *much* more important than what you are doing."

"We can break rocks. Or we can help build a cathedral." Only you can define your cathedral. Without one, you get "a mediocre existence punctuated with flashes of pleasure."
