# Desire, habits and freedom

Practices for addictions and stubborn habits, for desire and possessions, and for the inner prison that outer freedom does not open.

## The marvelous prison

A sage lived on lentils. A student who had grown rich at court begged him to come to the capital, where all he had to do was praise the king. The sage wept: "Do you not see that if you would learn to subsist on lentils, you would not have to praise His Majesty?" Krista Tippett, who had friends on both sides of the Berlin Wall, saw that East Berliners, denied their choice of paint and college major, formed poetry circles and led lives of dignity and purpose, while subsidized West Berliners were anxious amid plenty. A participant who grew up in Communist Hungary said most people he knew "were happier then than they are now." Rao is not arguing for autocracy. He is saying that our notions of freedom and happiness are flat-out wrong.

We define freedom too narrowly, as the elimination of restrictions on behavior. Meanwhile we remain prey to "the ruthless harpies of desire" and to addictions. Cocaine we outlaw, workaholism we applaud and reward, and hypochondria and gambling we barely acknowledge. "We give to others the power to determine our happiness and tranquility and do not even recognize that we have done so." Test yourself: how free are you if you cannot sit quietly by yourself for half an hour, or quiet your mind without the opiate of a screen? Cut the tethers, many of them electronic. Don Juan's "man of knowledge" lives by **controlled folly**: he chooses a path with heart and acts as if it matters, while knowing it doesn't, so that whether his acts worked "is in no way part of his concern." "Stone walls do not a prison make, / Nor iron bars a cage."

**Exercise: the cage journal.** Write down your own definition of freedom and compare it with this one. Then ask how fear becomes your cage, and what part your chatter, other people and your occupation play in building it. Keep a journal and write down each moment you feel "in the cage", with the circumstances and whether the source is a mental model, chatter, someone you let influence you, or your work. After ten entries, look for what they share: "If you know your enemy, you can better fight him off!" Then pick one and dismantle the cage. A physician who wanted Rao's course was blocked by on-call weekends. Once he saw that "barriers exist only between our ears," he simply told his partners that one of them would cover, without asking permission, and they did. Work through the rest. Some cages will be easy and some will feel insurmountable. Return to the hard ones in a few months, and again later.

## The overstuffed closet of fear

Sally, a well-paid single mother, hated her job but placated everyone. She agreed to bad action items, laughed at inappropriate jokes, and "died a little bit every day." Underneath was an unspoken chain: lose the job, lose the health insurance, her daughter falls ill, bankruptcy, leave the Manhattan apartment, move in with her parents. "Fear is a thing." But who decided that the Manhattan apartment and that salary were needed for happiness? "Sally did," without realizing it. Our lives are an overstuffed closet of likes, dislikes, desires, worries and guilt that we dare not open, so we cower in the small room we built from our fears. Rao does not yet advise opening the door. First recognize that the closet exists, which is why you are so full of fear. Then "take out your worst fear and examine it ruthlessly in bright sunshine and make peace with it." Think about it, again and again. Sally finally accepted that she might be fired and was ready to teach in rural Wisconsin. She then spoke up at an all-hands meeting against an ill-conceived project, and she was promoted. (The full worst-case procedure is in `chatter-emotions-adversity.md`, under the *beep*.)

## Kill that child

In a story Rao read as a boy at a Jesuit school, a king riding alone was attacked by a small child with a play sword, and laughingly kissed him. The child came back each day a little bigger, with a heftier sword, and the king kept sparing him. Eventually a muscular man knocked the king from his horse, and when the king said "Tomorrow will be a different story," the man answered "There will be no tomorrow" and beheaded him. Every destructive tendency and addiction begins with "a vague and comforting desire, like the tendrils of some tropical plant": one more cigarette to calm my nerves, one more drink after a horrible day. "One will not hurt. But many ones piled on top of each other become devastating." "You did not become fat with a single bite or a single meal." Your foe is probably not a child anymore, but he is still weaker than you. "Kill him right now," or regret will follow.

**Exercise: remove it.** List every addiction or unhealthy habit you have, including the subtle ones that come in small doses. Ask how you fuel each one: is it easy, or comfortable? Take one (smoking, drinking, desserts, a codependent relationship, gambling, comfort shopping, chronic negativity to keep expectations low), list the excuses you make for keeping it, cross them out, and make a plan to remove it. Do research, and give it time.

## Deep reflection

An alcoholic student had quit many times and always relapsed: "I know it's bad for me... but I just can't stop myself." Rao's own weakness is salty Indian snacks. Others check email compulsively, are driven by overweening ambition, or neglect their families for their careers. The disquieting news: "The reason you indulge in these self-defeating behaviors is that you *don't* know how much they are harming you." You have only a vague, nebulous feeling. Walking a hedged country lane, you see an out-of-control car careening toward you. You do not consult a sage. You go through the hedge instantly, scratches and torn clothes be damned, because you *know* viscerally what the car will do. With equal clarity about your habit, you would stop. The sudden death of someone close gives you that clarity for a while, and then you revert, because "you have been programmed to revert to your unthinking ways."

The tool that anchors the clarity is **deep reflection**: "you consciously bring to mind and meditate on the ramifications of the behavior that is troubling you." Match the reflection to the behavior:

- **Ambition, or work consuming your family and your sleep**: meditate on the inevitability of death. James Shirley wrote that scepter and crown must tumble down and in the dust be made equal with the scythe and spade. In India, seekers meditate in cremation grounds and graveyards to impress on themselves that life is transitory.
- **Lust**: reflect on what the body is made of: blood that pours from cuts, pus in wounds, bones, excrement, entrails.
- **Drink**: reflect on the artists and actors who died early of liver failure, and on the accomplishments that will never happen because of the addiction.

"Whatever it is that troubles you, there is a deep reflection that can rid you of that programming. This is a very powerful tool. Use it with care."

## Why resolutions fail

New Year's and birthday resolutions fail because we try to bring about profound change by an act of will, and "every time you try to bring about change by an effort of will, you do violence to yourself." Change the mental model instead (see `SKILL.md`), and the new behavior comes as a by-product. A friend who fumed at the bridge traffic on his two-hour drive, and who "never had time" to meditate, took up **driving meditation**, adapted from Zen walking meditation. He breathed all the way in and all the way out, and stayed with the breath. Traffic jams became a chance to practice, the peace lasted beyond the trip, and he began looking forward to the next snarl. Examine the models behind the "suffering" you endure, and change them.

## Want what comes

"Why do we need—ever so strongly need—the events of our life to be a particular way?" We ask questions about everything outside us and never this one about what is inside. You insist on the promotion, the rising revenue, happy children, delicious food, a caring spouse, and a dog that does its business quickly. The reason it is not all right for life to unfold as it does is "because there is fear in you. You have bought into the if-then model." Think of something you badly did not want that happened anyway and caused turmoil at the time. With hindsight, was it a big deal? The same will be true of today's. If someone else gets the department-head job, can you simply accept that "this happened" and keep enjoying each day, having tried your level best?

Train your preferences in small ways. When a dithering driver makes you miss the light, "smile and wish him well. Beam out good will." If you want to watch your favorite show and your wife wants dinner out, go, and decide to have a good time. Keep count of these occasions, raise the number, and notice what happens to your experience of life. Try it for a week.

## Deliberate intent and the journeys you take

After a good day, Rao watched a gory thriller out of sheer inertia while a book he loved about a revered sage lay at hand. He regretted it. Life is one long journey made up of smaller ones: every book, film, bar evening or tennis game is a journey. Ask: **"Is this a journey I want to take? Does it take me to a place where I want to spend time?"** Asked sincerely and repeatedly, the question changes the films you watch, the books you read, the friends you see, the topics you raise with them, and how you spend your leisure. Otherwise you are **hijacked**, like a carjacking but with no gun. A Netflix series promoted by an email with a tantalizing subject line turns out to be worse than its ad, and inertia makes you finish the episode. Meanwhile you could have meditated, finished the book, prepared for the big meeting, called your parents, or reached a friend you had lost touch with.

In the **attention economy** "everybody wants you to notice what they would like you to notice." Ranting politicians win, and you lose when your emotional domain is "hijacked by someone else's agenda." Strong negative emotions create a vortex, which is why newscasts lead with tragedy and social media multiplies the ways you can be assaulted. **Test**: for one week, tally the stories in your favorite news program that leave you morose against those that leave you energized and hopeful. Decide in advance, consciously, which journeys you will take and when. If you choose tranquil contemplation, the Netflix blandishments lose their pull. You will still be hijacked sometimes, but less often.

**"Do I *really*...?"** This one question, asked sincerely, changes your life. The closet fills because we ask "Is there any conceivable occasion when I may use this?", to which the answer is always yes. Ask instead **"Do I *really* want this in my life?"** What is the worst that happens if you give it away? Not much. The question works best on the mind. If you like slasher films or edgy thrillers, you spend time in a world where violence is the answer. "Do I *really* want to go there?" It is fine if you do, as long as you go consciously and mindfully. Make an emotional "Do I really...?" list of the baggage, grudges and political biases you are holding on to, and ask what you really need.

## Transience and clutter

Rao *really* wanted a smoked-glass dining table, and paid more than he could easily afford. It followed the family through three homes, held decades of games and parties, and became his son's model-building bench. Streaked with paint, it went to the curb before garbage day. "Whatever we gather and hoard and cherish is in time and space. And whatever is in time and space will wither and be stripped from us": a baby's first tooth in its silver casket, the diploma, the fine wine, the keepsakes. "It's all stuff. Just stuff." Reflect on impermanence until it seeps in. You will still use things, but without attachment, and you get "a tiny reflection of the freedom that sages have known."

**Exercise.** Pick up any item you have not used for a year or more. Does it still add value to your life? If you do not actively want it, give it to someone deserving. The joy outweighs the pang. Go through your wardrobe for clothes unworn in two years and ask each one, "Do I *really* need you in my life today?" Then do the rest of the house (furniture, linens, bric-a-brac, kitchen things, toys, games), asking "Do you bring joy into my life today, right now?" Give away everything that gets a no.
