# Work and teams: Think Brutal, Small, Motion, Casual

How Simplicity runs the people side of work: honesty and standards, group size and the decision maker, process and approvals, timelines, meetings, briefings and presentations.

## Think Brutal

**Blunt is Simplicity. Meandering is Complexity.** Jobs told people what was on his mind whoever they were and whatever the mood in the room. At their first meeting he told the author: "I really like the TV you've been doing... The print is really shit." There was no malice in it. He was simply being straight, and the relationship stayed that simple for years.

- **Discomfort averse.** Most people cannot be that honest all the time. They are not devious; they want to spare feelings or avoid wrecking the mood. Corporate life runs instead on "the varnished truth, followed closely by the sporadic truth."
- **The middleman.** Feedback relayed through someone else picks up their agenda. The CEO had comments that could easily be addressed, you hear "fatal flaws," the team goes off to rethink the whole project, and "Complexity has burrowed into your business relationship." Get feedback from its source.
- **Brutalvision.** Clarity is missing in an organization "90 percent of the time," and most people never notice. Open a few random company emails, activate your brutalvision and read: the muddy messages are everywhere. If people wrote brutally honest emails, the time spent on in-boxes would "surely decrease by half."
- **The easiest element to institute.** "Just be honest and never hold back. Demand the same from those you work with." Some people will squirm, but everyone will know where they stand, and all of the group's time goes to forward progress instead of decoding what people really meant. Jobs cut people off when they rambled.
- **Brutally honest, not brutal.** Straight talk does not require being manipulative or mean. It is "saying what needs to be said to push your team to deliver the best possible results." You can do the brutal thing without being brutal: explain your reasons. "Being brutal and being respected are not mutually exclusive." Honesty at the right moment earns respect.

### Standards aren't for bending

- "There's no 'almost' when it comes to making things simpler." Chiat/Day's T-shirt read "Good enough is not enough," and for Jobs even that was not tough enough. You cannot let yourself be talked into something you know can be better. Ever.
- Settling for second best violates Simplicity. It "plants the seeds for disappointment, extra work, and more meetings," and it lands you in the worst possible business position: "having to defend an idea you never believed in."
- **The B team.** The author once let compassion override his standards and kept a hardworking team's "good enough" ads in a show for Jobs, next to better work. Jobs pointed straight at them: "Ah. So you put the B team on this one, did you?" The weaker work diminished the whole show and cost credibility. It was "easier, not smarter."
- Showing work you know is weak is worse still. After the agency presented a campaign everyone privately doubted, Jobs opened the next meeting with: "I knew it was shit, *you* knew it was shit, but you came all the way out here and showed it to me anyway. That's not acceptable."
- In Apple's world "every manager has to be a ruthless enforcer of high standards." Compromise sends you back to the drawing board and raises questions about your talent in the client's mind.

### Know where you stand

- There is great value in understanding the state of a relationship: what you have done right, what you have done wrong, and what you must do next. "It's when things are left unresolved that people spend too much of their time looking over their shoulders instead of looking ahead. That's when Complexity creeps in." Once the work was fixed, Jobs went round the table and graded each agency person to their face, from A to F.
- **Real-time honesty beats a report card.** Intel and its agency exchanged formal quarterly report cards on the relationship. Jobs would never have spent time on that: "If something was bothering him, he'd simply tell us. That was our report card."
- **The hard reset.** Jobs reset from meeting to meeting. A flaming fight one day was business as usual the next, so every situation was judged on its merits and being a dunce yesterday did not make you one today. The way back into favor was "simply to demonstrate your worth."
- **Learn to take a punch.** Being attacked did not mean being fired. Survivors got stronger and developed "a growing immunity to attacks." A thicker skin is a decided advantage. Jobs had one himself: when he killed the Mac clones in 1997 and was crucified for it, he "absorbed what he wished and blocked out the rest."
- **The rotating turret.** A former senior staffer named the routine that followed a stupid remark in a Jobs meeting: an uncomfortable pause, the slow turn of the turret toward the speaker, the lock-on, the full barrage. Everyone knew the rule: show your lack of smarts and it comes "right between the eyes."
- Jobs respected the people who did the work over the "handlers." To an agency account man he said, "Oh, so you're overhead." He would invest millions in what he believed in and walk away from an investment the moment his strategy shifted (he once killed a TV campaign on the eve of production, losing more than a million dollars), but he hated spending foolishly.

## Think Small

**Small groups of smart people** is "one of Simplicity's most important rules." Everyone in the room is there for a reason, with no "mercy invitation": "Either you're critical to the meeting or you're not. It's nothing personal, just business." Spectators are not welcome. A smaller group is more focused and motivated, and smarter people do higher-quality work. Jobs, opening a meeting, spotted a stranger ("Lorrie"), asked "Who are you?", heard her out, and said: "I don't think we need you in this meeting, Lorrie. Thanks." Her crime: "She wasn't necessary."

- **Diagnose it.** How many overpopulated meetings do you sit through in a year? How many get sidetracked in a way that would never happen if the group were half the size?
- **No exceptions.** It is easier to stay silent and let the Lorries take their seats, and most people are too mannerly to eject anyone. But each allowance is an exception, and "Simplicity is never achieved through exceptions." Explain your reasons and keep the group small.
- **Small is relative.** Making groups smaller will not solve every problem. Only you know your business well enough to draw the line between too few and too many; then enforce it, and hit the process with the Simple Stick whenever it threatens unnecessary expansion.
- **Culture is the obstacle.** Large groups form because "the way we do things here" is bigger than any one person. The zealots of Simplicity have to overcome that inertia. Apple does not post rules for running meetings on the walls. It assumes every hire is a fully functioning adult with Brains and Common Sense; "Simplicity prefers not having to train a bucking bronco."
- **How to Have a Great Meeting** (the author's replacement for big-company wall signs, exaggerated on purpose): 1. Throw out the least necessary person at the table. 2. Walk out of this meeting if it lasts more than 30 minutes. 3. Do something productive today to make up for the time you spent here. Meetings are still necessary; the small-group rule is a guideline for meetings and "mandatory for project groups."
- Jay Chiat once ejected the author and his partner from a meeting: "You shouldn't be sitting around a table talking about this bullshit... Go create something."

### The people-count fallacy

Big companies follow "an instinctive but misguided principle: The more critical the project, the more people must be thrown at it." More brains do mean more ideas, but "only occasionally do more brains mean *better* ideas." Every added person makes briefings more complicated, takes more hand-holding to bring them up to speed, and takes more time reviewing their work and giving feedback. Adding people says you lack confidence in the group you started with, or you want "an insurance policy," which says the same thing. Either way the real message is that the wrong people are on the job. "So fix that." A small group is the most efficient way to succeed, "assuming that it also has the smarts." Well staffed, a small group gives management more confidence, not less; the group feels appreciated and takes on more work.

### Think big, act small

"Apple encourages big thinking but small everything else." Jobs at the 2010 All Things Digital conference: "You know how many committees we have at Apple? Zero. We're organized like a start-up. We're the biggest start-up on the planet." And: "I don't see why you have to change as you get big." He capped the original Mac team at one hundred people (to add one, you took one out), because he could not remember more than a hundred first names and liked to work "where I touch everything." Apple's annual off-site for its global executives, the **Top 100**, carried the same idea.

### The Laws of Small

1. "The quality of work resulting from a project is inversely proportional to the number of people involved in the project."
2. "The quality of work resulting from a project increases in direct proportion to the degree of involvement by the ultimate decision maker."

The ultimate decision maker need not be the CEO; it is whoever holds the authority. That person must be in the briefing and engaged at points throughout. Working for weeks or months before hearing from the responsible authority is "counterproductive—and frustrating as hell." "Look suspiciously at any project plan that doesn't include the regular participation of the final decision maker"; a decision maker who appears only at the end to say yea or nay is "a recipe for frustration and mediocrity."

Jobs was in every marketing presentation and was involved in every image and word of *Think different*. He also allowed nobody, not even the VP of marketing, to see the agency's ideas before he did: "I don't want someone guessing what I'm going to like or not like," he explained more than once. "Maybe I'll see a spark in there that nobody else sees." If you think something is important, you find time for it. If you truly cannot, or it is not your skill, hire or delegate someone "and give them real authority."

### Small groups make better relationships

The Apple and Chiat partnership survived Jobs's moods because both sides were small groups of smart people who knew exactly where they stood, shared a passion, and told each other everything, so problems were fixed quickly. Two large groups produce friction and formality: side issues, overanalysis, second-guessing, staged and choreographed meetings. "Honesty can be replaced by calculation and relationships can get 'managed' rather than nurtured." The signature line, from an agency account director at Intel's agency after the team had agreed a recommendation the client had asked for: "We can't have an opinion until we know what Intel's opinion is."

### Process vs. Simplicity

- **"When process is king, ideas will never be."** The more layers you add to a process, "the more watered down the final work will become." Big companies identify their challenges, develop strategies and even create great work, then "their processes choke the life out of that work" with "endless meetings and multiple approvals."
- **A better idea is a better idea.** At Apple, a better idea that turned up mid-production, after Jobs had approved the ad, went straight back to him, and he expected exactly that. At Intel, once an ad had been socialized among stakeholders, researched, revised and approved, no substantial change was allowed however much better it was: "All must bow to the process."
- **Zero-defect thinking.** Intel's CEO asked his marketers for "zero-defect advertising." Great ideas carry risk, and a defect or two is the price of brilliance; "in the hyperefficient world of Intel, risk is a defect." Battening down the hatches does not help Simplicity. Look at the processes in place and figure out "how to reduce them—not reinforce them."
- **The creative process is "the relative absence of process."** Jobs was not a creative genius so much as "a genius who loved creativity" (Pixar asked him to stay out of its creative meetings). You need no creative talent to be a steward of creative thinking: build an organization that recognizes creativity's needs, and "become skilled in recognizing when a process is more likely to kill a good idea than it is to promote it." Chief marketers who demand brilliance while supporting processes that prevent it imagine a machine "where briefings go in one end and great creative work comes out the other." Great ideas are messier than that.
- **Approval means go.** At Apple, approving an idea cleared the agency to produce it, and Jobs next saw the finished ads. At Intel, approval was the first step on a long road of checks and balances that signaled a lack of trust.
- **Trust your own judgment over testing.** In the author's time at Chiat, Apple tested not a single ad in any medium. Intel's standard development process, by contrast:
  1. Pick the top three campaigns.
  2. Test all three with focus groups in several cities (internationally when required).
  3. Produce the favorite, sometimes the second favorite as a backup.
  4. Retest the finished ads with more focus groups.
  5. Revise per the research.
  6. Run the ads.
  7. Re-retest the ads on air and make running changes.

  "Testing was a religion to Intel, just as Simplicity was to Apple." One test cut a thirty-second spot into thirty frames to graph memorability second by second and show where it needed to be "beefed up," as though a good ad should hit a high note throughout, which is not how storytelling works. Testing homogenizes work toward "the perfect ad," which hits every selling point and offends no one, scores well and makes no emotional connection. Intel's premise was that one bad idea would bring down the empire; Apple's was that "it's better to shoot for the stars and fall short on occasion." Apple handled international concerns by asking its own people on the ground in each country. Testing its hundreds of ads would have cost multiple millions, and a clunker now and then ends nothing. Goodby, Silverstein's *Sheet Metal* for Saturn bombed in focus groups, the client overruled the research, and it became a great success. If you are forced through testing, a client-side partner who shares your values is the best protection.
- "Good ideas need to be protected from those who would do them damage. The best way to do this is to minimize the processes through which these ideas must travel." Jobs had "an instant allergic reaction to any suggestion that might add a layer of complication."
- **Efficiency is for transistors.** Efficiency is right for etching a silicon wafer. Marketing and creative work are many subjective decisions by people with insight into human behavior. Apple is ruthless about operating efficiency and never loses its humanity.

### Keeping hierarchy at bay

Simplicity does not mean fewer hours; working with Apple took as much sheer work as working with IBM. The difference is frame of mind: at Apple most people wake up knowing what needs doing and why. Elsewhere you decode points of view and negotiate "a labyrinth of approvals that can span weeks or months." Hierarchies exhaust people and "whittle away the quality of the work as it's shuttled from meeting to meeting." The marks of a layered process:

- Getting through one meeting only clears you to present to a higher-up who missed the earlier discussion.
- Lower-level people demand more work, a giant pile in which their bosses might find something they like.
- They will not back a position that departs from their marching orders, even a smarter one; standing up for imaginative thinking "puts your neck on the line."

At Apple the top was as flat as "Does Steve like it or not?" Lower down, the same people met week to week, executives appeared at regular checkpoints, reactions were instant, next steps were clear, and the reasons for changes were understood. Jobs called or emailed between meetings as a working member of the group.

## Think Motion

**Simplicity is a big fan of context.** When Jobs returned, Apple was "about ninety days from going bankrupt" and was in the middle of an agency review: more than twenty proposals cut to twelve, then a round of meet-and-greets, then five finalists and a shoot-out. He killed it, called the agency he trusted, and had *Think different* on the air in the time the old process would still have been holding meet-and-greets. Urgency made accelerating the decision and relying on a small group of smart people plain Common Sense.

### The smart timeline

Timelines come leisurely, compressed, relaxed or impossible. "The easiest way to screw up a project is to give it too much time—enough time for people to rethink, revise, have second thoughts, invite others into the project, get more opinions, conduct tests, etc." Bernstein: "To achieve great things, two things are needed; a plan, and not quite enough time." Every industry has its own perfect timeline. In the author's technology work it was about three months from original briefing to finished work going live, enough to develop ideas, share them, respond to comments and produce. Less compromises quality; more invites overthinking. This held with difficult clients and with Jobs alike. Two addenda:

1. **Aim realistically high.** The first iPod was a music player, not a device for music, movies, podcasts and photos; the rest came later. Do not overreach, because the project has to end on time and deliver what was promised. Do not underreach into something lackluster either.
2. **Never stop moving.** The project begins on day one and consumes people from the start. "No time-outs allowed." Constant motion keeps the team focused with the right intensity. "Work isn't supposed to be easy; it's supposed to be gratifying."

The right timing is as important as the right people. "Always be wary of the 'comfortable' timeline": some pressure keeps things moving with purpose, and slack only invites more opinions and more chances to have your ideas "nibbled to death." You may not set the timelines, but you can influence those who do. Jobs's bottom line was that "the ship had to be moving forward every day"; if you weren't helping, you got in trouble. He asked people to work exactly as hard as he did, and no harder.

### Best possible 1.0, then recycle leadership

Build the best possible 1.0 rather than everything at once. The first iPod was extremely limited but pointed the way; the first iPhone did not even support apps. While competitors catch up to 1.0, Apple is at work on 2.0, and the features cut from the original become the starting point of the next version, so each cycle **recycles** its leadership. Competitors chasing a better device fall into specification-heavy advertising (more megapixels, more USB ports), while Apple keeps marketing benefits in human, emotional terms and, unless it has a breakthrough technology, stays out of the spec-comparison game. The longer people live with a product, the more locked into its ecosystem they become: "Simplicity is hard to leave behind."

### Apple vs. Dell: two identical assignments

The author worked on a brand campaign for Apple (1997) and for Dell (2008):

| Apple | Dell |
|---|---|
| Wanted to start immediately | Pondered a schedule of months |
| Led by its CEO | Led by a committee |
| Trusted a small group of smart people | Trusted a small group of incompatible people |
| Knew exactly who it was | Needed to figure out who it was |
| Jobs an active participant | Michael Dell would look in when it was done |
| Needed only the CEO's approval | Needed each division's approval |
| A month to conceive and create a campaign | A month just to talk about strategies |
| Got *Think different* | Got "a stack of presentation boards stored neatly in a dark closet" |

Dell's Consumer and Commercial divisions were separate kingdoms with their own P&Ls, priorities and even logos, represented on the committee by sworn enemies; one member said early on that he would not accept the committee's recommendation. What was missing was the leader reading everyone the riot act on day one. The meetings dwindled to none. "A fractured, leaderless group without an urgent mandate is Simplicity-proof."

### Know who you are

Nobody asked Jobs for a mission statement before *Think different*. He gave a "briefing from the heart," and Apple's essence was known. Dell spent its first weeks working out who it wanted to be. At Microsoft, an early employee loved that "they did things" and wasted no time on debate. That was the opposite of HP, where "process had become more important than progress" and meetings were about the next meeting and the muffins. Then, under legal siege, Microsoft drifted. An executive urging the company to tell the world its values asked the room, "What are our values? Are they written somewhere? Does anyone have them?" It took eighteen months to codify them. An outside pollster then repositioned the brand around "kids, puppies and small businesses," stories the marketers did not have, and the marketing manager ended up presenting ideas he did not believe in: "I feel like I'm working for a tobacco company."

## Think Casual

The agency once spent two weeks letting a new planner prepare a formal "brand audit" for Jobs. "The fact that he had things to set up was the first indication that something was amiss": graphs, charts of numbers and customer photos on four boards of foam core. Jobs sank lower and lower in his chair, heckled, and finally said, "Okay, whatever... Let's look at some ads." Afterwards he phoned to say he would not pay for it and never wanted to see the planner again. The agency had acted like a typical agency, all style over substance.

- It drove Jobs "batty to see in twenty slides what could be spoken in three sentences." He preferred straight talk and raw content. Slickness suggested you had spent your time on the wrapping instead of thinking the idea through.
- He worked best with "a table, a whiteboard, and an honest exchange of ideas," and resisted anything that made relationships feel formal or Apple feel like a big corporation. He put his bare feet on the boardroom table.

### Presenting to an executive

If you can present to Jobs, who was impatient, supercritical and quick to interrupt, you can present to anybody.

- **Respect their time.** "Simplicity gains power through brevity. Simplicity is the direct statement, not the meandering rationalization thereof."
- **Skip the setup.** The author laid three ads face down, as agency tradition dictates, and began recapping the strategy. Three sentences in, Jobs said: "Just show me the ads!" and then, "Are you going to be sitting next to me to explain things when I read the *Wall Street Journal* in the morning?" He flipped them over himself. He knew the goals, so the work had to speak alone, and the useful conversation was which ad was better and why. In effect: "Please stop being ad agency guys and just talk to me."
- "Simplicity is in a hurry." Many people think a bigger word count shows their smarts, when the opposite is almost always nearer the truth. People who communicate briefly come across as smarter.
- **Setup reads as manipulation.** Jobs wanted to judge ideas on their merits, not on your opinion of how he should react, and the more formal the presentation, the more suspicious he became. "There's a thin line between leading clients to a conclusion and treating them like idiots." Lay the facts on the table, start the discussion, and then argue for the conclusion you believe in.
- A formal presentation is a barrier; ending with "Any questions?" does not make it a conversation. Business comes down to relationships, and informal presentation with honest debate strengthens them and gets better results. Jobs treated most meetings as brainstorming sessions in which even finished work was dissected.

### Briefings

- Apple's product briefings, by product managers or by Jobs, were brief and interactive: no marketing treatises or attribute lists, just a small group in a casual setting, a tour of the product, its benefits and every question answered. Jobs briefed with no slide show, no leave-behind and no formal strategy, only a conversation about why the product existed, how it worked and what set it apart, with the whiteboard when needed: "Steve acted as his own slide show." Then he trusted the agency to work out how to present it to the world.
- An Apple briefing leaves you with a document of one or two pages. "Simplicity likes to make a point quickly." The author's record for a big-company briefing was thirty-two people for a whole day, followed by focus groups, after which Dell ran older ads with a holiday graphic added. An overblown briefing is "checking off boxes to make sure every last fact is on the table. It serves the purpose of the presenters, but not the attendees." People leave for the bar, not eager to set the world on fire.
- Templates are not evil. Apple's briefing template is built on Simplicity: give the small group of smart people what they need to do the highest-quality work.

### The onstage exception

The only formal presentation Jobs believed in was a product launch, choreographed and rehearsed for days or weeks, with a backup plan for everything. Even then his slides were "straightforward to a fault," and he followed the traditional playbook: lay out the agenda, give the facts for each topic, summarize each topic before moving on, summarize the whole show at the end, and repeat any thought he wanted to stick, over and over. He did not present like a graduate of presentation class. He spoke "like a passionate twenty-year-old with the wisdom of a fifty-year-old," and came across as the guy in the conference room. He never asked the agency for a formal presentation, "just our best thinking."

### Deformalize

Choreographed meetings and formal presentations may transfer information, but "they neither inspire nor bring a team closer together." Internally and with clients day to day, converse rather than present. Many of the agency's most compelling words for Apple emerged in conversation during briefings. Jobs designed Pixar's building so people would run into each other in common spaces. Process makes you efficient, he said, but innovation comes from people meeting in hallways, calling each other at 10:30 at night, and "ad hoc meetings of six people" called by someone with a new idea. "Think big—but don't act that way."
