# Fighting for ideas: Think Skeptic, Think War

How a champion gets Simplicity through: weighing expert advice, probing "no," ignoring naysayers, choosing enemies, bringing overwhelming force, recovering from mistakes, and spreading the religion of Simplicity through an organization.

## Think Skeptic

### Take advice, not orders

An Apple newspaper ad claiming the new Power Macs beat the fastest Intel PCs had been approved by the agency's lawyers and by Jobs when, three days before it ran, Apple's own lawyers raised four points they feared Intel might challenge. The author summarized the four points for Jobs and asked for guidance. The reply came within minutes, and its first sentence was "Fuck the lawyers." The ad ran unchanged; Intel got "a bit steamed," and nothing else happened.

- **Healthy skepticism.** Jobs's instinct was to doubt opinions that ran against his vision. The book calls it healthy because many expert opinions in business are "unnecessarily limiting": the expert has not given the situation enough thought, does not understand all the nuances, or is simply more cautious than you wish to be.
- **Counsel, not a court order.** The opposite failure is the leader who "blindly accepts the opinion of his or her legal department" and treats it "more as a court order than counsel." Jobs did not ignore his experts. He relied heavily on his lawyers to protect Apple's intellectual property and trademarks, but he weighed their advice "in context of other evidence, his larger goals for the company, and Common Sense," and he did not expect a lawyer to know the nuances of marketing. He vetoed conservative advice when he judged a degree of risk warranted.
- "A true friend of Simplicity will at least remain open to the possibility of rejecting an expert's advice after considering other factors, as opposed to reflexively implementing it."
- **Weigh the long term.** Over the iPhone name, Jobs set what he would gain by launching with that name, and the long-term value of an iPhone subbrand, against a likely lawsuit; most companies would have quietly picked another name. (The story is in [naming-and-words.md](naming-and-words.md).) Ask what your own company would decide in that spot. "It's about evaluating advice in context and having the strength to move ahead when the rewards outweigh the risks." The short-term cost, even a large one, "is often outweighed by the future benefit."
- **When the lawyers are right.** Skepticism cuts both ways. Jobs once arrived giddy with a plan to hide a golden certificate for the millionth iMac in one box, Willy Wonka style, and to greet the winner in top hat and tails. California law would have made it a sweepstakes requiring a "no purchase required" route, so the winner would most likely not have bought an iMac at all. Faced with that, he decided it wasn't worth it. A lawyer's skepticism can usefully derail a bad idea that is picking up steam.
- **Under the yoke of lawyers.** Intel's agency, under strain, expanded its small group (a favorite outside creative for the brainstorm, the agency CEO for approval) to "get this one right," and approved a campaign built on the single word "Yes" that the creative people found deflating. When an obscure German company claimed the idea, Intel paid it off and won the right to keep running the campaign, and then its lawyers, newly spooked that others might sue too, killed the campaign anyway, despite their own earlier due diligence. Intel took its lawyers' decision as "more of a ruling than a request," and nobody weighed the brand damage of switching campaigns twice in one year. "For Simplicity to gain the upper hand, there must be someone in the room willing to stick his neck out for it." Complexity got double credit: it gave birth to the campaign (too many people, too many approvers, political pressure) and killed it (attorneys who missed the bigger marketing picture).

### Probe every "no"

"There are always a thousand reasons why something can't be done—only a few of which can't be circumvented with creative thinking." When a colleague or vendor says no, they usually mean it would take too much effort, it isn't how things are ordinarily done, it would cost too much, or some other excuse. "Sometimes the impossible is truly impossible. Other times it is merely improbable." Take a long, hard look before you throw in the towel. Jobs accepted no only if you could prove "beyond a shadow of a doubt that there was an immovable object in the way."

- **The *Time* insert.** Jobs wanted a sixteen-page iMac insert in *Time* in two weeks. The agency's print producer laughed: inserts needed weeks of preparation. The author passed that on as fact. Jobs: "That's bullshit." *Time* produced a magazine of about sixty pages every week with a few days' prep. Then: "If you guys can't do it, I'll bet Arnold can figure out how to do it pretty quick," naming the rival agency. Begging, threatening and crying got it done in two weeks. The author's mistake was relaying expert testimony instead of applying his own Common Sense as Jobs had, which cost him credibility about what was possible.
- **The vendor.** When a packaging vendor said a delicate foam part melting in his molds "wasn't a solvable problem" and the box had to be redesigned, Apple's designers refused to compromise an approved design and told him to redesign his machines (aluminum molds) instead. He did, at great expense. What "It can't be done" really meant was "it couldn't be done without extraordinary effort." "It's amazing what people can accomplish when they see a juicy account about to walk out the door."
- **Move on.** "If you can't get satisfaction with one person or vendor, move to another." Believe more in people's talent to work miracles than in those quick to answer no. Above-normal results come from asking people to go above and beyond. "If there are rules, chances are they can be broken. If you let someone off the hook, it's your idea that will be diminished in the end."
- **Refusing no is not enough.** Dell's consumer chief demanded an ultrathin laptop in half the year his engineers needed, brought in former Motorola engineers, and got the Adamo in six months, leaving one of his chief designers as the man who had said it couldn't be done. Then the product drowned in Complexity: a $2,500 laptop from a company known for $600 ones, a machine you had to hold to appreciate sold only online, fashion-magazine ads run in the business press. It sold in the thousands while MacBook Air sold in the millions, and was dropped. "He slammed the door on Complexity when he set the project in motion" but "left the key in it": Simplicity is all or none. "But they sure did design it fast."

### Ignore the naysayers

Jobs at Stanford: "Don't let the noise of other's opinions drown out your own inner voice. And most important, have the courage to follow your heart and intuition." Fighting toward a goal while the naysayers predict doom takes special strength.

The Apple Stores are Exhibit A. Jobs knew people were easy to convince once someone showed them a Mac, but there was nowhere to have that conversation. A 1997 "store within a store" at CompUSA put Apple in a back corner behind a maze of PCs, with salespeople who would rather sell an HP. Apple had zero retail experience, about 3 percent of the market, and the example of Gateway's failing stores before it. One analyst gave it "two years before they're turning out the lights on a very painful and expensive mistake"; a magazine ran "Sorry, Steve: Here's Why Apple Stores Won't Work." Apple built first-class stores anyway. Apple now has more than 350 of them, earning more per square foot than Tiffany and drawing more visitors a year than Disney's four biggest theme parks combined; as of mid-2011 they brought in over $3 billion a year, and more than half their visitors were new to the Mac. "Apple was skeptical of those who were skeptical of its chances." Nearly every idea meets opposition, inside or out: ignore the negativity, keep moving forward, and "go to extremes to ensure that those great ideas survive."

## Think War

"Simplicity being in a constant war with Complexity, there are times when it must act with appropriate belligerence." In 1998, with PCs holding 97 percent of the market and the public sure Macs were slow, Apple ran *Snail*, the fastest Intel chip riding on a snail's back, claiming the new Mac chip was twice as fast. "You know, we could really get sued over this," said Jobs. "And that might not be a bad thing." He pictured the magazine story with the snail on it and knew Intel would have to be careful about bullying a struggling Apple. *Burning Bunny* and *Steamroller* followed. Intel only posted rival benchmarks, and the debate itself put Macs back in the conversation: "mission accomplished."

### It's good to have enemies

- "Simplicity allows people to focus on one thing. Conversely, focusing on one thing helps achieve Simplicity." An enemy gives people that one thing (here, taking Macs seriously as an alternative to PCs).
- **Pick a proxy.** Intel powered every major PC maker and advertised itself as their engine, so attacking Intel attacked them all "without ever mentioning a single one by name." The claim seemed so unbelievable that it got attention. Later Microsoft served as the proxy again in *Mac vs. PC* (how to portray an enemy is in [offerings-and-messages.md](offerings-and-messages.md)).
- **Sometimes the enemy is you.** Returning in 1997, Jobs told employees: "We have to get it out of our heads that for us to win, Microsoft has to lose." "The battle for the desktop is over. And we lost." Apple's enemy was itself; it had to make great products and survive before fighting anyone.
- **Let the enemy volunteer.** Asked what he would do with Apple, Michael Dell said: "I'd shut it down and give the money back to the shareholders." A month later Jobs put Dell's face on screen under a bull's-eye: "We're coming after you, buddy." It fired up the troops.
- **Wars end.** When Apple moved the Mac to Intel chips, it removed one of the chief arguments against the Mac at a stroke, and could advertise innovation, design and Simplicity instead of fighting old perceptions: "a tremendous simplifier."
- "Having an enemy can be fun. And if done right—quite profitable."

### Use every available weapon

Jobs wanted Nelson Mandela, then president of South Africa, in *Think different* as a genuine tribute. Mandela's office said no. Jobs asked Bill Clinton to help. The answer was still no (perhaps one day, but only after he had been out of office at least six months), but "he hadn't held back. He had used every weapon in his arsenal to work toward his goal." "Never be embarrassed to acknowledge that you can't do it by yourself."

### Use overwhelming force

Move ideas forward leaving nothing to chance: err "on the side of overkill," use only the most potent weapons you have, and "think of it as a war (because it is)."

- "When you're dealing with the forces of Complexity, the last thing you want is an even fight. Decisive victories are far more compelling than narrow ones," and they put a stake in the ground for future struggles. "Never use a peashooter when you have access to a howitzer."
- **The weapons at hand.** Make key allies before a critical meeting, so you present "more of a chorus than a lone voice." Bring research or evidence that supports your view to suppress arguments. Go to the CEO for the nod of approval that breaks up a "process-inflicted logjam before it appears." Bring in a consultant for firepower and credibility.
- **Represent your own work all the way to the top**, Article 1 of any Bill of Rights for people who generate ideas. Anyone else presenting your work to a higher level rarely has your passion; facing opposition, they will throw in the towel sooner or accept compromises you never would. "It's human nature." So it must become your nature never to relent.
- **A tie goes to Complexity.** No small-arms fire: "There's no nicking the arm of Complexity—you need to blow it away." Both the idea and your standing in the company are at stake. Hold back and you risk failure, and the sleepless nights of wondering what heavy artillery would have done.
- **Know the layer of resistance.** Every new idea must break through one: naysayers who want the status quo, people who believe it can't be done, and people without the commitment to spend the effort.
- When an idea's life is on the line, extreme times call for extreme measures. You cannot afford a single hit, so hit first; pull out all the stops; grab the unfair advantage if you can; and never forget your most effective weapon, "the passion you feel for your idea."

### Simplicity: the ultimate weapon

The warlike attitude, the enemy and the overwhelming force all exist to let Simplicity shine, because "Simplicity itself is the greatest business weapon of our time." The original iPhone was a "perfect storm" of Simplicity: a device that was Simplicity embodied, from a company that had just proven itself with iPod and iTunes, landing in a category of customers who lived with their phones but did not love them. It went to war with deep-pocketed incumbents (phone makers and networks invested in the status quo), and "revolution" seemed a woefully inadequate word for what followed.

## Mistakes and the brand bank

Believing in Simplicity does not exempt anyone from bad ideas. Apple's cash reserves (over $80 billion in October 2011) gave it the freedom to make imaginative leaps and land safely; for everyone else, Simplicity is its own backup plan, making mistakes easier to recover from.

- **Admit quickly, move on.** The round "hockey puck" mouse that shipped with the first iMac pointed nowhere in particular and sent cursors astray. When the author told Jobs it was "an insult to the pro users," he deadpanned "Fuck 'em," then laughed: a better mouse was already in the works. The Power Mac G4 Cube, beautiful enough for the Museum of Modern Art, could not be priced for consumers, and Jobs killed it a year after unveiling it. His rule: "Sometimes when you innovate, you make mistakes. It is best to admit them quickly, and get on with improving your other innovations." "Simplicity doesn't like to get tangled up in old problems. It vastly prefers to look ahead," and customers appreciate the honesty.
- **The brand bank.** A brand works like a bank account. Good things (a hit product, a great campaign) are deposits; setbacks (an embarrassing mouse, an overpriced computer) are withdrawals. With a healthy balance, customers ride out the tough times; with a low one, they are tempted to cut and run. Through "Antennagate," the iPhone 4 antenna controversy, Apple's customers stayed loyal. "Having a high balance in the brand bank makes all the difference."

## Spreading the religion of Simplicity

People resist change, so changing attitudes is hard, but Jobs's legacy helps: you are citing not "some oddball theory espoused by an obscure management guru" but a tool wielded by one of the most successful people in business history. Show the benefits Apple gained from Simplicity (in the third quarter of 2011, its 4 percent of the smartphone market took over half the industry's profits, and its roughly 5 percent of PCs worldwide a greatly disproportionate share of PC profits) and draw the parallels to your own business, and you have a compelling case.

- Spread it project by project, person by person and group by group.
- Better, hold a group or companywide meeting on how the organization can use Simplicity. Rally the troops and ask them for ideas to simplify anything: standard office policies, how project teams are created and empowered, how products and services are created and evolved, how the company deals with clients.
- "Forcing people to follow new rules is always an uphill battle, but getting them to buy into a concept to the point where they start contributing their own ideas can literally create a movement within an organization." Simplicity is contagious, and it can become an obsession, as it did at Apple.
- You can hit selected parts of a business with the Simple Stick, but the real power comes when Simplicity is a companywide obsession and many parts of the company are guided by it at once. Jobs's greatest achievement was no single product; he "built a monument to Simplicity," and that monument is Apple itself. Doing the same takes "your understanding, commitment, and passion—though a certain degree of Jobs-style relentlessness will greatly assist."
