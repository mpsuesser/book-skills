# Insanely Simple

Ken Segall spent seventeen years in the worlds of Apple and NeXT as an agency creative director, over a decade of it working directly with Steve Jobs, and also led marketing work for Intel, Dell and IBM. His thesis: Apple does many things well, and Simplicity is "the common thread that ties them all together." Inside Apple, "Simplicity is a goal, a work style, and a measuring stick." The other companies showed an interest in Simplicity; only Apple made it an obsession. The book measures Apple against companies losing "everyday struggles against Complexity" (Intel, Dell, Microsoft, HP, and Apple itself under John Sculley).

The book is "about ideas and processes" and is meant for anyone who engages with clients, manages a team, works against deadlines or coordinates groups to solve thorny problems. Most of its cases come from marketing, product design and agency-client work, because at Apple marketing is the context for nearly everything. Apply it at that breadth, in the author's terms. The book capitalizes **Simplicity**, **Complexity**, **Brains** and **Common Sense** as forces; keep that usage.

## The core frame

- **Simplicity** is "the love child of two of the most powerful forces in business: Brains and Common Sense." It can be a choice, a feeling, a guiding light, a spirit: "you can tell pretty quickly when you're in a place that believes in it." It takes many forms (an idea, a finished product, the inspiration, the end result, or the process between them), so it is "a concept more than any one thing": a way of looking at every part of your job, your colleagues' jobs and the way the whole company operates.
- **Complexity** is "Simplicity's evil twin," its equal and opposite force, "powerful and seductive," and possibly the stronger of the two. It lives in every organization's hierarchy and goals and in most colleagues, and it lives inside everyone, Jobs included. Its recruits believe their complex ways are more ingenious: "Sometimes it's possible to show them the light. Other times all we can do is try to defang them." Neither force can die. "Complexity normally offers the easy way out"; "being complicated is easy. It's Simplicity that requires serious work."
- **The Undeniable Truth**: "People prefer Simplicity." Given the option, any sane person (indeed any living thing) chooses the simple path over the complicated one. "As Simplicity becomes more rare, it also becomes more valuable," and so does the skill of keeping things simple and protecting them from becoming complicated. Simplicity is "the weapon of the few"; believing in it is by definition "thinking different," and people who master it become greater assets to their organizations.
- **The Simple Stick**. To be hit with it is to have work rejected "not because it was bad but because in some way it failed to distill the idea to its essence. It took a turn when it should have traveled a straight line." Jobs's verdict on a team designing packaging for two versions of the same product: "Just combine them," he said. "One product, one box." It is sometimes held up as inspiration and sometimes "wielded like a caveman's club," and it is "a lot more fun to wield the Simple Stick than it is to get whacked by it." Swing it at work, projects, processes, product lines, meetings and messages alike.
- **Simplicity needs a champion.** It does not spring to life by itself. It needs someone "willing to stand up for its principles," strong enough to resist Complexity, guiding the process "with both head and heart." Simplicity "is a fragile thing" that must be carried unscathed through every process a project passes through.
- **All or none.** "Simplicity is not a smorgasbord from which you can pick and choose at whim. You buy the whole thing or you buy none of it." Companies that copy one small part of Apple's method rarely succeed: "If you can only muster up the energy to buy into part of it, you're just going to hurt yourself trying." It is no layer to graft on, has no prepackaged version and no on/off switch.
- **A skill, not just a goal.** "Simplicity is more than a goal—it's a skill"; you cannot just learn it, "you need to make it second nature." In the end, "Simplicity is not the goal—it's the guiding light that can help a business achieve its goals."
- **Simplicity is the ultimate efficiency.** Assuming the organization has the talent, its rules yield "lower costs, faster work, better work and, most important, more effective work." Intel's quest for efficiency made its marketing slower, costlier and worse.
- **The curse of Simplicity**: "sometimes it just seems too easy." To the casual viewer, or the manager reviewing it, a simple idea can look merely obvious, though it took night-and-day work. What looks effortless "requires a lot of hard work."
- **Quality counts.** "A simple idea is not necessarily a better idea." Start with something fresh and compelling and Simplicity takes it to great heights; "a bad idea remains bad no matter how you try to simplify it."
- **Peel more layers of the onion.** Jobs: the first solutions to a problem "are very complex, and most people stop there," but if you keep going and live with the problem you can often reach "very elegant and simple solutions." When Apple comes up with a solution, "it's more of a beginning than an end." The same holds for a presentation or a page: "I've yet to meet a paragraph that couldn't lose a few more words."

## Applying the book

1. **Locate the Complexity.** Read the situation through the ten elements below, decide which are in play, and run each one's tests. Use **brutalvision**: read the emails, plans, decks, product lists and org charts as they really are, and name what is muddy, crowded, slow, layered, hedged or bloated.
2. **Swing the Simple Stick.** For each element in play, state the straight-line version: one product, one box; one button; one message per ad; the smallest group of smart people with the decision maker in it; a name that is obvious. Then peel another layer.
3. **Check quality.** Confirm the simplified thing is also a fresh, strong idea. Simplicity sharpens a good idea; it cannot save a bad one.
4. **Champion it.** Plan how the simple version survives: who the ultimate decision maker is and how they get involved early, which "no"s to probe, which experts' advice to weigh in context, and what overwhelming force to bring (allies, evidence, a voice at the top, presenting your own work yourself). For this, read [fighting-for-ideas.md](fighting-for-ideas.md).

Done when every element in play has a named Complexity, a Simple Stick verdict and a quality check, and the plan names who will champion the result and how.

## The ten elements

**1. Think Brutal.** "Blunt is Simplicity. Meandering is Complexity." "Clarity propels an organization," and the book means pervasive, round-the-clock, "take-no-prisoners clarity." Say what needs to be said, demand the same from others, and nobody has to decode anything: "There is a huge difference between being brutally honest and simply being brutal." Standards do not bend: "there's no 'almost' when it comes to making things simpler." "Submit only the work you believe in 100 percent and approve only the work you believe in 100 percent." Settling plants "the seeds for disappointment, extra work, and more meetings" and leaves you "having to defend an idea you never believed in." Tell people where they stand, in real time. Depth (middlemen, report cards, the hard reset, taking a punch): [work-and-teams.md](work-and-teams.md).

**2. Think Small.** "Start with small groups of smart people—and keep them small. Every time the body count goes higher, you're simply inviting Complexity to take a seat at the table." There is "no such thing as a 'mercy invitation.'" The **Laws of Small**: the quality of a project's work "is inversely proportional to the number of people involved," and it "increases in direct proportion to the degree of involvement by the ultimate decision maker." Keep hierarchy flat and process minimal: "When process is king, ideas will never be." Big thinking, small everything else: zero committees, "the biggest start-up on the planet." Depth (meetings, the people-count fallacy, approvals, testing, hierarchy): [work-and-teams.md](work-and-teams.md).

**3. Think Minimal.** Focus "means saying no to the hundred other good ideas"; "Innovation is saying no to a thousand things." Cut product proliferation to a lineup where every product has a distinct, easily understood reason for being. Offer distinct models with obvious differences, then let customers customize. "A sea of choices is no choice at all." See customers simply as people and let them self-select. One point per message: "Don't bury your fact in facts." Minimize the processes ideas must travel through. "When in doubt, minimize." Depth (the four-quadrant grid, ease of purchase, single focus, the paper balls): [offerings-and-messages.md](offerings-and-messages.md); process minimization is in [work-and-teams.md](work-and-teams.md).

**4. Think Motion.** "The easiest way to screw up a project is to give it too much time." Leonard Bernstein: "To achieve great things, two things are needed; a plan, and not quite enough time." For technology work the author's perfect project time is about three months from briefing to finished work going live. **Aim realistically high** and **never stop moving**. "Simplicity is a big fan of context": urgency justifies blowing up a slow process. "A fractured, leaderless group without an urgent mandate is Simplicity-proof." Depth (the smart timeline, the 1.0 strategy, Apple vs. Dell, knowing your values): [work-and-teams.md](work-and-teams.md).

**5. Think Iconic.** "A single iconic image can be the most powerful form of communication." A brand campaign should "shine a light on the spirit that was already there," and a perfect theme line is one you could attach to the company "at any point in its history." Make the products personify the brand, so one campaign does both jobs. One button, because "Three is more than one." Find icons for an argument, including your enemy. Find "a conceptual image that actually captures the essence of your idea," which is not the same as decorating a slide deck. Depth: [offerings-and-messages.md](offerings-and-messages.md).

**6. Think Phrasal.** "Product naming is the ultimate exercise in Simplicity": capture in a word or two the essence of a product or company, or create a personality for it. The same applies to titling a report or theming a conference. Names should be obvious, consistent across the family, singular, and sound good. Words matter one at a time: "For Steve, there was no such thing as an unimportant detail." "More words are not more powerful." Depth (the iMac naming process, criteria, failure modes, copy): [naming-and-words.md](naming-and-words.md).

**7. Think Casual.** Shun the trappings of big business. It drove Jobs "batty to see in twenty slides what could be spoken in three sentences"; a slick presentation made him suspect you were "fluffing up the few facts you really had." What he wanted was "a table, a whiteboard, and an honest exchange of ideas." "Simplicity is in a hurry," and it "gains power through brevity." Converse with people rather than present to them: **deformalize**. Depth (presenting to an executive, briefings, onstage exceptions): [work-and-teams.md](work-and-teams.md).

**8. Think Human.** "The most powerful form of Simplicity is that which connects directly to our humanity." Speak **human-speak** ("1,000 songs in your pocket," not specifications). Humanity in a message comes from *tone*, "the combined effect of the words and images it chooses to use," not from babies and puppies. Look past the numbers: "the intangibles are every bit as real as the metrics." A product concept must be quick (the customer gets it in a second), usable at once, and an order of magnitude better or a new category. Compete hard without arrogance. Depth (numbers vs. humans, authenticity, the ecosystem of Simplicity): [offerings-and-messages.md](offerings-and-messages.md).

**9. Think Skeptic.** Take advice, not orders. Weigh expert counsel (lawyers above all) "in context of other evidence, his larger goals for the company, and Common Sense," and stay open to rejecting it rather than "reflexively implementing it." "Expect the first reaction of others to be negative." Probe every "no": "Sometimes the impossible is truly impossible. Other times it is merely improbable." Ignore naysayers and "go to extremes to ensure that those great ideas survive." Depth: [fighting-for-ideas.md](fighting-for-ideas.md).

**10. Think War.** "Simplicity being in a constant war with Complexity, there are times when it must act with appropriate belligerence." An enemy focuses people on one thing. "Use every available weapon," and use **overwhelming force**: "the last thing you want is an even fight," because "a tie goes to Complexity." Represent your own work all the way to the top, and remember your most effective weapon, "the passion you feel for your idea." Depth, including recovering from mistakes, the **brand bank** and spreading the religion of Simplicity: [fighting-for-ideas.md](fighting-for-ideas.md).

Jobs, closing the book: "Simple can be harder than complex. You have to work hard to get your thinking clean to make it simple. But it's worth it in the end, because once you get there, you can move mountains."
