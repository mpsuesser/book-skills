# Offerings and messages: Think Minimal, Iconic, Human

How Simplicity shapes what you offer and what you say: product lines, choices and targets, the single message, iconic images, brand campaigns, tone, the human voice, and the whole customer experience.

## Think Minimal

### Saying no

Apple "removes the extraneous" and focuses on one thing. Jobs at the 1997 Worldwide Developers Conference: focus "means saying no to the hundred other good ideas that there are. You have to pick carefully. I'm actually as proud of the things we haven't done as the things we have done. Innovation is saying no to a thousand things." Apple resists making new products "simply because it can."

Even Jobs was tempted. Before Mac OS X he seriously weighed a free, advertising-supported edition of Mac OS 9: a sixty-second commercial at every start-up and contextual ads through the system (an ink ad in the print dialog when ink ran low). The numbers worked, and the room nodded. He scrapped it, and the clean environment of the Mac survived. Expect a good-looking idea that violates the product's essence to win a room; Simplicity has to win the decision.

### The four-quadrant grid

In 1998 Apple's line was a confusing spread of models (Quadra, Performa, Macintosh LC, PowerBook, Power Macintosh), the product of trying to please everyone. Supporting them splintered resources from R&D to marketing, and worst of all "the sheer number of choices was creating confusion for Apple's customers and employees alike." Jobs replaced it with a square of four quadrants: laptops and desktops, for consumers and for pros. The one chart was both an example of Simplicity and a corporate road map. Every employee could see where the company was going; it inspired the engineers, set customers' expectations, and saved millions spent supporting models. Later the line "expanded," not "become more complicated": every product "has a distinct reason for being that is easily understood by its customers," and every one is first quality. "No fillers."

- Jobs's advice to Nike's CEO: you make beautiful products, "But you also make a lot of crap. Just get rid of the crappy stuff and focus on the good stuff."
- Fewer, higher-profile products plus no compromise on quality gave Apple greater efficiency and a premium price.
- Minimizing "can be a tremendously powerful tool—but often requires great audacity": the determination to act even if it upsets a number of people. The author's fantasy of Michael Dell cutting fifty desktop and laptop models to six (focusing the company, cutting operating expenses by at least a third while sales rose, and aiming "to make history" rather than to be "relevant") illustrates the move and its scale. "Minimizing is about being both smart and clear."
- Most readers need to minimize processes, not product lines; see the process section in [work-and-teams.md](work-and-teams.md).

### Product proliferation and the sea of choices

**Product proliferation**: companies that "can't stop themselves from responding to every opportunity, trying to please every customer and close every sale," when a logical lineup would serve them better. "Trying to please everyone is a good way to please no one." Apple builds its following on the products it *chooses* to make, makes premium products only, and cedes the low end to anyone who wants it. In November 2011 Apple's site offered two laptop models (MacBook Air and MacBook Pro); HP offered more than twenty-three and Dell eighteen, with overlapping features spread across pages.

- **Ease of purchase.** "Complexity loves nothing more than a sea of choices." It stretches decision time from minutes to hours and sends buyers to an expert friend or buying guides. Apple's method: "offer distinct models with obvious differences (ultrathin and ultralight versus full-featured) and then allow customers to customize their choice to taste."
- **Overdose, not choice.** Customers do want choice; "it's the overdose that becomes damaging." Past a point choice becomes a liability: it torpedoes confident decisions and causes postpurchase angst over whether one bought the right thing. Apple customers "see less confusion," not less choice; Apple led every PC maker in the American Customer Satisfaction Index for eight consecutive years up to 2011.
- **Mystery-shop it.** Ask the seller's own reps to explain the differences ("I want a thin and light laptop, but I'm confused by the many different models"). Dell's reps were pleasant, clarified nothing, and different reps recommended different machines.
- **The buy button.** "The faster and simpler you can make the buying experience, the more business you'll do"; minimize the obstacles between customer and "buy" button. A neutral buying experience is not enough: "Thriving in a competitive world isn't about achieving a joyous neutrality." Positive impressions are what people share.
- **Subtraction attracts.** Critics said the iPad lacked features; rival tablets added the ports and card slots it was "missing," and did not sell. "It was the subtractions made by Apple during the design stage that customers found more appealing." The second-generation iMac replaced three plastic pieces meeting at each lower corner with one jointless piece, an improvement no customer would notice, and Jobs described it like a passage of the Sistine Chapel. The law: "Simplicity attracts."

### Pricing and editions

Mac OS X Leopard came in one version for $129 that ran on every Mac with every feature. Vista came in four versions from $200 to $400; Windows 7 in three, from $200 to $300, against $30 for Lion. Minimizing choices gives customers "a simpler path, a better value, and a happier frame of mind," and earns "a more honest and trusting relationship." Confusing choices at excessive prices make customers feel squeezed for every dollar. Microsoft's Zune Store showed the same failure of Common Sense: instead of a fixed price per song, customers bought "Microsoft Points" by the hundred and converted them at eighty points to the dollar to buy a ninety-nine-cent song. Fix such schemes at the whiteboard stage, when it is cheap. "When in doubt, minimize." For the editions trap (Final Cut Studio, one box, one product), see [naming-and-words.md](naming-and-words.md).

### Simplifying the target

PC makers splinter their marketing across small business, big business, education, government and every kind of consumer, making many models and little profit on each. Apple "simply sees its customers as people," looking not for a great home computer or a great business computer but "a great computer, period." One marketing message serves all and gives customers credit for the intelligence to **self-select**: some home users want the Pro machine, some business users the entry model. Dell's separate Consumer and Commercial divisions, each with its own management, goals and products, sold laptops with nearly identical specifications: "They compete with themselves." Many Dell managers admired Apple's approach, but the company had "developed an immunity to Simplicity." Consolidating products and audiences "requires an organization that's willing to take a long, hard look at itself," and "it never happens by itself. Simplicity needs a champion."

The iPad continued this. Apple opened people's eyes to the possibilities, showed apps for business and pleasure side by side, and let each owner self-select a use, ignoring the conventional warning that games turn off business users. RIM positioned its PlayBook for business with the headline "Amateur hour is over," then advertised games, movies and social sites too, so the message became all business except when it was fun and games; the first PlayBook could not even do email without a tethered BlackBerry. In its first full quarter RIM shipped 200,000 PlayBooks; Apple sold 9.25 million iPads, more than RIM's quarter in under two days. "Simplicity calls for a clear strategy, followed by consistent messaging." "Simplicity has universal appeal."

### Don't bury your fact in facts

"Give them one idea and they nod their heads. Give them five and they simply scratch their heads." Minimizing is the key to making a point stick, and it "may also be the most violated principle in marketing or any other business."

- **The paper balls.** Jobs wanted four or five points in a thirty-second iMac spot. Lee Clow crumpled five sheets of paper, tossed one ball to Jobs, who caught it ("That's a good ad"), then threw all five, and Jobs caught none ("That's a bad ad"). "The more things you ask people to focus on, the fewer they'll remember." Pick the most compelling feature and present it in the most compelling way. Clow won the simpler ad.
- **Minimize from the start.** An Intel spot ended up with four feature titles through the ad and four wrap-up screens at the end, because every stakeholder needed to see a certain message or logo. "Simplicity can't just swoop in like Han Solo and save the day at the eleventh hour. Your message needs to be minimized from the start."
- **Multiple messages.** If you must deliver several, "find a common theme that unites them all and push hard on that idea." Every message beyond one splinters the attention of customers and colleagues alike.
- **One focus a quarter.** Apple had a large ad budget and had been "saying a hundred things quietly instead of one thing loudly." Jobs insisted on a single focus every quarter, featured on the apple.com home page and in nearly all advertising. "By saying fewer things in more important places," Apple got more notice than IBM with its vastly larger budget.

### The website and the URL

- Jobs saw apple.com as a place to get information about Apple's products, period: interesting and attractive, but "a glorified catalog," with "edgier" ideas regularly rejected. Whizzy animations were "lazy," eye candy instead of substance. Deep sections of the site carry a lot of content, but "the pages are designed so they *feel* simple."
- He squelched an ad ending on a specialized URL ("www.apple.com/specialoffer"). Ordinary people do not remember such URLs; interested viewers go to apple.com, and if an ad is worth running, the home page will feature the link. He refused to make people work harder so the company could track their movements: "Apple was there to serve them, not the other way around."

## Think Iconic

"A single iconic image can be the most powerful form of communication." Jobs thought this way in conversation too, explaining new technology through iconic people and periods (the Industrial Revolution, Shakespeare, Henry Ford). Over time a conceptual image gives people an easy way to identify your company, idea or product, and memorable images often communicate better than words. "There's a big difference between finding a great image and decorating a PowerPoint presentation." Find "a conceptual image that actually captures the essence of your idea. Be simple and be strong."

### The *Think different* campaign

- **The brief.** Jobs promised that great products were coming in about six months; the agency's job was to project a positive image and reintroduce the spirit of Apple in the meantime, then let the products become "the physical manifestation of Apple's brand." Three audiences: people who remembered the great Apple of old, a young generation who had known only the anemic one, and, every bit as important, Apple's own employees, who needed inspiration.
- **Shine a light on what is there.** Some agencies treat a brand campaign as a chance to invent a new personality. The task here was "to simply shine a light on the spirit that was already there." The ads had to be authentic, and the big idea "couldn't be complicated." Apple "wasn't for everybody; it was for those who valued creative thinking."
- **The theme-line test.** "The perfect theme line is one that you could attach to the company at any point in its history": "Think different" (by art director Craig Tanimoto) would have fit the garage as easily as the present. It also became a battle cry for employees.
- **You are who you admire.** "You can tell a lot about someone by the people he or she admires." Celebrating world-changers (Einstein and Jim Henson, Hitchcock and the Dalai Lama, Dylan and Martin Luther King Jr.) told the world what Apple was without using more words than "Think different." For each hero the team hunted for "the one truly iconic shot," the image that conjures up the person's life or accomplishments. The ads were posters paying tribute: one black-and-white portrait bleeding off the page, the logo and two words in a corner. They registered "in a nanosecond."
- **Marketing is about values.** Jobs, introducing the campaign to his marketing team: "This is a very complicated world, it's a very noisy world, and we're not going to get a chance to get people to remember much about us... And so we have to be really clear on what we want them to know about us." Not speeds and feeds, MIPS and megahertz, or why Apple beat Windows. The models were "Got milk?", which sold milk by focusing on the absence of the price, and Nike, which sells a commodity and honors great athletes and great athletics. Apple's core value: "people with passion can change the world for the better." Markets change; "core values—those things shouldn't change."
- **Invest in the crisis.** A company facing extinction usually cuts marketing, because marketing needs a leap of faith. Jobs said Apple could not withdraw into a shell; "The company had to invest in itself."
- **Place it where it shines.** Monica Karo's media plan was as simple as the images. The ads ran only where they would get maximum notice and "never in places that would degrade the message": back covers only, never inside pages; the most visible billboard sites locked up near airports and city approaches; backlit bus shelters; whole sides of buildings. Nothing was tested beforehand.

### Simplicity's unfair advantage: one campaign

Brand campaigns are a hard sell, like persuading a nineteen-year-old to start a retirement account, and agencies that promise to build the brand and sell product hard at once rarely manage it. Apple achieved "the holy grail of advertising": "a line of products that are the personification of its brand." After six months of "Think different," iMac paid it off; the agency could almost have run a photograph of the translucent Bondi blue iMac under the headline. Each ad after that relied on one striking product visual with an "Apple-clever" headline, product ads that were also brand ads. Apple stopped running the hero ads, had "only one campaign," and made every product sold a deposit in the brand. Following that first investment, Apple never needed a separate brand ad again.

### One button

"One is the simplest number ever invented... The further away you get from one, the more complicated things get." Jobs rejected many iPhone designs to arrive at a single button. Smartphones of the day had buttons everywhere, and learning them took effort; "Simplicity requires little effort." The button signals Simplicity before you touch the phone, and it always takes you back home. Three buttons for the three main uses (Internet, phone, iPod) would arguably have been faster and still better than rival phones, but "Three is more than one," and the result would have been "almost perfect." Jobs "never dealt in 'almosts.'" Apple weighs every message a design sends, down to the subconscious ones: how it looks, how it feels, "how it makes *you* feel," so the product wins you over before you touch it.

When a team brought Jobs mock-ups for a DVD-burning app (later iDVD), he ignored them and drew on the whiteboard: one window; drag your video into it; click the button that says "Burn." "That's it. That's what we're going to make." With voice control, Apple heads toward zero buttons: "zero is the only number that's simpler than one."

### Iconifying products

At key moments Apple creates an image that becomes the icon of a product, giving it a personality that is harder to forget.

- **The failure.** The first iPod ad, mocked online as "iClod," showed a young man trying to act cool while dancing out of his apartment. It layered artificial amusement on the product that was not amusing, and it featured a "real person," and someone cool to one viewer is a turnoff to another. Because Apple tests nothing, the clunker ran. That cost little: the next campaign was "fantastically better."
- **The icon.** Susan Alinsangan's *Silhouettes*: dancers as flat silhouettes against intense colors, the iPod and its white earphones in white, no product photography at all. Instead of asking you to buy the device, Apple asked you "to buy the emotion": "incredibly human commercials, yet they never showed a human face." Backlit outdoors, they were visible for miles. iPod held over 70 percent of its market more than ten years after launch.
- **When the boss says no.** Jobs first rejected *Silhouettes* ("It's not Apple"): no product shot, no white space, unlike anything he had approved. "The only thing you could do was stick to your guns, keep the debate going," and let the idea work into his comfort zone. He came round and was proud of the spots. His favorite review comment was "You can do better."

### Iconifying the enemy

How you portray your "enemy" decides whether you look honest and good-natured or condescending and mean.

- Apple's old "The hard way"/"The easy way" spreads compared Windows and Mac logically and did not connect emotionally: two computers and a lot of words.
- *Mac vs. PC* turned the two platforms into people with personalities, a down-to-earth Mac and a PC projecting confidence against the evidence, whose exchanges drew out the differences with good humor. "The secret was in finding icons to represent the two sides of the argument instead of using real computers." It ran to sixty-six ads over four years, "high on the aggression meter and low on the arrogance meter"; each devastating blow landed as a friendly jab.
- For the Y2K scare (global estimates of the cost ran from $300 billion to $600 billion, while Macs were safe until the year 29,940), Apple used HAL from *2001: A Space Odyssey*, who had already lived through it: the whole ad was a slow move into HAL's red eye, "a perfect image for a complicated problem." Douglas Adams's line ran on the web: "We might not get everything right, but at least we knew the century was going to end."

## Think Human

"The most powerful form of Simplicity is that which connects directly to our humanity." When PCs made us think like machines, the Mac let us think like people. Apple's genius is seeing human potential others miss; sometimes, as with music players, the concept already exists and is "missing only one thing: Simplicity." Jobs on iPod: the key insight was to manage the music library in iTunes rather than on the device, where competitors had tried to do everything "and made it so complicated that it was useless." "It was technology that would make iPhone capable but Simplicity that would make iPhone lovable." The computer-phobic "knew how to use it before they ever picked it up." BlackBerry did one thing well, email anywhere, but nested its other capabilities so deep in menus that even experts rarely used them.

### The product test

Apple's executives review new ideas regularly and measure each against the principles of Simplicity and its ability to connect on a human level:

- **The concept has to be quick.** The customer gets it in a second: "phone, Internet, and iPod, all in one."
- **Instant use.** People can pick it up and start using it at once; the manual is superfluous.
- **An order of magnitude.** It must improve lives by an order of magnitude over what exists, or create a new category. Jobs at NeXT: in technology, "the only truly meaningful change is a 10x improvement over what came before." Hold the first iPhone beside the BlackBerry of its day.

An idea that fails any of these gets the Simple Stick, which "will either improve it or put it out of its misery."

### Human-speak

Apple did not describe the first iPod as a 6.5-ounce player with a five-gigabyte drive. It said "1,000 songs in your pocket." "Human-speak is a hallmark of Simplicity": put things in human terms and use the words people use in everyday conversation.

- It cannot be had by proclamation. Companies order a new ad campaign, write a manifesto, call meetings, hand out T-shirts, put up a new sign, "and then . . . nothing really changes." "Complexity is far too clever to allow a company to attain Simplicity through proclamation." The love of Simplicity has to be instilled in people and burned into products, and people have to be "rewarded for acts of bravery in support of Simplicity," or it comes and goes like the annual benefits meeting.
- **Consistency.** Apple's voice is an "overnight sensation" thirty years in the making. A company adopting Simplicity has to stay consistent with its new values long enough for them to become culture; otherwise Simplicity is "a suit that's only worn at special events." Clients who ask their agencies for something "more like Apple" are really asking for Simplicity, but most want a quick fix, not a meaningful change. "Simplicity is more about commitment than indulgence."
- **Simplicity resistant** companies have cultures and institutionalized processes that block it, or staff who manage only a surface commitment. They fall back on what is comfortable or "reserve a place at the table for Complexity's old friend, Compromise," and the new approach soon feels like the old one.
- Human-speak predates simple products. The 1977 Apple II brochure was headlined "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication." A famous early insert asked "Will someone please tell me exactly what a personal computer can do?" and opened on four pages listing a hundred things you could do with one, many illustrated. "Though the volume of information was large, the concept was simple," and because it spoke people's language it succeeded.

### Tone: babies and puppies not required

When clients ask for more humanity, they usually mean pictures of people. But humanity and humans can stand apart. "What determines the humanity in a company's messages is *tone*—which is the combined effect of the words and images it chooses to use." Telling a colleague you cried at your daughter's school play needs no photograph; the telling carries the emotion. A mom with a baby on her lap might have made Apple's messages feel staged, manipulative and less authentic. Apple "doesn't really *try* to be anything. It simply acts like itself—which is one of Simplicity's guiding lights."

- When products like iPhoto and iMovie demanded real people, the fear was casting one person who would turn off another segment when the target was "everyone." The answer was a planned range of people across the campaign over time: young families, old couples, proud dads.
- Human images are "only one means of expressing humanity." For long stretches Apple's humanity came from **"intelligent wit,"** in the voice Steve Hayden gave the early Macintosh ads, and from **realspeak**: nothing tricky or manipulative (whatever workout "magical" and "revolutionary" have had).
- Emotion needs a backstory. The agency shot *Crying Man*, a father moved to tears by a home movie he had made, and found in the edit that without setup he looked like a grieving dad mourning his daughter. A joyful home movie of kids jumping on a bed to Bob Dylan's "Forever Young" did the job instead.
- Apple feels authentic at every point of contact, from ad to website to purchase to the look and feel of the product, because its tone matches its core values, Simplicity first. "Simplicity requires that you have a set of core values that pervade everything you do—and everything you say." "Simplicity is what makes people feel like they know you, understand you, and ultimately trust you."

### Competing without arrogance

Some see arrogance in Apple: the *1984* ad cast IBM as Big Brother, the 1985 *Lemmings* ad showed prospective business customers walking off a cliff, and Jobs answered an iPhone 4 complaint with "Just avoid holding it in that way." Apple cares about the people who share its values, but its marketing still has to compete hard without feeding that perception. *Switchers* (2002 and 2003) got attention and little switching. *Mac vs. PC* (from 2006) managed sixty-six scathing attacks while staying lovable, thanks to wit. Jobs rejected script after script; the agency then shot real ads with the proposed actors over one weekend, and on the screen he loved them.

### Numbers vs. humans

- Dell ran on click metrics, with bonuses tied to them, and struggled to win hearts; Apple put delighting customers first and earned far higher profits. Jobs demanded all the information he could get and digested it, "but he took it all in context." He would never trade human connection for a few more clicks. He treated "slavish devotion to numbers as a big-company behavior" and decided "based on head and heart."
- Apple stands "at the intersection of technology and liberal arts." Jobs "recognized the difference between a fact and an emotion," and that "the intangible and the unprovable can be the most important parts of building a brand." He quoted Henry Ford ("If I asked people what they wanted, they'd say a faster horse") and said: "A lot of times, people don't know what they want until you show it to them."
- He did not consider validation from outsiders very valid. Apple fills a room with smart marketers like any company, and "like few companies, Apple is willing to let the final decision remain in that room." "Apple isn't interested in ideas that try to please everyone. Those are the ideas that end up stripped of their character, feeling calculated and worst of all—less human."
- Every target group is human beings, "and human beings respond to Simplicity." "Just be true to your species."

### The ecosystem of Simplicity

"If there is one focus at Apple that transcends all others, it's the customer experience": the same values and the same tone through TV ad, website, shopping, unboxing, everyday use, repair and support. In effect Apple has built "an ecosystem of Simplicity." A strong, loved brand is "like cash in the bank": people pay a premium for it, where a mediocre brand can attract customers only by cutting prices.

- **Details matter whether noticed or not.** Apple's packaging team works in a locked room, and the box is "hardly 'just a box'": it gives the feeling of quality from the first touch. When a vendor said a delicate piece of foam in a box could not be molded, Apple told him to rebuild his molds in aluminum instead of redesigning the box. He did, at great expense, for a part probably no customer ever noticed. The Apple Store works the same way: shoppers sense thoughtful design without picking out specifics.
- **The store as the brand.** The Apple Stores were built first-class (top architects, materials from a quarry in Italy, steel from Tokyo, glass from Germany for the Fifth Avenue store) as "a physical representation of its brand": quality, design and Simplicity. They filled the one link in the customer experience Apple had not controlled, the shopping. For the fight to build them, see [fighting-for-ideas.md](fighting-for-ideas.md).
