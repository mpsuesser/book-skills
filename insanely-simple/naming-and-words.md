# Naming and words: Think Phrasal

How Simplicity works at its most concentrated: product names, and then every word of the copy.

"Of all the things in this world that cry out for Simplicity, product naming probably contains the most glaring examples of right and wrong": "iPhone" on one side, "Casio G'zOne Commando" and the "Sony DVP SR200P/B" on the other. The lessons apply to anyone who communicates: how you title a report, how you theme a conference. "In the end, it's all about getting people's attention and making sure they get the feeling you want them to get."

**Product naming is the ultimate exercise in Simplicity.** "It requires one to capture in a single word, possibly two, the essence of a product or company—or in some cases create a personality for it." You have a few characters to say something you could write an essay about, and the name will give the product its identity and personality and help build the brand. Complexity enjoys this challenge too, and mostly wins it.

## How iMac got its name

The new consumer computer (code-named C1) was on a fast track to production, so the name had to be settled quickly for manufacturing and packaging. Jobs already liked "MacMan," Phil Schiller's suggestion, and challenged the agency to beat it.

1. **Get the brief, including the traps.** Jobs's requirements: you have to know it's a Mac, so the name needs "Mac" in it; and it is the easiest way onto the Internet. His two warnings: it is a full-powered Mac that some will mistake for a toy, so the name must not sound frivolous; and its handle might suggest a portable, so the name must not sound portable. Check every candidate, including the client's favorite, against the brief. "MacMan" failed both traps (Pac-Man, Walkman). It also echoed Sony at a company that stood for originality, and carried an obvious gender bias.
2. **Show something better.** "Once they've fallen in love with something you don't like, the only way to really move them off of it is to show them something better." Arguing against the favorite comes after you have better names in hand.
3. **Generate, trim, present.** Work through a long list, trim it to five favorites, and give each its own board: the name in big type plus a short bullet list of its virtues.
4. **Make the case for the favorite.** "iMac" solved every problem at once. It was clearly a Mac; the *i* said Internet (and could also mean individual or imagination); it was succinct, one letter added to "Mac"; it sounded neither toylike nor portable. It was also the first Apple product name to use the colloquial "Mac," and it laid a foundation for future "i" products.
5. **Survive the first no.** Jobs: "I hate them all. 'MacMan' is better." One more week.
6. **Re-present the favorite with new work.** The rule from a wise man in advertising: "As long as you've got new ideas to share, you are free to re-present the old one." Present the new names first, then bring the favorite back. Jobs: "Well, I don't hate it this week... But I still don't love it."
7. **Let the decision maker live with it.** The next day Jobs was having "iMac" silk-screened onto a model and asking people what they thought. He took it and ran.

Jobs held very strong opinions, the kind that "might knock you over and kick you a few times," but he would change his mind "if confronted with heartfelt opinions presented with passion." That single letter became one of the most important parts of the Apple brand.

## What makes a name work

- **Common Sense, not spectacle.** Apple does not aim for the spectacular. "iPhone" did not make people jump up and down, but it made sense: the *i* marked it as Apple, and "phone" named the category it set out to revolutionize. "Simple and natural names stick with people, while jargon and model numbers do not."
- **The joy of obvious.** The obviousness of "iPhone" was its appeal: there could be no mistaking how the product was positioned or which industry Apple was about to upend, and instant understanding feeds the buzz. It was even an oversimplification, a name for a third of a device that was phone, Internet browser and iPod in one. Jobs knew the product would soon be known for all its characteristics and that buzz and marketing would make the name stand for far more than its word. Beware the curse of Simplicity here too: to a reviewing manager, the obvious name can look like no work at all.
- **Consistency across the family.** Apple's computers are "all Macs, all the time": iMac, Mac Pro, MacBook Air, MacBook Pro. The *i* marks consumer devices and is always attached to a word that describes the product or category. "Every time you say the name of an Apple product, you know it's an Apple product." Dell's names (Inspiron, Vostro, XPS, OptiPlex, Precision) come from unrelated worlds, bear no relation to one another or to Dell, and each has to fight its way into the customer's vocabulary. Phone makers' names ("Enlighten," "Breakout," "Astound," "Curve," "Citrus," "Fascinate") tie back to their brands about as well as random dictionary words.
- **Simplicity is Singularity.** "Human beings are naturally programmed to identify products by single words." People say "I'll look it up on my iPhone," never "my Apple iPhone," and rarely "my iPhone 3GS." Every year's model keeps the same name; modifiers such as 3GS, 4 and 4S exist "for your information only," because "iPhone" is the brand. Mac lines keep their names however radically the design changes, even though that complicates record keeping behind the scenes; Apple does the extra work to keep it simple for customers. Where models truly differ in use, give them descriptive single words for size or purpose, as with iPod touch, nano and shuffle, with "no arcane number-and-letter schemes." Owners of most rival phones likely just say "my phone."
- **Own the name.** "Droid" is an excellent name (short, memorable, futuristic, familiar) but it builds no single maker's brand, because Lucasfilm owns it and rents it to Motorola and HTC. Phone makers also have to invent unique model names for each carrier, a cycle only a category revolution like iPhone could break.
- **Sound good inside the framework.** Jobs required names to fit the logic of Apple's existing naming framework and to sound good. Even "iMovie" was debated for weeks.
- **Autobiographical and visionary.** Jobs first wanted to call his second company "Two." His designer friend Tom Suiter objected that everybody would ask what happened to the first, and came back with "Next," which was at once autobiographical (Jobs's next adventure) and visionary (the next great computer).
- **Simple naming serves the customer.** Apple keeps naming simple "so it doesn't confuse the hell out of people." With perfect clarity a name tells customers "who you are and what you sell."
- A great name does not guarantee a hit, but it tilts the odds, and a bad name can hobble a product before it reaches the shelves.

## How naming goes wrong

- **Approval by attrition.** Complexity lives in the naming process. Names must pass multiple stakeholders and legal review, and many go out "simply because time is up and 'this is the best we can get approved.'" At Apple, "every name needs to be perfect."
- **Cool and legally safe.** Jobs kept naming in-house (Apple's product and marketing teams and its agency), sure that nobody knew Apple's products, plans and culture better, and wary of paying an outside firm hundreds of thousands of dollars for names like "Quadra" and "Performa," which "reek of traditional corporate values, trying to be simultaneously cool and legally safe."
- **Cart before horse.** Intel had its agency build a marketing plan for a new mobile platform with a blank space where the name and logo would go, while the naming debate ran on for months. Common Sense puts the name first.
- **Abandoning a family.** Pentium was a synthesized name that came to mean something through ubiquity and heavy investment. Its "-ium" could have spawned a family of names, like elements of a periodic table, which fitted a company that makes the vital ingredient inside computers. Intel already had "Mobilium," a name with Logical Sense ("a close relative of Common Sense"). It became a code name, probably over a trademark issue, and an outside naming firm produced "Centrino."
- **Giving up a name too easily.** Cisco already sold an "iPhone." Jobs, seeing enormous value in the name, launched without signing the agreement Cisco had sent the day before and settled later; the name's value now dwarfs the legal cost. "Apple is a rarity in that it is more than willing to push things to the brink to get the name it believes will deliver the most marketing clout." See [fighting-for-ideas.md](fighting-for-ideas.md) on weighing legal advice.
- **The thesaurus wall.** After walls of candidates it is easy to believe the world's supply of good names is exhausted, about as likely as musicians running out of songs. "The great name is always there, waiting to be discovered."

## Praying to false gods

"Thou shalt not lust after thy neighbor's marketing." Clients pin competitors' ads on the wall and wish them their own, though the competitor's circumstances and values differ. Be inspired, but "keep your eyes on the road and stay true to your own company's values. It's about authenticity."

Apple acquired a Hollywood color-grading tool that had sold for $25,000 a copy and named it "Color." A project leader who admired Adobe's tiered editions planned regular and premium editions of Final Cut Studio 2, and asked for two package designs and a name for the premium one. The candidates were names like "Extended Edition" and "Platinum Edition," with the air of Adobe or Microsoft about them. The author, applying the obviousness principle, offered "Final Cut Studio 2, With Color." Jobs looked at the mocked-up boxes and said: "Put the software in the box... We sell one product. Period." The leader explained she had promised the CFO a special edition so the cost of the acquisition could be tracked and justified. "Looks like you made a big mistake." One box meant no website explanations of the differences, no second package to design, produce and stock, and no customers agonizing over versions, just excitement about a big new component. "Simpler is way, way better." Her quest had been "inspired by a competitor's values—and not by Apple's rules of Simplicity."

## Every word

- Jobs went over copy page by page and sentence by sentence, often late at night: an hour or more on a sixteen-page iMac insert, a comment or question every sentence or two, sometimes a debate over "a" versus "the." His main concern "was always what the casual reader would take away from it." The debates were never heated. When they could not agree they horse-traded: "Well, you let me have the last one, so I'll let you have this one." He argued over single letters not to control but because "there was no such thing as an unimportant detail."
- **More words are not more powerful.** In internal business and with customers, "dissertations don't necessarily prove smarts. In fact, they tend to drive people away." Intelligent-sounding words do not make you look smarter; expressing an idea "simply and with perfect clarity" does. Communicate as people naturally do: "In simple sentences. Using simple words. Simplicity is its own form of cleverness—saying a great deal by saying little."
- apple.com is "a primer for intelligence in communications": much of its feeling of smarts comes from brevity and straightforwardness. "In a world where too many people are trying too hard, Simplicity can be extremely refreshing."
- Keep trimming. Every paragraph can lose a few more words, even one trimmed many times before.
