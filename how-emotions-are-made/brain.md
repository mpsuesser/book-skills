# How the brain constructs emotion

Use this when explaining the mechanism in depth. Barrett asks *how* an emotion is made, never *where* it is: "each instance of emotion is a whole-brain state." If instances of emotion are like cookies, the brain is a kitchen stocked with common ingredients (flour, water, sugar, salt): the **core systems**. They are multipurpose and combine, roughly as recipes do, into diverse instances. Fear and anger can be made from similar ingredients, as cookies and bread both contain flour. An instance has **emergent properties**, like bread's "crustiness", which no ingredient has: "You cannot reverse-engineer a recipe for an instance of fear from a feeling of fear."

## Prediction, not reaction

- **Against the stimulus-response view.** The brain does not wait to react to the world. **Intrinsic brain activity** runs continuously, like breathing. It is the origin of dreams, daydreams and mind wandering, and of every sensation. **Intrinsic networks** are groups of neurons that consistently fire together, like the members of a sports team.
- **Wiring favors prediction.** There are ten times as many connections running from visual cortex back to the thalamus as forward, and about 90% of the connections into primary visual cortex carry predictions. World events merely *tune* the predictions, like stones skipping on an ocean wave. A reactive brain would be too inefficient and too expensive metabolically.
- **Predictions explain input.** A perfect prediction leaves the input carrying no new information. Motor predictions are launched before awareness, so the "illusion of free will" is a misnomer: "You *are* your brain."
- **The brain as scientist.** Barrett describes the balance of prediction against input as five scientist modes:
  - a *responsible scientist* changes predictions to fit the evidence
  - a *biased scientist* selects the data that fit
  - an *unscrupulous scientist* ignores the data
  - a *curious scientist* focuses on the input
  - an *armchair experiment* is pure simulation, as in imagining or dreaming
  
  "Your brain is wired for delusion."
- **The forest snake: three cases.**
  1. A snake appears and you predicted it: a match.
  2. No snake is there, but you see one: affective realism.
  3. No snake is there and you don't see one, but your interoceptive predictions persist, so you are slow to calm down. Body-budgeting regions are "mostly deaf".
  
  Their sluggish correction explains why you should "Wait 20 minutes before you take a second helping." It also explains why marathon runners feel fatigue early and keep running.
- **Imagining spends the budget.** Imagining perturbs the body budget, and people spend at least half their waking hours simulating. Pictures change body budgets even when the viewer is motionless.

## The interoceptive network

- **Two parts.** The **body-budgeting regions** (also called limbic or visceromotor regions) send predictions that control the internal environment. **Primary interoceptive cortex** (the posterior insula) represents the sensations. The network encompasses the salience network and the default mode network.
- **They lead.** Body-budgeting regions issue predictions but receive none from other cortical regions. They are the most powerful feedback system in the brain, so "what you feel alters your sight and hearing."
- **Degeneracy keeps affect.** Roger, whose body-budgeting circuitry was destroyed by illness, still has affect. People with spinal cord damage or pure autonomic failure still have affect, from uncorrected predictions.
- **Affect can be moved.** Helen Mayberg's deep brain stimulation of this network gave immediate relief to patients with treatment-resistant depression.
- **Precision is not the goal.** The brain is not built to experience interoception precisely. You feel it in general terms (pleasure, displeasure, arousal, calmness), and sometimes, intensely, as emotion. Interoception evolved to regulate the body budget, not for feeling.
- **Prediction includes the body.** A visual prediction answers: "What did I see last time I was in this situation *when my body was in this state*?" Body-budgeting regions are also hubs, which is why no decision is free of affect.

## Concepts in the brain

- **Similarities are separated from differences.** The cortex compresses sensory details into efficient multisensory summaries, as video compression does. Lines become "Angle," then "Eye," then "Face"; summaries such as "Mother" and "Self" are multisensory.
- **Concepts are built on the fly.** At three, Sophia saw a stranger with dreadlocks in a mall and cried "Uncle KEVIN!" Her brain launched 174 predictions from the three people with dreadlocks she knew, and that population was the concept "People with Dreadlocks". No grouping was stored anywhere; the concept was constructed ad hoc, and the instance most similar to the situation, by pattern matching, won.
- **The concept cascade.** A prediction *originates* in the default mode network, part of the interoceptive network, as a summary that represents the concept's goal. It unpacks into ever more detailed predictions, checked at each stage, down to the primary sensory regions. Prediction errors flow the other way. Learning concepts runs the same circuitry bottom-up; applying them runs top-down. The whole cascade is the instance. Four consequences:
  1. Emotions feel triggered because simulation starts before categorization is complete.
  2. Every construction includes the body's state.
  3. Granular concepts cost less: the Korean *jeong* (attachment to a close friend) predicts more precisely and efficiently than "Pleasant Feeling".
  4. A concept is a population of predictions fitting a goal.
- **"The remembered present"** is Gerald Edelman's phrase for this. An instance is "an anticipatory guess about how you should act in the present moment and what your sensations mean." It is probabilistic, never 100 to 0.
- **Infants.** Infants build concepts by **statistical learning**, computing the probabilities of sensations that occur together, even in the womb. An infant's brain is awash in prediction error, with a diffuse "lantern" of attention. An adult's attention is a "spotlight".
- **Concepts are not things.** They are not stored anywhere, and the default mode network holds no fixed set of neurons per goal. Reading "hammer" fires motor neurons for the hand, and reading "Apple, Tomato, Strawberry" fires color neurons. "Really you have a conceptual system." "Concepts have no existence separate from the process that creates them."

## The control network and regulation

- **Choosing winners.** The **control network** helps select among predictions and shifts the lantern toward a spotlight. Take chest pain after an argument: heart attack, indigestion, anxiety, or a partner being unreasonable? The network selects between emotion and non-emotion concepts, between emotion concepts, between goals (escape or attack) and between instances (scream or not).
- **It optimizes.** It is "an optimizer", not a central authority, working like a car-racing team.
- **"Regulation is just categorization."** Feeling your rational side temper your emotional side is constructing an instance of the concept "Emotion Regulation".
- **Hubs.** The interoceptive and control networks hold the brain's major hubs, its JFK airports. Hub damage is associated with depression, panic disorder, schizophrenia, autism, dyslexia, chronic pain, dementia, Parkinson's and ADHD.
- **One process, many names.** Experience, perception, conceptualization, pattern completion, perceptual inference, memory, simulation, attention, morality and mental inference all name the same process. "To make meaning is to go beyond the information given." "Categorization bestows new functions on biological signals."

## Volition

- **Reflexes are the involuntary part.** Withdrawing from a knee tap, a flame or a puff of air runs on direct sensory-motor wiring, as in a sea anemone.
- **Association neurons make choice.** They sit between sensory and motor neurons and can stimulate *or inhibit* a response, "the biological basis of choice". The control network is made of association neurons and is always engaged.
- **Control is an experience.** "Your experience of being in control is just that—an experience." There is "no scientific justification for labeling a 'moment without awareness of control' as emotion."

## Complexity and degeneracy

- **Sources of variation**: microwiring, neurotransmitters, plasticity, degeneracy and multipurpose circuitry. Together they make the brain a **complex system**. **Complexity** is a metric for a structure that efficiently creates and transmits information, making many new patterns by combining pieces of old ones. The classical view's brain of single-function blobs would be low in complexity.
- **Payoff.** High complexity and degeneracy carry more information and are robust and resistant to injury, as AM and Roger show. "Complexity, not rationality, makes it possible for you to be an architect of your experience."
- **Preset concepts are few.** Perhaps only valence, arousal, loudness and softness, brightness and darkness. Variation is constrained by efficiency and speed, by the outside world, and by the human dilemma of **getting along versus getting ahead**.
- **Many minds.** "The human brain evolved, in the context of human cultures, to create *more than one kind of mind*." "Your mind is a computational moment within your constantly predicting brain."
- **Barrett's own caveat.** Prediction, interoception and categorization are scientists' concepts too: the best current way to describe what neurons compute, and open to revision as tools improve.
