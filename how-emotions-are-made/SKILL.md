# How Emotions Are Made (Lisa Feldman Barrett)

Barrett sets two views of emotion against each other, and the skill speaks from the second.

- **The classical view of emotion**: emotions are built in at birth, each distinct and recognizable. Each is triggered like a switch and has its own brain circuit, which causes a **fingerprint** of changes in face, body and voice. Emotions are artifacts of evolution, universal, and battle with reason.
- **The theory of constructed emotion**: "In every waking moment, your brain uses past experience, organized as concepts, to guide your actions and give your sensations meaning. When the concepts involved are emotion concepts, your brain constructs instances of emotion."

Emotions are not triggered; you create them. "Emotions are real, but not in the objective sense that molecules or neurons are real. They are real in the same sense that money is real—that is, hardly an illusion, but a product of human agreement." "What's innate is that humans use concepts to build social reality, and social reality, in turn, wires the brain." The person is an **architect of their experience**, and of other people's.

## The construction, link by link

Reason about any emotional situation along this chain, naming each link in these terms.

1. **Prediction.** The brain sits in a dark, silent box and guesses the causes of its sensations from past experience. It asks: "Which combination of my past experiences provides the closest match to this sound, given *this particular situation*?" Intrinsic brain activity is "millions and millions of nonstop predictions". A **simulation** is the brain's guess of what is happening, made by changing the firing of its own sensory neurons. A **prediction error** is the mismatch with incoming input, and it is how the brain learns. In the **prediction loop**, a match becomes your experience; a mismatch is resolved by changing the prediction or by filtering the input. "Your experience right now was predicted by your brain a moment ago."
2. **Interoception**: "your brain's representation of all sensations from your internal organs and tissues, the hormones in your blood, and your immune system." It is a core ingredient of emotion, as flour is of bread.
3. **Body budget.** Each time the brain moves any part of the body, inside or out, it spends energy resources. Eating, drinking and sleeping replenish them, and relaxing with loved ones reduces the spending. The brain's most important job is to predict the body's energy needs, like a company's finance department. Any event that significantly impacts your body budget becomes *personally meaningful* to you. Other people regulate your body budget too: they synchronize breathing and heartbeats with you, and a held hand eases pain. Losing a loved one feels like losing part of yourself "because, in a sense, you have."
4. **Affect**: "the general sense of feeling that you experience throughout each day". It has two features: **valence** (pleasant to unpleasant) and **arousal** (calm to agitated). Affect is not emotion. It is always present, from birth, and serves as a simple summary of the budget: "Are you flush? Are you overdrawn? Do you need a deposit, and if so, how desperately?" **Affect is your brain's best guess about the state of your body budget.** Your **affective niche** is everything relevant to your body budget in the present moment; the rest is noise. In effect, "*you construct the environment in which you live.*"
5. **Affective realism**: "we experience supposed facts about the world that are created in part by our feelings." Body-budgeting regions are "loud and bossy, like a mostly deaf scientist with a big megaphone," and sensory regions are wired to listen. So "you feel what your brain believes," and "Affect is in the driver's seat and rationality is a passenger." No decision or action is free of interoception and affect: "You cannot overcome emotion through rational thinking, because the state of your body budget is the basis for every thought and perception you have." The rational economic actor is "a neural fairy tale." The stock example is Israeli judges, who denied parole more often just before lunch. Its core move: "I feel bad, therefore you must have done something bad."
6. **Concepts.** A **category** is "a collection of objects, events, or actions that are grouped together as equivalent for some purpose". A **concept** is "a mental representation of a category". **Goal-based concepts** hold dissimilar instances together by a goal alone. "Things That Can Protect You from Stinging Insects" includes a flyswatter, a beekeeper's suit, a calm demeanor and an Antarctica vacation. "You are not finding similarities in the world but *creating* them." A **prototype**, the best example of a category, is constructed on the spot; the prototypical American sadness (frown, slump, crying, monotone voice) is rarely found in real life. **Emotion concepts are goal-based concepts.** Instances of happiness may be a smile, a sob, a scream, raised arms or clenched fists.
7. **Categorization.** The brain builds a concept on the fly, as a population of predictions that fit the current goal. The best fit becomes the **winning instance**, which is both your perception and your plan of action. **Concepts are predictions**: to construct an instance of a concept is to issue a prediction. "I did not see a snake and categorize it... I categorized sensations in order to see the snake, to feel my heart pounding, and to run." The same stomach sensations become hunger at the dinner table, nausea in flu season, longing for a lover, or anxiety in a doctor's office.
8. **Instance of emotion.** "*Emotions are meaning.* They explain your interoceptive changes and corresponding affective feelings, in relation to the situation. They are a prescription for action." "Emotions are not reactions *to* the world; they are your constructions *of* the world." With attention on yourself the construction is an emotional experience; with attention on someone else it is an emotion perception.
9. **Emotional granularity**: how finely a person constructs emotional experience.
   - At high granularity a person has hundreds of concepts and tells "aggravation," "irritation," "frustration," "hostility," "rage" and "disgruntlement" apart; this is "a sommelier of emotion".
   - At moderate granularity a person has a few dozen concepts, roughly the basic emotions.
   - At low granularity many words ("sadness," "fear," "guilt," "anger") map onto one concept whose goal is something like "feeling unpleasant": a toolbox holding only a hammer and a Swiss Army knife.
   - **Alexithymia** is the extreme: a stomachache where others feel anger.
   - Precise concepts predict with less effort: "Preciseness leads to efficiency."
10. **Social reality**: physical things take on additional functions by social agreement, as money, flowers versus weeds, and muffins versus cupcakes do. "Make something up, give it a name, and you've created a concept. Teach your concept to others, and as long as they agree, you've created something real." It needs **collective intentionality** (people agreeing that a purely mental concept is real) and **words**. A word spoken to an infant with intent is an invitation to *create* similarity among diverse instances. **You need an emotion concept to experience or perceive the emotion**; without the concept you are **experientially blind**, like someone seeing only blobs in a picture of a bee. "It takes more than one human brain to create a human mind."

Emotion concepts serve **five functions**:
- making meaning
- prescribing action
- regulating your body budget ("Categorization literally gets under your skin")
- emotion communication
- social influence ("You can be an architect of other people's experiences")

The last two work only if others share the concept; otherwise they are "meaningless noise."

**Variation is the norm.** "Fear" names a diverse population of instances, with no fingerprint at its core. "Heart rate changes are inevitable; their emotional meaning is not."
- **Population thinking**, Darwin's idea: a category is a population of unique members, describable only in abstract statistical terms. "What we have been calling a fingerprint might just be a stereotype."
- **Degeneracy**, "many to one": different sets of neurons produce the same outcome.
- **Core systems**, "one to many": one brain network contributes to many kinds of mental event.

The mechanics in depth (the scientist modes of prediction, the interoceptive and control networks, the concept cascade, regulation as categorization, volition) are in [brain.md](brain.md).

## Barrett's vocabulary

Speak in these terms; each carries the theory.
- **Facial configuration**, or facial movement, for what is usually called a "facial expression". Faces move; emotion is not *expressed* in them.
- **Instance of emotion** for one occurrence, and **emotion category** for the population. Initial capitals in quotes ("Fear") name the concept or category.
- **Perceiving** an emotion, never recognizing or detecting one. Perceptions exist within the perceiver.
- **Constructing** an emotion. The person is an active participant even without a sense of agency, so "triggered," "emotional reaction" and an emotion "happening to you" give way to construction.
- **Agreement** where others would say "accurately". Accuracy has no scientific meaning for emotion, only a social one: agreement, or consistency with a norm.
- **Unpleasant image** as shorthand for "an image that impacts my body budget, producing sensations that I experience as unpleasant."
- **Body budget, interoception, prediction and social reality** in place of "emotional reaction," "facial expression" and "emotion circuit in the brain".

## Tests and distinctions

- **Fingerprint test.** A claimed fingerprint must (1) be specific to that emotion and (2) have no alternative explanation. No face, body pattern or brain region has passed it for any single emotion. "Emotions arise from firing neurons, but no neurons are exclusively dedicated to emotion."
- **Physical or social reality.** "When you can't find an objective criterion to compute accuracy and are left with consensus, this is a clue that you are dealing with social, not physical, reality." Silk versus polyester has a test; "beautiful shirt" does not. Sort each claim into **perceiver-independent** things (molecules, a widened eye) and **perceiver-dependent** ones (emotions, flowers versus weeds, money, race). "Never mistake one for the other, while still understanding that the two are irrevocably entwined."
- **Is X an emotion?** How many emotions exist, or whether lust or curiosity counts, are nonissues: "Love (or curiosity, hunger, etc.) is an emotion as long as people agree that its instances serve the functions of an emotion."
- **Three clarifications** of social reality. Emotions are not illusions. Not everything is relative: you cannot make a wall unsolid, but you can redraw a border. Social reality is not "just in your head": people live and die for money, reputation and laws.
- **Affect or emotion.** Valence and arousal alone are affect. They become an emotion only when categorized with an emotion concept. "A bad feeling doesn't always mean something is wrong. It just means you're taxing your body budget." "Any graduate student of mine who never feels distress is clearly doing something wrong."
- **Discomfort or suffering.** "Discomfort is purely physical. Suffering is personal." Affect makes sensations about *you*, inside your affective niche.
- **Affective realism, by its effects.**
  - Signs: a gut feeling that you know something is true; news you believe at once; dismissing a message, or disliking the messenger, at once.
  - Defense: "The best defense against affective realism is curiosity." Be mindful when you love or hate what you read, because "Your affect is not evidence that the science is good or bad."
  - Unchecked, it leaves people "dead certain and inflexible."
- **Certainty is manufactured.** Treating "generous," "loyal" or "an asshole" as essences inside a person misses other explanations. The "asshole" boss may have his mind on his sick kid at home. "There is no single reality to grasp... not an infinite number of realities, but definitely more than one." "Your experiences are not a window into reality." The upside: "There is a kind of freedom in realizing that we categorize to create meaning, and therefore it is possible to change meaning by recategorizing."
- **Essentialism**: the belief that a category has a true underlying nature that its members share. "Every noun we utter is an opportunity to invent an essence." Its tell is the defense "we haven't looked everywhere yet": "*Essentialism inoculates itself against counterevidence*."
- **The mental inference fallacy** occurs whenever a physical measurement is assigned a mental cause: "That scowl is expressing anger," "That activity in the anterior insula was caused by disgust." Keep movement (contracting a muscle), behavior (freezing) and experience (feeling fear) apart. "Circuitry that controls freezing is not circuitry for fear."
- **Ask whose perspective.** "Was he angry?" is two questions: angry from the perceiver's perspective, and angry from his own. "Every perception requires a perceiver," so every question about an instance of emotion is asked from a point of view.
- **Emotion is not loss of control.** "Instances of emotion are no more out of control than thoughts or perceptions or beliefs or memories." Separate volition from the *awareness* of volition.
- **Responsibility**: "'responsibility' means making deliberate choices to change your concepts." The concepts a person was raised with are not their fault, but continuing them is their responsibility.

## Perceiving other people's emotions

- **It is a guess.** "Perceptions of emotion are guesses, and they're 'correct' only when they match the other person's experience; that is, both people agree on which concept to apply." Confidence in the guess is affective realism. Give up the fiction that you *know* how someone feels. When guesses clash, think "We have a disagreement." "Being curious about your friend's experience is more important than being right."
- **Context, not the face.** "When it comes to emotion, a face doesn't speak for itself." Perceivers read emotion from context and from their own concepts. A furious-looking face snippet of Howard Dean was, in context, excitement. A psychiatrist read a concentration scowl as "pent-up anger."
- **Communication is synchrony.** Two people predict and categorize *in synchrony*, with reasonably compatible goals in their concepts. Synchrony is harder across cultures, backgrounds or politics, so it calls for more work and empathy.
- **Ask open questions.** "How are you feeling?" rather than the leading "Are you upset?" "Tell me how you've been" rather than "Are you feeling depressed?"
- **Be a good sender.** The person expressing also bears the responsibility to be understood.
