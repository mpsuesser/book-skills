# Do animals feel emotion?

The theory of constructed emotion asks whether an animal has the **three ingredients** for making emotion:
1. **Interoception**: the neural equipment to create interoceptive sensations and experience them as affect.
2. **Emotion concepts**: purely mental concepts like "Fear" and "Happiness", used to predict and categorize sensations.
3. **Social reality**: sharing emotion concepts so they pass to the next generation.

## The evidence, rung by rung

- **Interoception and affect: yes for mammals.** Macaques have an interoceptive network structured for prediction. Their autonomic responses to videos of other monkeys mirror humans' (the Eliza Bliss-Moreau studies). Dogs wag more to the right for pleasant events and to the left for unpleasant ones. "I think it's best to assume all animals can experience affect." Opioids exist in fish, nematodes, snails, shrimps, crabs and some insects. An animal belongs in our moral circle "if there is *any possibility at all* that it can feel pain."
- **Affective niche: smaller in most animals.** A majestic mountain does not move a macaque's body budget: "Simply put, more things matter to us." Toys enter a macaque's affective niche only with a reward. Human caregivers share attention, looking from object to infant and back while naming it ("lamp"); other primates do not. The human brain is about five times a macaque's and three times a chimp's, with more control-network connectivity to compress prediction error into purely mental concepts.
- **Concepts: definitely.** Animals categorize by smell, and sheep recognize faces. Baboons tell "B" from "3", and macaques can distinguish paintings by Monet, van Gogh and Dalí. Dogs need a thousand or more trials to learn dog-versus-other-animal photos, where infants need a few dozen.
- **Goal-based concepts: no firm evidence.**
  - Chimps cannot imagine a flying leopard, take another point of view, imagine a different future, or realize that goal-based information resides in other minds.
  - The test: would a chimp see that running up stairs, climbing a ladder and crawling up a rock face share the goal "To Climb"?
  - Apes learn words for a reward. The only concept they map to a symbol without one is "Food": "To apes, words alone are not worth learning."
  - Chimps saved tokens to trade for food without understanding "Money".
  - "Emotion concepts are *only* goal-based," so chimps very likely cannot learn "Happiness" or "Anger".
  - Bonobos are the possible exception: Kanzi picked up symbols on his own. So are the chimps of Tetsuro Matsuzawa's troupe, raised in trusting relationships with humans: their infants learned concepts on their mothers' laps without reward, as well as human infants did.
- **Social reality: uniquely human.** Some macaques spread a food-washing practice, but that is limited collective intentionality. "Social reality is a human superpower."
- **Dogs.**
  - Dogs are better than chimps at perceiving human gestures and following human gaze, and Border Collies understood miniature replicas as symbols for toys to fetch.
  - In the guilt study, owners were told, truthfully or not, that their dog had eaten a forbidden biscuit. Dogs that were scolded showed "guilty" behaviors whether or not they had disobeyed. The owners were perceiving guilt.
  - The jealousy study did not control for the owners' behavior.
  - A shared dog "Anger" is "vanishingly remote."
  - What looks like dominance is anxiety: a body budget out of balance in an animal bred to depend on us and left alone all day. We regulate an affiliative creature's body budget, then abandon it for most of every day.
  - Social animals such as dogs and elephants may have some concept of death, and a grief rooted in attachment and body budgeting: "love *is* a drug."

Affect alone accomplishes a great deal. A rat helps a rat in distress, and one infant comforts another, because the other's discomfort taxes its own body budget. Barrett avoids "empathy" for this, because English uses one word both for simple affective synchrony and for a mental concept. "Non-human animals feel affect, but the reality of their emotion is, for the moment, only within ourselves."

## The two-perspective answer

"Is a growling dog angry?" is "the wrong question to ask," or at least an incomplete one. Split it:
- **Angry from the perceiver's perspective?** Yes, if the perceiver constructed a perception of anger. Accuracy here is consensus: do we agree? Do we agree with the owner, who knows the dog best? Do we match the social norms?
- **Angry from the dog's own perspective?** Almost certainly not, for lack of the concept. The dog feels distress, pleasure and other affect. Rowdy jumped on a boy days after losing his "sister" Sadie, from a body budget thrown off by loss, perhaps made worse by his owner's grief.

## The mental inference fallacy in animal science

- **"Fear learning"** is classical (Pavlovian) conditioning: a tone paired with a shock makes a rat freeze, with amygdala circuitry involved. "Freezing is a behavior, whereas fear is a much more complex mental state." In a larger enclosure rats run, and when cornered they attack; restrained, their heart rate *drops*. "You can't study fear by shocking rats unless at the outset you have defined 'fear' circularly as 'the freezing response of a shocked rat.'"
- **Baby rats' "distress" cries** come from rats that are *cold*: the sound is a byproduct of regulating body temperature.
- **A rodent's anterior cingulate** is active when a neighbor is in pain. Calling that empathy goes beyond the evidence; more simply, the animals influence each other's body budgets.
- **Survival circuits.** Joseph LeDoux revised his own view: the circuit controls freezing, a **survival circuit**, not fear. Survival circuits for the "four F's" (fighting, fleeing, feeding and mating) cause bodily changes experienced as affect. "For emotion, you also need emotion concepts for categorization."
- **The rule**: "If a scientist labels a behavior like freezing using a mental state word like 'fear,' you should think, 'Aha, the mental inference fallacy!'" It is the same move as viewers granting emotions to Heider and Simmel's moving triangles. Mental inference grows stronger the more an animal resembles us: joy is easier to see in a scampering dog than in a scampering cockroach.

Mental inference toward animals is normal and may protect them; "When we have our scientist hats on, however, we must resist the lure of mental inference." Seeing animals through our own identity harms them. Anxiously attached dogs get punished as "too dominant". Baby chimps are taken from mothers who would nurse them for five years. Understand animal minds on their own terms, not as inferior human minds.
