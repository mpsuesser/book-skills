# Mastering your emotions

You cannot snap your fingers and change how you feel right now. You can change what your brain will predict next, and so shape your *future* experiences "in a very real, predicting-brain way". The **recipe for living** has two major ingredients: **the body budget and concepts**. Work both, the body budget first.

## 1. Keep the body budget in good shape

This is the most basic step. Without it, "you're going to feel crappy no matter what self-help tips you follow. It's just a matter of which flavor of crap." Modern culture is engineered to wreck body budgets:
- pseudo-food, refined sugar and bad fats
- over 40% of Americans aged 13 to 64 sleep-deprived
- advertisers, social rejection, and the ambiguity of social media
- cell phones and late-night screens

"Social reality transmutes into physical reality." A chronically miscalibrated budget is hard to fix, because the loudmouthed body-budgeting regions are slow to respond to correction.

As a baby, your caretakers ran your budget; now it is yours. Barrett's practices:
- **Eat** healthfully: greens, and go easy on refined sugars, bad fats and caffeine.
- **Exercise** vigorously and regularly.
- **Sleep** plenty. There is "no substitute, biologically speaking."
- **Touch**: massage and human touch. Massage limits inflammation after exercise.
- **Yoga**: people calm down faster, and it reduces proinflammatory cytokines.
- **Surroundings**: less noise and crowding, more greenery and natural light. Even a houseplant helps, and such surroundings help psychiatric patients recover.
- **Absorption**: a compelling novel or film engages the default mode network and keeps you from ruminating. A good cry helps too.
- **Giving**: regular lunch dates, taking turns treating, bring giving and gratitude together.
- **Other**: a pet, walks in a park or garden, hobbies such as knitting and cross-stitch.
- **Breathing**: slow, deep breathing calms the body through the parasympathetic nervous system. Quick, short breaths have the opposite effect.

Early warning: when you feel bad, check the budget before the story. "When you feel bad, treat yourself like you have a virus, rather than assuming that your unpleasant feelings mean something personal. Your feelings might just be noise. You might just need some sleep."

## 2. Build emotion concepts (granularity)

**Emotional intelligence**, redefined: "getting your brain to construct the most useful instance of the most useful emotion concept in a given situation. (And also when *not* to construct emotions but instances of some other concept.)" It is not detecting emotion "accurately". A person with only "Feeling Awesome" and "Feeling Crappy" has low emotional intelligence. Higher granularity splits Awesome into happy, content, thrilled, relaxed, joyful, hopeful, inspired, prideful, adoring, grateful and blissful. It finds "fifty shades of 'Crappy'": angry, aggravated, alarmed, spiteful, grumpy, remorseful, gloomy, mortified, uneasy, dread-ridden, resentful, afraid, envious, woeful, melancholy.

The chain to explain: "Words seed your concepts, concepts drive your predictions, predictions regulate your body budget, and your body budget determines how you feel."

Ways to grow concepts:
1. **Collect experiences.** Trips (even a walk in the woods), books, movies and unfamiliar foods. "Be a collector of experiences. Try on new perspectives the way you try on new clothing." Barrett's husband reframed her irritating "wishful recycling" as a "Superpower", making a habit he found irritating into an amusing foible.
2. **Learn new words.** Read outside your comfort zone. "Don't be satisfied with 'happy'": try "ecstatic," "blissful," "inspired"; "discouraged" or "dejected" rather than "sad". Borrow words from other languages, such as *gezellig* or *enohi* (see [cultures.md](cultures.md)).
3. **Invent concepts by conceptual combination**: combine existing concepts into a new one. Examples from Jeffrey Eugenides's *Middlesex*: "the hatred of mirrors that begins in middle age," "the disappointment of sleeping with one's fantasy," "the excitement of getting a room with a minibar." Try naming the feeling of driving away from your hometown, never to return. Name the concept and teach it to others, so it becomes social reality. Barrett invented "Chiplessness" for discovering that the chip you just ate was the last in the bag: disappointed that the bag is empty, relieved you won't eat more, slightly guilty you ate it all, and yet hungry for another chip. Named and taught, it becomes as real an emotion concept as "Happiness".
4. **Practice categorizing: ask *what* you feel, in fine grain.** A teenage son comes to dinner disheveled. Are you concerned his teachers won't take him seriously? Disgusted? Nervous that it reflects on you? Irritated about the money? Sad that your little boy grew up? Therapists and coaches help people "reframe situations, that is, find the most useful categorization in the service of action."

Why it pays: in the spider study, people with arachnophobia described the spider granularly: "an ugly spider and it is disgusting, nerve-wracking, and yet, intriguing". They became less anxious and approached spiders, and the effect lasted a week. This beat reappraisal ("little spider, and it's safe") and distraction. People with high granularity are 30% more flexible at regulating emotion, drink less when stressed and are less likely to retaliate aggressively. They also visit doctors less, use less medication and spend fewer days in hospital. Low granularity is associated with major depressive disorder, social anxiety, eating disorders, autism spectrum disorders and borderline personality disorder.

**Every experience is an investment.** Rumination adds instances that the brain will predict with again: "You don't want them to become paved roads. Every experience you construct is an investment, so invest wisely. Cultivate the experiences you want to construct again in the future." Track positive experiences daily and write about them. Constructing an unpleasant emotion on purpose is also emotional intelligence, as when football players cultivate anger before a game.

## 3. In the moment

1. **Move your body.** Dance or walk; movement changes predictions and lets the control network bring other concepts forward.
2. **Change location or situation.** During the Vietnam War, 15% of U.S. soldiers were addicted to heroin; back home, 95% of them stayed off it in their first year, where only 10% of users in the general population avoid relapse. The new location changed their predictions and lessened the craving. Barrett wonders whether a midlife crisis is an attempt to change predictions by changing context.
3. **Deconstruct, then recategorize.** "Deconstruct an affective feeling into its mere physical sensations." "Dissolve anxiety into a fast-beating heart." Then categorize those sensations as something more useful, such as anticipation, excitement or determination.
   - The test is whether you say "Oh no, I'm doomed!" or "I'm energized and ready to go!"
   - Chess grandmaster Joe Esposito's line: "Make your butterflies fly in formation."
   - Evidence: students score higher on math tests such as the GRE when they recategorize anxiety as merely a sign that the body is coping. Recategorizing anxiety as excitement improves public speaking and even karaoke, with fewer of the proinflammatory cytokines that lower performance. Remedial math students at community colleges raised their exam and course grades this way.
   - The Marine Corps saying "Pain is weakness leaving the body" is a recategorization. Stopping a workout at the first unpleasant feeling is categorizing that feeling as exhaustion.
   - With chronic pain, separating sensation from affect and from catastrophic thoughts leads to fewer opiates.
4. **Separate discomfort from suffering** (the distinction in SKILL.md). A virus is egalitarian, "nothing personal". The same sensations, once inside the affective niche, make the whole world seem awful. Ask whether you are irritated or just having caffeine withdrawal. Ambiguous sensations of the menstrual cycle or menopause can be categorized as having emotional meaning when they do not. Barrett once sobbed when her laptop died; her husband asked, "Are you premenstrual?" She lashed out at him, and three days later discovered he was right.

## 4. The self is a concept

The self is part of social reality. "You can't be a self by yourself." "The Self" is a goal-based concept whose goal shifts with context; the constant is the body. The default mode network is sometimes called the "self system". Buddhism's practice of **deconstructing the self** answers its reification: treating the self as a fixed thing.

To shrink your **affective niche**, notice that many worries (finances, a lost promotion, a teacher's opinion, a lover) concern "Reputation," "Power," "Wealth". Ask: "Are you really in jeopardy here? Or is this so-called injury merely threatening the social reality of your self?" Then recategorize the feelings as physical sensations, and they "dissolve like an antacid tablet in water". "Not About Me" takes an event out of your affective niche. Pride is social reality too: "Celebrate your achievements but don't let them become golden handcuffs."

- **Mindfulness meditation** observes sensations non-judgmentally, as close as an adult gets to a newborn's experience. The next best thing is to recategorize sensations as physical. You cannot turn concepts off. Meditators show larger, better-connected interoceptive and control network regions, though findings are inconsistent.
- **Awe** is "the feeling of being in the presence of something vastly greater than yourself": crickets at a beach house, a weed pushing through the sidewalk, ocean waves, stars, storm clouds. Take comfort in your insignificance. People who report feeling awe more frequently have the lowest levels of inflammatory cytokines, though cause and effect is unproven.

## 5. Raising children's emotion concepts

Children learn emotion concepts from the words adults speak to them, with intent, inside their affective niche. A toddler learns "Anger" because parents say "Sophie, sweetie, are you angry?" across diverse situations; the word invites the search for what they share. Teaching a child emotion words is *creating reality* with them.
- **Talk about emotions early**, even to infants. Look them in the eye and widen your eyes. "See that little boy? He is crying. He is feeling pain from falling down and scraping his knee. He is sad and probably wants a hug from his parents."
- **Elaborate**: on storybook characters' feelings, the child's and your own. Use a wide variety of words and name causes and consequences, like a tour guide.
- **Avoid essentialist stereotypes.** A smile may mean happiness, embarrassment, anger or sadness. Admit uncertainty, and admit when you guess badly.
- **Hold full conversations** with turn-taking, not baby talk.
- **Externalize tantrums.** Barrett used the "Cranky Fairy" and the "Elmo Chair".
- **Give specific feedback that names the emotion**:
  - "Your whining is irritating me, so stop it. If you are having a problem, use your words."
  - Instead of "bad boy": "Stop hitting your sister; it hurts her and makes her feel sad. Tell her you are sorry."
  - Instead of "good girl": "You made a good choice not hitting your brother back."
  - Tone of voice matters.
- **Evidence**:
  - Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence: 20 to 30 minutes a week on emotion words brought better social behavior and academic performance.
  - By age four, children from higher-income families have seen or heard four million more words than low-income children.
  - Children in low-income families hear 125,000 more words of discouragement than praise by age four; children in higher-income families hear 560,000 more words of praise than discouragement.

## 6. Responsibility for your concepts

You slam the desk because your brain predicted anger from your concept "Anger" and past experience, slamming included; the control network can only choose among concepts you have. Are you responsible for your concepts? Not as a baby. As an adult you choose what you expose yourself to, so "'responsibility' means making deliberate choices to change your concepts." Nobody is at fault for inherited hatreds, as between Israelis and Palestinians, but each person bears responsibility for continuing them. Israelis trained to *recategorize* negative events, such as rocket attacks, as less negative became less angry and more supportive of conciliatory policies five months later. Responsibility extends to others: you shape their concepts in "a very real brain-wiring way." A therapist working with abused women saw them "victimized twice", their brains now modeling a hostile world. "Sometimes, responsibility means that you're the only one who can change things."

"If you change your current experiences today, you can change who you become tomorrow." The short version: "eat less junk food and learn lots of new words," and remember that perceptions of others are guesses, not facts.
