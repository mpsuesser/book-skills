# Emotion concepts across cultures

Our biology lets us create goal-based concepts, but "exactly which concepts may be a matter of cultural evolution." "The human brain is a cultural artifact": culture helps *wire* the brain. Emotion concepts pass to the next generation through brain development, language development and socialization. They serve as "instruments of culture" for the dilemma of getting along versus getting ahead. "No emotion concept is universal, but even if one were, universality itself does not automatically imply a perceiver-independent reality." Even universality does not imply innateness: think Coca-Cola.

## Words from other languages

Each word names a concept English lacks. Learning one raises granularity. A shared word also communicates: Barrett's lab understood her instantly when she said "I am feeling a bit of *schadenfreude*" about a rival's failure, where describing the feeling would have sounded petty. "One word efficiently communicated my emotional experience and made it socially acceptable."

- **Different divisions of the same ground.** Russian has two angers, German three and Mandarin five. Russian *serdit'sia* is anger at a person, and *zlit'sia* is anger at something abstract or political. English speakers learning Russian overuse *serdit'sia*: a cultural error, not a biological one. A Greek speaker who calls eating pie *enohi* sounds overly dramatic.
- **Concepts can be taught across cultures.** Barrett proposes teaching *liget*, with its social rules, to military cadets who feel pleasure in killing, to broaden their granularity and help unit cohesion and mental health. Westerners experience parts of *liget* (athletes, players of first-person shooter games) but not the whole package.
- **Foreign emotions are like foreign money.** Instances of *fago* "are not emotions . . . to *you*." "Emotion from Another Culture" is like "Currency from Another Culture."

## Cultures that carve emotion differently

- **Behavior rather than mind.** The Himba and Hadza focus on action rather than mental states. Some Japanese concepts do as well.
- **Transactions between people.** For the Ifaluk, an emotion is a transaction. Anger is two people in a script, a dance around a common goal; it does not "live" inside either one.
- **Interpersonal emotions.** Cultures with interpersonal concepts of emotion: Ifaluk, Balinese, Fula, Ilongot, Kaluli, Minangkabau, Pintupi, Samoans.
- **No unified "Emotion".** Tahitians, Gidjingali, Fante, Dagbani, Chewong and Himba have no single concept of "Emotion". The concept of "Emotion" is itself an invention of the seventeenth century; before it, scholars wrote of passions and sentiments. Anna Wierzbicka calls English a "conceptual prison."
- **Missing concepts.** The Utka Eskimos have no "Anger". The !Kung of the Kalahari have no "Fear".
- **No thinking/feeling split.** Balinese and Ilongot cultures, and to some extent cultures guided by Buddhist philosophy, draw no hard line between thinking and feeling.
- **Preferred arousal.** Younger Americans prefer pleasant, high-arousal feelings; middle-aged and older Americans, and people from Eastern cultures such as China and Japan, prefer pleasant, low-arousal ones.
- **Cultural rules.** A concept comes with rules for when it fits. In the US, fear fits a rollercoaster, a cancer screening and a gun; fear of leaving the house is agoraphobia.
- **Colors work the same way.** Russian drawings of the rainbow (радуга) have seven colors, splitting blue into light blue and dark blue. The Berinmo of Papua New Guinea see differently divided colors.

## Emotion acculturation

**Emotion acculturation** is learning a new culture's emotion concepts, which brings new predictions.
- **American positivity.** Batja Mesquita found Americans unnaturally happy; Barrett hugged her for an "excellent job!" Americans are excited, never merely curious or perplexed, and give "Certificates of Participation": a "culture of positivity."
- **Immigrants converge.** Situations that bring anger in Belgium bring guilt, shame and respect as well in Turkey, but Turkish immigrants' experiences look more "Belgian" the longer they live there.
- **It costs the body budget.** An immigrant's brain faces prediction error like an infant's, and acculturation taxes the body budget. Less acculturated immigrants report more physical illness.
- **Synchrony is harder across cultures.** Successful communication requires synchronized concepts.

## Social reality becomes physical reality

Culture is not a "gauzy, amorphous vapor." The social reality of skin color can lead to lower salaries and poorer nutrition. Those change children's brains: a child born into poverty gets poorer nutrition, which means a thinner prefrontal cortex, poorer school performance, less education, and back to poverty. In this cycle, "society's stereotypes about race, which are social reality, can *become the physical reality of brain wiring*." Barrett answers the claim that such stereotypes are statistically accurate: "the official welfare statistics are true because we, as a society, made them so." "We are a bunch of brains regulating each other's body budgets, building concepts and social reality together, and thereby helping to construct each other's minds and determine each other's outcomes."

**Three inevitabilities.** Barrett names three universal ingredients of every human mind: affective realism, concepts and social reality. *Which* concepts and *which* social reality are not inevitable; any set that keeps the body budget regulated will do. "Social reality is a superpower only if we know that we have it."

**Psychology's own categories.** Psychologists debate "will power," "tenacity" and "grit" as if each were distinct in nature, and keep a hundred concepts for "the self." This is social reality misread as physical: it is why psychology keeps rediscovering the same phenomena under new names.
