# Emotion and the law

The legal system sets a society's ultimate rules for emotion, and it embodies the classical view. Barrett writes about US law and calls her proposals those of a scientist, not a legal scholar. The law treats you as "an emotional beast enrobed in rational thought."

## Classical assumptions in the law, and what replaces them

- **"Hijacked by anger."** The *heat-of-passion* defense and "extreme emotional disturbance" make rational killing worse than emotional killing. Gordon Patterson shot John Northrup after finding his wife Roberta with him in a state of "semi-undress", and was convicted of second-degree murder.
  - *Replacement*: there is no universal anger fingerprint. "Moments of emotion are not synonymous with moments that you're out of control." Dylann Roof cultivated his anger for months. Emotion is never a loss of control any more than "a fit of thinking" would be.
- **Reason restrains emotion.** The law assumes a two-system brain, with *actus reus* (the act) and *mens rea* (the guilty mind) mapped onto it.
  - *Replacement*: the control network is always engaged; "you just don't always feel in control." The law fails to distinguish your ability to choose (the workings of the control network) from your subjective experience of choice, and "there is no scientific justification for labeling a 'moment without awareness of control' as emotion."
- **The reasonable person** is a fiction built from stereotypes.
  - Women are expected to feel fear, not anger. Robert Elliott's killing of his brother out of "overwhelming fear" had its conviction overturned. Judy Norman, who killed her abusive husband, had self-defense rejected.
  - Men who kill in domestic violence get lighter sentences.
  - An angry rape victim is judged negatively, and the "angry black woman" stereotype harms defendants.
  - "When people perceive emotion in a man, they usually attribute it to his situation, but when they perceive emotion in a woman, they connect it to her personality. She's a bitch, but he's just having a bad day."
  - Recalled from memory, women report more emotion than men (except anger); measured in the moment, there are no sex differences.
- **"My brain made me do it."** In Italy in 2011, Stefania Albertani's sentence was reduced from life imprisonment to twenty years after two expert witnesses testified that a "causal relationship" between her brain (fewer neurons in the insula and anterior cingulate) and her crime was possible.
  - *Replacement*: "*All* behavior stems from the brain... The wrong way to use neuroscience in court is to argue that a biological explanation automatically releases someone from responsibility. You are your brain." "Mere variability in the brain... is not an extenuating circumstance for a crime. Variation is the norm."
  - The exception is tumors and neurodegeneration, such as some frontotemporal dementias.
- **Remorse can be seen.**
  - Dzhokhar Tsarnaev was called "stone-faced", and only 2 of 12 jurors believed he felt remorse. Chechen culture expects men to be stoic in adversity, and he reportedly became tearful only when his aunt took the stand to plead for his life, consistent with a culture of honor.
  - Dominic Cinelli was paroled unanimously in 2008, then killed a police officer; five of the seven board members resigned.
  - Remorse is a category, and a juror's perception of it is a mental inference. Synchrony is likelier between people of similar backgrounds, and agreement is a poor substitute for accuracy.
- **Intent is a fact.** Intent is a *guess*, and "there is no objective, perceiver-independent criterion of intent." DNA can place a defendant at the scene but cannot establish intent.
  - In one study, people watched a video of protestors. Told the protestors opposed abortion, liberals inferred violent intent and conservatives peaceful intent. Told they were gay-rights activists, each side reversed. "The brain is wired for us to see what we believe."
- **Evidence is objective.**
  - Gruesome photographs and victim impact statements, especially videos set to music, tip jurors' body budgets: "I feel bad, therefore you must have done something bad."
  - Seeing an African American man force open a door, a citizen called 911 and brought about the arrest of Harvard professor Henry Louis Gates, Jr.
  - Florida's stand-your-ground law was justified by the story of James Workman shooting an intruder. In fact Workman's trailer was in a hurricane-damaged area, and the man he shot, Rodney Cox, was a FEMA employee whom Workman, likely under affective realism, perceived as meaning him harm.
  - With concealed carry, you predict a threat, the control network downplays the prediction error, and a harmless citizen is shot.
- **Eyewitness memory is reliable.**
  - Memories are simulations, and recall is "a cascade of predictions that reconstruct the event". Confidence is unrelated to accuracy.
  - Eyewitness testimony figured in over three quarters of DNA exonerations.
  - A rape victim identified Donald Thomson, who was on live TV at the time; her TV had been showing his interview, about memory distortion.
  - The New Jersey, Oregon and Massachusetts Supreme Courts now instruct jurors that memory is constructed. No such guidance exists for emotion.
- **Judges are dispassionate.**
  - "Dispassionate" should mean "having no affect", which is a biological impossibility. Justice Robert Jackson called dispassionate judges "mythical beings" like "Santa Claus or Uncle Sam or Easter bunnies."
  - Judges deny parole more often before lunch.
  - Over 1,800 judges ruled for more likeable defendants.
  - The side that Supreme Court justices address with more unpleasant language tends to lose.
  - Barrett: "Rather than pretend that affect is absent, it's better to use affect wisely. ... The key is emotional granularity." Justice Brennan said sensitivity to "intuitive and passionate responses" is "an aspect more to be nurtured than feared."
- **Emotional harm is lesser harm.** "You can sue someone for breaking your leg but not for breaking your heart."
  - Stress shortens **telomeres**, the protective caps on chromosomes, and children with early adversity have shorter ones. Emotional harm can therefore last longer and do more damage than a broken bone.
  - Bullying can shorten children's telomeres too, yet it is legal.
  - Solitary confinement is legal while psychological torture is not.
  - An Atlanta company demanded DNA samples from employees; the two plaintiffs won $1.75 million in punitive damages for *potential* future suffering, a fear "easy for jurors to simulate and therefore empathize with." Chronic pain gives jurors nothing to see, "so empathy suffers and consequently so does compensation."
  - "The law is about creating and enforcing social reality. Empathic claims about pain are fundamentally claims about whose rights matter . . . and whose humanity matters."

## The affective science manifesto: five teaching points for legal actors

1. **Expressions.** Emotions are not revealed objectively in face, body or voice. "You can only guess, and some guesses are more informed than others." A fair trial depends on synchrony between experiencers and perceivers. Jurors must work harder, and put themselves in the other's shoes, when the defendant differs from them in politics or ethnicity.
2. **Reality.** Senses are always colored by feelings, and even "I saw him holding the knife" is a perception infused with affective realism.
3. **Self-control.** Automatic does not mean uncontrolled, and it does not mean emotional. A defendant is not "a man-shaped sea anemone." "You have relatively more responsibility for your actions when you have relatively more control, regardless of whether the event is an emotion or a cognition."
4. **"My brain made me do it" is junk science.** Unlawful behavior has never been localized to a brain region.
5. **Essentialism.** Sex, race, ethnicity and religion are social categories, not biological ones, and emotion stereotypes do not belong in court. Perhaps it is time to bury the reasonable person.

## For judges and jury selection

- **Emotion is wisdom.** "Emotions are neither the enemy nor a luxury but a source of wisdom." Judges need not reveal their emotions, as therapists do not, but must be aware of them and use them explicitly.
- **Granularity on the bench.** Distinguish anger from irritation or hunger. Treat anger as a reminder to cultivate empathy for an unsympathetic defendant: "Anger is a form of ignorance; in this case, ignorance of the defendant's perspective." Even with James Holmes, who killed 12 in the Aurora theater, empathy guards against punishment that undermines justice. Judges can build granularity with the methods in [mastering-emotions.md](mastering-emotions.md), and should be briefed on other cultures' emotion norms.
- **Goals decide which emotions help.** Retribution, deterrence and rehabilitation call for different ones. Whatever the goal, preserve the defendant's humanity and honor the victim's.
- **Voir dire.** Examine a juror's **affective niche** (the magazines they read, the films they watch, whether they play first-person shooter games) rather than asking them to report their own biases.
- **The radical step.** Perhaps reevaluate trial by jury. Before DNA, the law could ensure consistency but not truth. Panels of multiple judges, randomly drawn and trained to be self-aware and emotionally granular, might resist affective realism better.

## Three types of responsibility

The law has two: actions caused by you, and actions caused by the situation. Construction has three:
1. **Behavior** in the moment (*actus reus*).
2. **The specific predictions** that produced the act (*mens rea*). Stealing from a cash register runs on concepts like "Cash Register," "Money," "Ownership" and "Stealing". Ask whether others with similar concepts would have done the same.
3. **The content of your conceptual system.** "Every human being is the sum of his or her concepts," formed by the culture you were "pickled in", including TV police dramas. "Your mind is not only a function of your brain but also of the other brains in your culture." This is not "society is to blame": "you are ultimately responsible for the concepts that you accept and reject." "Each of us, ultimately, is responsible for our own predictions."

**Foreseeability** extends this. A drunk driver is liable because the risk is known. Likewise, if you predict a weapon in a Black youth's hand and see a gun that is not there, you bear some culpability, because changing your concepts is your responsibility. Inoculating yourself against stereotypes diminishes, but does not erase, that culpability. The First Amendment's war of ideas assumed ideas could simply be rejected, but "culture wires the brain. Ideas get under your skin, simply by sticking around for long enough."
