# Marketing, PR and communications

## The four functions

Product management used to be a subset of marketing, growth marketing didn't exist, and PR meant press releases. What hasn't changed is that all marketing and PR build brand, perception and customer acquisition. Each function needs a different kind of hire.

- **Growth marketing** is analytically driven and covers all quantitative marketing: online ads, tightly measured email, SEO and content, viral loops and funnel optimization. It does demand and lead generation and converts the leads, moving a handful of key metrics (signups, logins, conversions) with a focus on ROI. Facebook pioneered many of its tactics. Hire someone numbers-driven and quantitative.
- **Product marketing** (often just "marketing") is canonical old-school tech marketing: customer testimonials, feature requests, user testing and interviews, competitor analysis, collateral and case studies.
- **Brand marketing** is squishier: awareness, perception, logos and design. Nike's swoosh stands for athleticism and perseverance.
- **PR and communications** is the "ongoing telling of the company story":
  - story development (the narrative);
  - press: proactive, reactive and contributed content;
  - events, product reviews and awards;
  - culture storytelling, exec profiles and influencer relations;
  - first contact in a crisis.

  Hire for storytelling, positioning and process management. Social media lands in either growth marketing or comms.

**Org structure is pragmatic here too.** At an enterprise or sales-heavy company, marketing may sit under sales, product, the COO or the CEO. It may be matrixed or split by business unit. Product marketing may sit under PM while PR and brand sit elsewhere. The trend is for regulatory affairs and lobbying to join PR and comms (or sit under legal), and for growth engineers to sit in engineering. Eventually PR and brand come under one person, sometimes along with product and growth marketing.

**To PR or not to PR.** Wish reached an $8 billion valuation with little brand or PR, through growth and distribution. Twitter grew partly through PR. Tailor the mix to your user base, your product and your best growth vectors. A high PR profile speeds up recruiting, deal-making and partnerships, and fundraising.

## Shannon Stubo Brayton: building the marketing and comms org

Brayton was CMO of LinkedIn.

**Internal communications matters far more than it used to.** At eBay in 2001 it meant emails about org changes and departures. Now one internal comms person is among the first hires at **100 people**, and her **rule of thumb is one internal comms person per hundred people**. A brand message should land the same way inside the company as outside, and employees act as brand ambassadors. Internal comms reaches into:
- recruiting: a consistent brand from the first recruiter email through the paperwork, whether or not the person is hired;
- onboarding: the laptop works, they're invited to the right meetings, the little things.

This "special-touches thing" isn't young male founders' sweet spot. Before social media, internal comms sat under HR. Now employees ask what to say online and how to respond to negativity about the company or the founder, so internal comms belongs in the broader comms and marketing team, as at LinkedIn.

**The CMO.** The lines used to be clear: PR talked to reporters and marketing bought ads. Now a CMO must be an "agile, versatile player" who is "good at a hundred different things": copywriting, creative, research, NPS.
- **Number one: an excellent leader.** They make decisions well and know how to tell the company's story so that ads, demand gen and events dovetail with the press narrative.
- **Background depends on the company:** comms at LinkedIn, demand gen at Salesforce, product marketing at DoorDash. They should be expert in one area and learn the others.
- **How a non-marketer CEO hires one:**
  - Check for **chemistry**: they must be willing to be honest with the CEO, and you can tell within the first hour. Check for shared values.
  - Ask "If I had this problem, how would you go about approaching it?" and see whether their approach is close to yours.
- **The biggest mistake** is "falling in love with a super creative person who has zero operational expertise or interest in management and putting them in a CMO role". This is the flashy New York hire behind a famous campaign at Pepsi, Apple or Nike. Check whether they can manage a team, scale it, learn demand gen, product marketing and comms, and run an NPS survey.
- **The CMO's standing.** At consumer giants such as General Mills the marketer is effectively a GM. In Silicon Valley, marketing has had to fight for its seat and is often there to enable sales, so the marketer must make the value clear and contribute strategically.

**Org design.**
- Ask "If you were to start with a blank slate, what would that team look like?", and don't optimize for one person ("we have to have Stephanie in that job because she just relocated").
- For marketing by line of business, avoid full vertical teams with no **horizontal shared resources**. LinkedIn's redundant teams shared no best practices. Best practice is **shared services** (operations, creative) that every business line uses. They build trust and prevent bloat and redundancy.
- Gil adds: hire people who have been through hypergrowth before, so they know re-orgs are normal.

**Where to invest first.**
- **Consumer:** **product marketing** (the road map, usage, what users like and dislike). No brand work at the outset, until you have market fit.
- **B2B:** **demand gen**, because "You've got to have customers."
- **Later:** PR, then brand. A common mistake is using PR in place of brand. A big TechCrunch story no longer does anything, though five years earlier it could make or break a company.

For her signs that a leader isn't scaling, see [`executives.md`](executives.md).

## PR basics

Early on you are too small for full-time PR; later you'll have an in-house team plus an agency.

- **Get media trained.**
  - Learn the definitions (below).
  - Learn the interview types: broadcast, in person, phone, video.
  - Practice the tough questions.
  - Have a concise storyline on your products, your competitors and yourself, including the founding narrative and your personal why.
  - Learn how to answer a question other than the one asked.
- **Iterate on the company pitch.** Practice it like an actor, including objection handling, until the story is crisp and memorized. Stay authentic, because reading from a script kills credibility. On the phone, a talking-points document is fine.
- **On the record, on background, off the record.** Specify which one and get the reporter's agreement; your PR person pre-arranges it and reconfirms at the start. Without an agreement, assume you are on the record.
  - **Off the record:** the reporter may not write about it or quote it. You can ask for it mid-conversation if the journalist agrees.
  - **On background:** usable without direct attribution, as in "sources say that Google is moving into flying cars".
  - **On the record:** quoted and directly attributed.
- **Correct factual errors,** such as a misunderstood scientific fact or a wrong product name. Leave opinion alone ("they hate my product"). Reporters may not share drafts with you first. Most coverage is forgotten, and most companies have had bad stories.
- **Know a reporter's agenda.** Most press is ethical, but some reporters write to a predefined narrative and massage the facts. Read their previous stories. For thoughtless attack pieces, limit your engagement. PR people know which reporters are which.
- **Hiring PR people.** There are "only a few dozen truly great PR people in technology," clustered at a handful of companies. Ask PR people, agencies and journalists whom they respect. Partner-level agency people are an option.
- **Build press relationships early.** Avoid transactional contact: send articles they'd find interesting, and have coffee with no agenda. Later the CEO should be judicious and designate exec spokespeople.
- **Engage PR early.** A big launch takes **4–10 weeks** to coordinate, more with a new agency. Product comms is part of launch timing, like design. Amazon writes a product's "headline", the press story, into the design doc at conception, which forces crisp thinking.
- **Press does not equal success.** Profitable, scalable revenue is what matters. PR is not a recurring distribution channel. Don't confuse coverage with traction, and know that good press can't cover a bad business decision.

**Crisis management.** Companies get built up and then torn down. The steps:
1. **Analyze the problem.** What went wrong? What is the impact on the company, customers and other stakeholders? How will the press and competitors portray it? What are your options?
2. **Acknowledge the problem.** A negative cycle runs its natural course, and acknowledging the problem speeds the resolution. The **river metaphor**: swim with the current and you reach the bank quickly; swim against it and you exhaust yourself and get swept downstream anyway. Acknowledge the mistake, state the plan and act. "Do not lie."
3. **Take action.** Do what you said you would, quickly.

## Erin Fors: building the PR team you really need

Fors cofounded the PR firm Cutline Communications.

**Know what PR can and can't do.** Founders get conflicting advice: the board says get PR, reporters say "PR is stupid", and PR people and reporters are in natural tension. PR gives a company a voice and credibility, communicates its purpose and humanizes it, and helps recruiting and morale. It is not a channel for most companies. A launch spike fades, so keep evergreen story ideas for the gaps between news moments, use marketing and social media between cycles, and be patient. "PR cannot come in and fix a bad business decision", and it needs a story to tell, about the product or the culture. Not every company needs PR: if the goal is user acquisition, full stop, that's marketing.

**The team depends on the company:**
- Pinterest, a consumer company with low regulatory and privacy risk: a small focused internal team or person, plus an agency for launches.
- Airbnb: a regulatory focus.
- Stripe, in payments and security: a stronger crisis plan and regulatory, Capitol Hill comms.

Most high-growth companies use an internal team plus an agency. Founders and execs buy into the strategy and trust the team with the day to day.

**Where the ball drops is a lack of communication.** Founders fear admitting they were wrong. They make unclear pricing changes on enterprise customers, sneak in product changes, or bury them in the terms of service or privacy policy. Be straightforward and ride it out. One misstep can set back a media darling, and canned, airline-style responses lead to backpedaling. Often the problem is a founder unwilling to risk personal credibility by saying "I screwed up and I need to fix this"; PR will usually tell them to be honest.

**The first internal PR person** must be in complete lockstep with the founder, and trusted. Jeff Weiner's trust in Shannon Stubo Brayton, with a seat at the table, is the model.

**Selecting PR people.** "PR is more art than science", so weigh chemistry, cultural fit and experience. Walk through scenarios to check that you think alike. Founders should be involved in the hire and respect PR as a discipline. Put weight on experience, **back-channel references** and people skills, and little on the interview itself. Ask board members, advisors, reporters and agency people whom they respect; the list will be short.

**Choosing an agency.**
- **Early stage:** you may need only a freelancer or consultant who scales up and down for funding announcements and launches, not in-house PR all the time.
- **Finding candidates:** ask reporters which PR people they respect and look at those people's agencies; ask VCs and advisors. Include the founder and leadership in the discussions.
- **Checking fit:**
  - Do they use and love the product?
  - Check their own social presence and brand voice.
  - Culture alignment feels "like magic"; a clash makes the work "virtually impossible."
  - **Meet the actual team.** Avoid the new-business team's **bait-and-switch**, and look for people who are engaged and ask questions.
- **The RFP:** keep the asks broad-ish ("How would you engage women between the age of 18 and 34?") rather than overly specific. Weight agencies that **challenge you** and push back ("you asked for X but… more value to do it a different way").

**Working with PR:**
- Focus on the quality of coverage, not the count, since reporters change beats.
- Nit-picking ("Why aren't we in TechCrunch?") demoralizes the team. Trust them, and spend founder time building product and scaling.
- Talk to the press when you have something good to say, for example in trend stories.
- Reporters calling founders directly is good. PR's job is to prep you with background and angles, so don't greet that with "why am I paying you?"
