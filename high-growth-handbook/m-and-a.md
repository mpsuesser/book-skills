# Mergers and acquisitions: buying other companies

As your valuation rises, your stock becomes a currency for buying other companies. Done well, acquisitions speed up your product and hiring plans and let you make strategic or defensive moves against competitors. At Twitter, Gil ran the M&A team and saw how many startups flounder looking for an acquirer. At Google he worked on Android, Google Mobile Maps (which began as a company called ZipDash) and the first Gmail client (Reqwireless). Marc Andreessen points out that Cisco and Google were built by M&A, and that the new big incumbents underuse it. Gil: "Most companies wait too long before making their first acquisition or are hesitant to use their stock as currency."

## When to start

Strategic acquisitions can be necessary early. Twitter bought Summize, which became Twitter Search, when Twitter had about 15 people and was worth around $100 million.
- **At $1 billion+**, the CEO and board should treat M&A as a serious tool. At that size, a $10M acquisition costs 1% of your equity. If it raises your valuation by just 10%, it is clearly ROI-positive.
- **At $5–10 billion+**, M&A can become central to company strategy.
- **For revenue-generating buys,** value is often directly quantifiable, and the price becomes "a simple math problem". If an ad-tech acquisition would add $50 million of revenue, you know what it's worth. Translate revenue plus margin into market cap: at **10X earnings**, an extra $10M of margin is worth about $100M of your market cap. That lets the M&A team make ROI arguments and prioritize product purchases.

## Three types of acquisition

High-growth companies make three kinds of buy. A fourth, the "synergistic" acquisition for market share and cost cutting, belongs to mature companies.

| Type | Valuation range | Why buy | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Team buy, or "acqui-hire"** | From a small signing bonus for the founders up to **$1M–$3M per engineering, product or design employee** | Hire faster, and get talent you couldn't otherwise hire. The product is usually discarded and the team reassigned | Facebook's acquisition of Drop.io, largely to get Sam Lessin |
| **Product buy** | **$5M–$500M**; most from several million to $100M | Fill a product hole, or reposition a team onto an area already on your road map. The product may survive standalone, be integrated, or be replaced by your own launch | ZipDash, which seeded Google Mobile Maps; Android; Summize, which became Twitter Search |
| **Strategic buy** | Up to **$20B** | Buy a non-reproducible asset of strategic value. Facebook could launch a photo app but couldn't reproduce Instagram's community | Instagram and WhatsApp (Facebook); DoubleClick, Motorola and YouTube (Google) |

How you negotiate and execute each type differs wildly. Strategic buys are heavily negotiated, usually involve the acquirer's CEO, and come down to connecting with the target's founder and painting a big picture. They can be very emotional sales. At the other end, small team buys are often desperation moves by founders seeking a **"soft landing"** because they are running out of money or lack product/market fit.

## The M&A road map

Have someone in corporate development, product or BD build an M&A road map, with input from three groups:
- (i) key hiring managers, on the people or teams they most want;
- (ii) product and engineering leaders, on the holes in the product road map;
- (iii) the exec team, on big-picture strategic buys.

Gil's speculative road map for Facebook around 2012:
- **Hiring M&A.** Buy teams of 3–10 people strong in mobile engineering, product and design, or in advertising products, to answer the need for a mobile team and Wall Street's pressure on ads. Break the teams up into the areas of greatest need. Also buy machine-learning and data-science teams, since those can't be hired fast enough.
- **Product M&A.** Buy Snaptu to get mobile clients into Latin America and Asia-Pacific, an email-scraping company for international growth, and an IM-focused team to reposition for Messenger.
- **Strategic M&A.** Build relationships with the founders of the top five social apps. Set up quarterly 1:1s for Zuckerberg with the CEOs of WhatsApp, Instagram, Pinterest, Twitter and Weibo, and decide when to bid for each.

For any purchase, ask:
- Can we absorb a team of this size without screwing up our culture?
- What will the org chart and reporting structure be?
- Will the incoming leaders have a broader impact? Are there areas we struggle with that the incoming entrepreneur can own?

## Managing internal stakeholders

Internal pushback on an acquisition may reflect a real strategic or tactical insight. It may instead reflect a lack of pragmatism about how little the company can do with limited resources, or jealousy over the price and the founders' payday. Junior employees and old-timers push back more than experienced execs who have seen acquisitions done well elsewhere.

**Keep the target list an internal secret,** not an all-hands topic, for four reasons:
1. **Word may leak.** That can trigger competing bids, disrupt the target's operations (an employee tells a friend there), or prompt competitors to lobby regulators to block the deal.
2. **Most employees lack the context.** They may not understand M&A as a tool, or that you might talk to ten companies to buy one, and they'll worry about deals that never happen.
3. **It becomes harder to bid again later.** After a public attempt and a public pass, a second attempt on the same company is harder to justify internally.
4. **People may try to block the deal inappropriately.**

## Dealing with objections

1. **"Couldn't we just build this ourselves for cheaper?"** For example: why pay $20M for a small team when $20M would hire 100 people for a year? Answers:
   - Resources, including how fast you can hire, limit what you can do. Without the deal, this objective won't get done, or will be delayed dramatically.
   - You're buying a team that has thought deeply about the area, needs no ramp-up, and brings its own insights.
   - They bring their own leaders to drive it.
   - Getting it done a year earlier may be worth far more than the $20M.
2. **"We are going to crush this competitor!"** Sometimes the right move really is to outcompete everyone. Answers:
   - The competitor drives down margins by contesting every deal, and removing it pays for itself.
   - The deal is **accretive**: you trade at 10X revenue and pay 5X for them. You gain share, grow your market cap by more than the stock you spent, and perhaps lower costs through scale.
   - The deal is **defensive**: buying them keeps "large-scary-co" from getting a toehold in your market through acquisition.
3. **"Does the team really meet our bar?"**
   - For a team buy: you'll put the team through your interview process, make the deal contingent on enough of them joining, and leave out or give a short trial to anyone you're unsure about.
   - For a strategic buy: you're buying the core asset and market share, not just the team. At integration, assess each person and decide where, how and whether they fit. Anyone who wants to switch teams afterward does a full interview circuit.
4. **"It will take a long time to integrate."** They say it will take a year or two to port the code to your stack, and your own team could build it faster. Answers:
   - You don't have, and won't soon have, the headcount for this. The team has a live product you can distribute, so buying is faster. They can stay on their own stack or port over.
   - Meanwhile you have a product in the market that attracts customers and blocks competitors.
5. **"What if the acquisition fails?"** Answers:
   - Internal new products don't succeed 100% of the time either, by design: "If nothing fails it means we are not taking enough risk."
   - Enough successes pay for the failures.
   - You also get talent you couldn't hire directly because of their risk profiles.

## Interviewing acquired teams

Employees care about price and about whether acquired people meet the bar. A team whose only professional experience is your company may be bad at interviewing senior people, and senior Google or Facebook engineers might fail a junior team's interviews. To give acquired people a fair shot, in engineering and analogously in other functions:
1. **Standardize interview processes and questions** (see [`recruiting-and-talent.md`](recruiting-and-talent.md)). Make sure the questions work for senior as well as junior engineers; coding questions can reward how recently someone finished a CS degree, through theory, more than their ability to code.
2. **Put mainly senior engineers with cross-company experience on preset M&A panels.**
3. **Remind the panel** that most of these candidates were just told their company is for sale and may not have had time to prepare.
4. **Do references, if it makes sense, and weigh them highly.** Acquired employees had no say in where they're going and haven't interviewed recently. Discount standard items such as "do they have passion for our company" that you'd use for someone walking in off the street.

## Setting a valuation

Setting the price is "more art than science".

**For all three types, assess:**
- **The target's cash position.** With only 3–6 months of cash left, they have little time to negotiate, raise more money or find other buyers.
- **How desperate the founders or managers are to sell.** Teams get tired; this was famously the motivation behind Flickr's sale to Yahoo!.
- **Whether it's competitive.** Who else might bid, and at what price? If your stock looks to have more upside (Airbnb at a $1 billion valuation), you're a more convincing acquirer than Google, which is unlikely to 10X again soon.
- **Whether it's defensive.** Must you block someone else from buying it? Google's purchase of Waze may have been partly to keep Facebook or Apple out of mapping.
- **How truly unique the target is.** A dozen mobile teams running out of money is a different situation from Instagram, a one-of-a-kind asset.

**Team buys.** Price depends on:
- **team quality:** brand-name companies or schools, great products launched before;
- **unique expertise:** mobile client developers right after the iOS App Store launched; deep learning more recently;
- **the acquirer's desperation:** Google "paid through the nose" for "social" PMs, designers and engineers in the Google+ era;
- **a celebrity cofounder or engineer,** who helps you recruit and brings outsized networks.

At the low end, an acqui-hire means a **20% signing bonus for the founders**, standard packages for everyone else, and nothing back for the cap table. This is more common than people think; many touted acquisitions are really hiring a subset of the team and shutting the product down. A true team buy pays **$1M–$3M per engineer, designer and PM who gets an offer**. Businesspeople and operations staff range from a marginal bump in value to a negative, if they need severance. Depending on structure, much of the per-head value pays off the cap table (say, the $5M the VCs invested) or funds retention. You'll usually interview the team first and reserve the right to cut people who don't meet the bar, which lowers the price. That is a tough conversation.

**Product buys.** Also ask:
- How much time does this save us? What hole does it fill? Can the team be repositioned?
- What is the quantifiable impact: how many users, at what value per user, or how much revenue or cash flow, at what margin?
- How does it change the strategic landscape? Does it block a competitor, and what does it enable us to do?

Rule of thumb: give the target's employees **at least a 20–50% bump** over what they'd get walking in off the street. They didn't choose to come, so paying a bit more up front discourages them from leaving two months later. Treat this as a worst case; early employees from product and strategic buys may make dramatically more.

**Strategic buys** do at least one of four things:
- change the market structure of your industry;
- give you a key, non-reproducible, defensible asset;
- block a competitor from a major action or position;
- dramatically change some part of your business, such as its cost structure or distribution channel.

Expect multiple deep-pocketed bidders. Google, Facebook and Apple all reportedly bid on AdMob, which went to Google for a reported $750 million in 2009. Ask:
- How does this change fundamental aspects of my business? Does it consolidate a key market, allow cross-selling, or open a new area?
- Could I reproduce this asset myself, or through other purchases?
- Would a competitor buying it cause an **existential crisis** for my company or product line?
- Could this company become a major competitor in its own right? Can I buy it before it does?
- Is there unique talent here that could change my trajectory?
- What is the P&L of the combined entity? What revenue will it generate, and what costs can be cut?
- Which metrics should set the purchase price?

## Convincing someone, and their investors, to sell

Founders sell out of **fear and exhaustion** (they're tired, out of money, or about to be crushed by a competitor) or out of **ambition and excitement** (the impact they'll have, or the payday).

**Team and product buys: the entrepreneurs.** A burned-out or cash-starved founder will try to sell *you*. Otherwise, your levers are:
- **Compensation.** People have "levels" of wealth in mind: paying off school debt, buying a house, never working again. A founder reacts differently to $1M in stock ("I can work for your company for a few years and then try again"), $5M (Bay Area real estate and fewer worries), and $10M ("I probably do not need to work ever again"). Stock and even cash typically vest over one to four years, and stock the founder already earned may be re-vested.
- **Impact.** You likely reach millions or hundreds of millions of people. Can their product or vision shape the lives of millions through yours?
- **Role.** Can you offer a larger team or more influence than they had at their startup?
- **Threats.** Gil isn't a fan, but some companies are known for threatening the startups they want: an IP lawsuit, or a casual mention that they plan to enter the market. Most of these threats are idle.

**Team and product buys: the investors,** who may be able to block a sale. Investors want at least a **3X return** on average, which means more than 3X on the winners, so an offer below 3X may draw a fight. What to tell them:
- **"You are trading low-appreciation stock for high-appreciation stock."** The target isn't on a breakout trajectory, but you are. Taking your stock now beats even a doubling of the startup. Mixer Labs sold to Twitter in 2009, when Twitter was worth about $1 billion, and has since seen at least a further 10X.
- **"We plan to buy someone in this market. If it is not this company, it will be someone else."** It's now or never.
- **"Our relationships are important to us, and you are in our ecosystem."** VCs who repeatedly sell portfolio companies to the same acquirers want a direct line to corp dev. Silicon Valley is a long game.

Some investors behave badly during an acquisition. One of Mixer Labs' investors was "an enormous pain" who kept trying to negotiate more deal value for himself.

**Strategic buys.** Assets that are truly strategic and breaking out are much harder to buy, so build relationships long before. Once you reach a certain scale, meet the CEOs of companies you might want to buy **every quarter or two**. Zuckerberg's focus on the founders of WhatsApp and Instagram put Facebook in pole position for both. The big levers:
- **Autonomy.** Founders started companies to drive things themselves. Promise to leave them alone while supplying money and SG&A (sales, general and administrative) resources for scaling. Android and YouTube ran autonomously, with their own hiring processes, for years after Google bought them, while Google funded them and gave them headcount for operations and sales.
- **Support.** Some founders don't want to build a standalone public company, with a sales team, employee ladders and accounting meetings. Offer autonomy plus support so they can focus on what they care about.
- **Impact and role.** Founders often get a bigger role than they had. John Hanke, of Keyhole (the software behind Google Earth), ended up running all of Google Maps.
- **Competitive dynamics and fear.** YouTube sold to Google partly because of the threat of media-industry lawsuits. Some founders can be convinced that third-party forces, lawsuits or other external threats are about to crush them.
- **Money.** It cuts both ways. A founder sure of doing well may not be tempted, but another can be convinced that selling is the safe road. "One of the biggest causes of early exits are founders' spouses, who are typically less risk-seeking."

## Negotiating the acquisition

"All negotiations are about relative leverage (or the perception of leverage)." Ask about cash and burn rate, the cap table, team size and product growth to gauge how desperate the founders are.

**Team or product buys, rules of thumb:**
1. **If the target closed a round in the preceding 3–6 months, expect to pay at least a 50% premium** on that valuation; their investors may push for 2–3X. If the market has shifted dramatically, the founders are desperate, or the prospects are poor, you may pay the last-round price or less.
2. **Value per engineer, PM or designer is $1M–$3M,** and up to $5M for special talent. Business, operations and community staff are worth little or less than nothing, since some won't join and will need severance. The money is spread across the cap table (mainly investors and founders) and retention.
3. **Get a range from your CEO or board, then anchor as low as you reasonably can.** Boards approve an "up to" price with a likely range, say $15M up to $20M. In a non-competitive situation, open low (around $12M) to leave room. In a competitive one, open aggressively (around $18M).
4. **Corp dev opening bids usually quote total deal value,** without splitting what goes to the cap table (preferred and common) from what goes to retention, and they usually ignore cash on hand. Say you offer $10M for a six-person team with $1M in the bank. You get their cash, so the real price is $9M. The offer may really mean $6M to the cap table and $3M in retention, so a founder who owns 20% gets $1.2M, not the $2M they expected. The $3M is split across all six people, about $500,000 each in four-year retention, founder included. Some acquirers even use part of the company's own cash for team bonuses. Once a founder is excited by an offer, they rarely walk away after learning the details. They have seen the light at the end of the tunnel ("I don't need to wake up in a cold sweat") and mentally spent the money. That's why "Entrepreneurs should always talk to a small subset of their investors or advisors when they receive an acquisition offer", to avoid the common traps set by savvy corp dev people.

The person who negotiates with the founder should **not be the person the founder will report to** or work with day to day. Deals leave bad feelings, because letting go of a startup is hard, especially for first-time entrepreneurs.

**Strategic assets:**
1. **Buying a strategic asset is also a sale by your company.** Zuckerberg built trusted relationships with the founders of WhatsApp and Instagram long in advance and sold them on joining Facebook. In Gil's phrasing, he "sold Facebook to Whatsapp rather than the other way around."
2. **The CEO has to get involved** in the relationship-building and in parts of the deal itself. If the other CEO feels a relationship with you, they'll trust you with their company's future, and the deal is easier to close.
3. **Move quickly.** Especially against other bidders, speed creates time pressure and a sense that you're the inevitable choice. It also shows professionalism and no dickering over small items. Google bought YouTube for $1.6 billion in less than a week.
