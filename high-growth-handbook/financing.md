# Late-stage financing, valuation and market cycles

Companies used to go public early: Intel two years after incorporating, Amazon three, Apple four, Cisco five, Microsoft about ten. In the 2000s it began taking a decade or more. Investors shifted into private rounds, large secondary markets grew, and founders became skeptical of going public. Gil calls this "one of the best times" to raise late-stage rounds.

## Types of late-stage investors

| Type | Check size; valuations | Benefits | Drawbacks | What they look for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional VC | Up to $50M a round (more from a growth fund); rarely leads above mid-hundreds of millions | Operating and scaling advice, depending on the partner | Takes a board seat; may not broaden your network | Macro trends, unit economics, differentiation, the **"moat"** |
| Growth or mezzanine fund | $25M–$500M; $100M–$10B | Hands-off; a different network | Less operational; numbers-driven | Growth rates, margin, user adoption, CAC, unit economics |
| Hedge fund | $10M–$500M; mainly $500M+ | Industry understanding from public holdings; may be valuation-insensitive; may not need a seat | Doesn't understand startup uncertainty; an early hedge fund round may send a "low quality" signal | Leaders in large markets; long-term cash flows. Thinks like a public investor |
| Private equity | $10M–$500M; mainly $500M+ | Broad networks; intros to late-stage portfolio companies for enterprise sales | One PE fund is known for nasty terms or bad behavior after the term sheet. Bank-affiliated PE (Goldman, Morgan Stanley) values the banking relationship and is friendlier. Diligence with founders they funded | Revenue scale, margin, growth, defensibility |
| Family office | $5M–$500M; usually late | Network; may be valuation-insensitive | Often doesn't understand startups and gets uneasy when things get tough. Work with the person whose money it is, not the staff, or pick offices with private-market experience | Signals from institutional investors; large markets; high margins |
| Angel SPV | $1M–$50M; Series A onward | Lets an existing angel raise for you in a big round, increase ownership with permission, and deepen engagement; votes go to a trusted party | VCs may push back; delivery risk. Define the process and what information goes to its LPs | Lead SPVs act like VCs; syndicates are momentum-driven; late or large ones run full diligence |
| Public market investor | Up to $500M; hundreds of millions to billions | Trusted "smart money" that holds after the IPO; a legitimacy signal | May publicly mark down your stock, which hurts fundraises and secondaries | Views you through an IPO lens |
| Strategic | Tens of millions to $1B+; hundreds of millions+ | Valuation-insensitive, may pay a premium; ties to the core business; a broader strategic deal | **Signaling** risk early (a Pfizer stake in a digital health company deters other pharma partners and acquirers); may be learning how to compete | Strategic value; learning how the market is reshaping; sometimes a prelude to acquisition. **Save strategics for later rounds** |
| Large foreign internet company | Up to $1B | Less valuation-sensitive; entry to China and elsewhere (Tencent, Alibaba, Rakuten) | Ties to JV structures; may learn to launch a competitor at home | |
| Sovereign wealth fund | Up to a few billion | Some are less valuation-sensitive; entry to new markets and state-owned enterprises; scale | Slow, with hurdles; newer ones may be less savvy | |

Strategics invest for three reasons: (1) to cut a broader partnership, (2) to learn how technology will affect their industry, or (3) to acquire you later. Examples are Roche in Flatiron, GM in Lyft and Intel in Cloudera. An **SPV** (special purpose vehicle) is a one-time fund that raises money from LPs to invest in a single company. Treat firm names as examples only; lists of investors go out of date.

## How to evaluate a late-stage funder

- **Follow-on capital.** Can they keep investing in larger rounds?
- **Public market impact.** Long-term holders such as T. Rowe Price and Fidelity send a positive signal. One public investor publishes month-by-month marks of its private holdings, which causes problems for fundraises, secondaries and morale.
- **Strategic value.** Industry knowledge, intros, a country. Uber's China subsidiary raised from Chinese funders to help with government relations. Google took an investment from Yahoo! while powering Yahoo Search.
- **Simple terms.** Avoid complex structures, extra liquidation preferences, provisions about issuing below the IPO price, and clawbacks. **Simple terms are worth a lower valuation.**
- **Board seats.** DST pioneered investing without one.
- **Ability to buy secondary** or run a tender: their appetite, and any SEC registrations they need. See [`secondaries-and-ipo.md`](secondaries-and-ipo.md).

## Key terms: preference and the board

Late-stage negotiations come down to **preference and board membership**.

**Preference.** Top early-stage investors use clean, non-participating preferred stock. PE firms and family offices may ask for a 2X or 3X preference, a ratchet on the next round (which turns their equity into something close to debt), or IPO provisions that give them extra stock if the IPO prices below their valuation or takes longer than six to nine months. Avoid these if you can. They may be unavoidable when your valuation runs ahead of your metrics or capital is scarce.

**The board.** Larger boards are harder to run. Late investors may bring financial discipline and a public-market perspective, which can be very helpful or very destructive. They are more driven by numbers, revenue and margin, and less used to "oh shit" moments. Some are hands-off (Yuri Milner at DST). Consider adding no one. Before you add a late-stage board member:
1. Diligence their past investments and board seats.
2. Have frank conversations about direction and expectations.
3. Look for other ways to give them meaningful impact and information without a seat.

A late investor can also help clean out poor early-stage board members (see [`board.md`](board.md)).

**DST** (Yuri Milner) changed late-stage investing, starting with Facebook in 2009. It bought primary stock, common stock on the secondary market, or a mix, took no board seat, behaved in an entrepreneur-friendly way, and wrote very large checks ($1B+ in a company's lifetime), effectively a "private IPO". At the time this was radical, since late investors wanted complicated preferences, seats and control. Gil counts it among the three biggest venture innovations of the decade, with Y Combinator's early-stage revolution and AngelList Syndicates.

**One word of caution.** One PE group signs a term sheet and then, about three weeks later, after the company has told its other investors and lost its leverage, renegotiates. It was kicked out of a unicorn round for this. There are good actors too, such as KKR.

## Don't over-optimize your valuation

A high valuation helps with recruiting and comp, PR and M&A currency, and ego. But your ability to raise the next round depends on the last valuation as much as on the business. The four problems:
1. **Follow-on rounds get hard.** Investors expect a **2–3X** step-up each round, falling to a **50–100%** markup once you're valued in the billions. Adding a billion dollars of market cap takes a lot of value creation.
2. **The investor mix shifts** toward non-traditional late investors with **18- to 24-month** horizons, who push for liquidity.
3. **Internal pressure to hit the target valuation causes bad behavior.**
4. **Employee expectations.** People get upset by a down round, or by a valuation that stays flat for 3–4 years while the business catches up.

**Founder pressure** at a multi-billion valuation shows up two ways:
- pushing unsustainable growth at all costs. Doubling down on, say, a money-losing acquisition can flip a company from **default alive to default dead**. The pressure often comes from the board or late investors when deck projections are missed;
- distraction: press, speaking, personal investments.

**Before taking a valuation, ask:**
- Will this money get us to a healthy multiple on this valuation? If not, should we take a lower one?
- What milestones will it reach, and will they change our perceived worth?
- What exits are possible above this valuation? Which acquirers could pay more?
- Will an IPO price above this private valuation?

## Naval Ravikant: hacking late-stage funding

- **Many companies don't need large rounds.** The internet makes many kinds of companies cheaper to build. Hardware and businesses that expand locally are the exceptions. Naval calls this "the single most important elephant in the room": software is open source, hardware sits in AWS, marketing runs through Google, Facebook, the app stores and contact lists, half the engineers can be outsourced, and the community handles customer service. WhatsApp sold for $19 billion with about 50 people, YouTube had fewer than 60, and Instagram had a few.
- **Build custom bundles.** Late rounds are increasingly led by mutual funds, strategics and family offices going direct, so you can shape the deal. Keep control, avoid giving board seats, and avoid vetoes over M&A, the option pool and future fundraising. The ultimate hack is to **sell common stock**. The exception is non-arm's-length transactions and insider self-dealing, where investors should have a veto.
- **"Valuation is temporary. Control is forever."** Whoever has control ends up controlling your valuation later. Control slips away through **protective provisions**, which were created to protect minority preferred holders. A veto on future fundraising means "they effectively have a lock on your company". The same goes for vetoes on expanding the option pool and on M&A, which is where the biggest fights happen.
- **What the liquidation preference is for.** Say an investor puts $1M into a company at a $9M pre-money valuation and owns 10%. Without a preference, the founders could distribute the $1M to all shareholders: the founders get $900,000 and the investor gets $100,000 back. The preference protects the investor in a distribution. Stacked onto later rounds, it has become "this giant freebie". It doesn't exist in public markets, which are all common stock, because nobody shuts down a $900M company to hand back a $100M investment. "You'd be a fool to do a seed round buying common stock", but at late stage a preference makes no sense.
- **The fallback ladder.** This is negotiable only when you have multiple bidders.
  1. Sell common stock.
  2. Sell common plus a liquidation preference, leaving out "all the other junk".
  3. Grant protective provisions only for **non-arm's-length transactions**, such as issuing stock to yourself or a friend, or selling the company to your brother.
  4. Let the veto belong to **the preferred as a class**, not to each series. Your earlier investors are the ones you trust more, and later ones "just showed up yesterday".

  Founder shares with extra voting power work up to a point. Some preferred rights can't be contracted away under Delaware and California law. Under California law, as of 2003, every series had to separately approve an M&A. Delaware gives even a small shareholder **inspection rights** to demand the financials.
- **Raising too much hurts.** Money is cheap, and it works as insurance, a scorecard and acquisition currency. But "when you raise more money you do spend more money". You move slower, meetings get bigger, there are more stakeholders to coordinate, and you take on too many projects. Somewhat **cash-constrained companies do better**. Pierre Omidyar credited eBay's success to having little outside financing for a long time. Its competitors put humans in every transaction; eBay built automated ratings and so scaled faster. A lack of headcount forced scalable processes from day one.
- **New investors are good for entrepreneurs.** VCs who bash them as dumb money are like a laundromat owner angry about a new laundromat down the street. "filter venture advice through venture incentives." **Venture capital is a bundle of advice, control and money.** More options let you unbundle it: take advice from the people you want, money from the cheapest source, and leave the control behind.
  - **Hot money:** new investors may not support you in a future round. "almost nobody adds value as an investor in a later-stage company", but they can hurt you by skipping their pro-rata or vetoing something. The fix is to give them no control and expect no future money from them.
  - **Money has karma:** "You cannot take someone's money without having a moral, ethical obligation to them." You owe them your time, and you don't want to get sued, which makes you untouchable later.
  - **Later-stage players swap people.** A corporate investor's head of corp dev gets sacked and the CEO's brother takes over. VCs are stable, in ten-year partnerships. Keep some control over who the relationship or board person is.
- **Interview your investors.** "people's true motivations and behavior are revealed, not said". Someone who spends ten minutes telling you how honest they are is dishonest. Reference-check "the hell out of them" and watch how they behave during the negotiation. Easy, quick, hassle-free and smart is a good sign. An exploding term sheet, inflexibility or intransigence will be "ten times worse once the money is in." The same is true of VCs. Call off the close on negative signals; Naval has done it and never regretted it. You are "married to investors, with almost no possibility of divorce," after a dating period of a week to a year. Use your early investors' nose, carefully: they can be brand-obsessed, wanting a big markup or a markup from Sequoia. Find the one with a high bar who says the unpopular thing.
- **Hire extremely slowly.** People work hard, tire, and hire someone to do their job, and "it always takes two new hires to do your job". Repeat that and a web app company ends up with five thousand people. Then a new CEO has to fire half and can't tell which half, because everyone is politicking. Hire only after there's a **burning need**, be ruthless about trimming, and keep a tight eye on waste, because "there's always waste."
- **Equity design.** Stock grants beat no stock but are outdated. AngelList uses six-year vests, and VC firms ten-year ones. Give stock to the early people building the scaffolding, then shift toward **profit sharing** (or revenue sharing before profits) as the company ages. RSUs are diffuse in a company of thousands. Alternatively, give a small standard grant to everyone, and the next year either increase a person's grant substantially or let them go. Gil likens this to McKinsey's "up or out". Before product/market fit, early hires are really **"late founders"** and should get 1, 2, 3 or 4% rather than 0.1–0.4%. "not that there's a shortage of engineers, it's that there's a surplus of founders."

**Careers.** Naval sees two groups doing best, risk-adjusted, over a long period: venture investors, and people good at spotting companies that have just hit product/market fit and joining to help them scale (from Google, to Facebook at a hundred people, to Stripe at a hundred).

For Naval on founder secondaries, see [`secondaries-and-ipo.md`](secondaries-and-ipo.md).

## Market cycles

When public markets collapse, private markets overreact, for three reasons:
1. **Comparables.** If public valuations drop 20–30%, private ones follow: a billion-dollar valuation becomes $700M. Companies that raised high in a strong market may need a down round.
2. **LP rebalancing.** Endowments, pensions and family offices cap the share of their capital in venture. After a big public-market drop they must pull money out of venture, so funds raise less. This plays out over one to three years.
3. **Fear replaces greed.** "When people get scared they sit on their wallets."

The best time to go public is during an ongoing bull market. Capital raised in an up market lets you survive a down market and act aggressively in it. Amazon used its market cap to make acquisitions during the 1990s bubble, then lived on its public-market cash through the early 2000s. The longer founders wait, the more hurdles they face: market drops, private terms that demand an IRR or a minimum IPO price, or a valuation set too high that takes years to grow into. "While the 1990s were characterized by companies who went public too early, the 2010s perhaps had many companies that waited too long." For Rabois's warning that capital is like oxygen, see [`secondaries-and-ipo.md`](secondaries-and-ipo.md).

## Managing in a downturn

Tech runs in 5- to 15-year cycles, and each new generation of entrepreneurs repeats the mistakes. In an up market, capital is plentiful and growth with poor unit economics gets funded. In a down market, cash is scarce and growth is often sacrificed for profitability. A very early company (five people, $2M raised) should keep seeking product/market fit and not spend irresponsibly; little else changes. HP and Cisco were founded or funded in downturns, and Google and Amazon hit their stride after 2001. A company of 40 to several hundred people has to plan its finances:
1. **Focus on cash.** Running out of cash is how later-stage companies die.
   - Pad to **three years** of runway, in case capital freezes for one or two years.
   - Raise money now, even at a low valuation, without over-optimizing.
   - Watch expenses with "pound-foolish/pennywise tradeoffs" in mind: don't cut office snacks and then fly sales first class. Keep scaling sales if you must, cheaply.
   - Increase profitability. Fix sales with negative unit margins: if you lose money on each sale, chasing growth accelerates the burn. The exceptions are a consumer network-effect business valued on user growth in its first years, and a huge cash pile you can use to bleed competitors.
   - Fire unprofitable customers or markets.
   - Re-plan hiring for the next 6–12 months, and perhaps shrink. Great people start looking for jobs, so raise your bar.
   - **Real estate is the silent killer.** You can't sublet in a downturn, and a failing subtenant takes its rent with it. Don't sign big multi-million leases or build-outs unless you are profitable or have ongoing access to capital.
2. **Be open with your team.** Ignore the noise. Six months earlier the press said nothing could fail and valuations were $10B; in a downturn it questions even great successes for 6–12 months. Explain the financials, the cash flow and the sales plan, and be a "stable oasis."
3. **Take advantage.** Can you start a price war with cash-strapped competitors? Hire the amazing people being cut elsewhere, and recapture your frugal roots.

"watch your cash, be open with your team, and don't panic." Sam Altman adds that not running out of money can mean making money, not just fundraising.
