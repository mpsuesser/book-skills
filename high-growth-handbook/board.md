# The board

## "Hiring" your board

A cofounder is like a spouse. Board members are like the mother-in-law and father-in-law: you see them regularly, they are hard to get rid of, and they have a huge impact. They are among your most important "hires". Board members help with strategy, with sourcing, hiring and closing execs, and with fundraising, operations and governance. By mid-stage they select, keep or fire the CEO and hold the CEO accountable, and later they staff committees.

A board is made up of three groups:
- VCs;
- independents;
- founders, who hold the "common seats": usually 1–2, rarely 3. An external CEO, and sometimes a COO, also gets a seat.

**Choosing a VC partner.** Gil: "I would always take a lower valuation in order to work with a board member or VC partner I really like, rather than a higher valuation and a lesser board member." The fund invests, not the partner, so a firm can swap partners on you. A junior partner may arrive as a board observer, pitched as "more bandwidth to your team from our firm", or a less valuable partner may be swapped in if you're doing poorly. Either way, you end up training them.

## Choosing an independent board member: eight steps

1. **Write a job spec.** Weigh these against each other:
   - **Experience.**
     - Operating: scale, and process and management best practices.
     - Market: domain knowledge, information flow, intros.
     - Functional: an ex-CFO, a VP of international sales.
   - **Involvement with other high-growth companies, optimally as a founder.** You want someone who has seen the path from "two people in a coffeehouse to a multi-thousand-person organization" and lived through existential crises. They know that early "each bit of execution is an act of sheer will, rather than an act of momentum", and that later there is too much momentum to steer the ship easily. If you have only one independent slot, use it for a current or former entrepreneur. Such a person relates to the founder's emotional state, answers "stupid questions" without condescension, and counterweighs the VCs: if they are successful enough, the VC has no leverage over them.
   - **Raw intelligence.**
   - **Business and strategic sensibility:** the strategic landscape, M&A at scale, pricing, team management.
   - **An entrepreneur-friendly orientation.** VCs suggest a "friend of the (venture) firm" who will vote with the VC "when shit hits the fan". At that point the independent seat has become an investor seat and you have lost control. Look for someone the VC respects who is an entrepreneur at heart, ideally with a long preexisting relationship with you.
   - **The respect of investors.** They should remind VCs to vote in the company's interest and keep the VCs "honest", without being a rubber stamp for the founder.
2. **Agree on the spec with your investors.** This lets you call out a friend of the firm with no relevant background, and lets them call out your best friend from high school.
3. **Create a prioritized list** through a board search firm, or through investors, advisors and entrepreneurs. "Your board members are optimally people that you wish you could hire for the company, that are truly out of reach otherwise."
4. **Spend time getting to know them, over many months.** Push back on investor pressure to fill the seat; you wouldn't rush a crappy engineer "just to fill the spot". Ask:
   - What are the key directions for the company? Do you align, and do they have insights?
   - How will they help, and what are they good and bad at?
   - What are their goals, and how does this board role fit them?

   Then **ask them to do something for you**: a consulting project, or something lighter such as an intro, help structuring a deal, or advice on a live strategic issue.
5. **Check personal rapport and attitude.** You want someone you would call "at midnight on a Friday", someone you'd be excited to start a company with. Avoid these types:
   - the **condescending, gray-haired operating executive** who talks about a "bunch of kids" needing "adult supervision", and may push to replace the founder with a visionless "operator";
   - the **micro-manager**, who confuses a board seat with being your boss;
   - someone in it for the financial reward, or for the stature of joining a board;
   - someone who wants to network with your investor board members, and will side with them;
   - **the VC crony.** The signs:
     - the VC has worked with them a lot, sits on their board, or placed them before;
     - they worked at several companies backed by that VC, or sit on boards with the same VCs;
     - they make generic comments and have no relevant experience;
     - they are likely to get their next job through the VC (a VP sales who wants a CEO role).
6. **Check alignment of vision.** Will they support the company or second-guess it? Will they flip from long-term to short-term thinking under pressure? Look at their record: what happened to their companies, did they sell early and why, and are they thoughtful in hindsight?
7. **Check references** on integrity and on what they are actually helpful with, including from entrepreneurs whose boards they sit on.
8. **Finalize.** The usual structure is **"common nominates, preferred stock approves"**. Naval compares it to the US Supreme Court: the power lies with whoever nominates, so your leverage is in who you nominate.

Independents may lose their usefulness as the company scales, and a public board needs different people. If you control the seat, change the people out.

## Chair of the board

When the CEO and chair roles are split, the chair has the legal ability to call a board meeting independently of the CEO. At an early stage the title is meaningless. Later the chair coordinates: funnels board feedback to the CEO, sets the agenda and follow-ups, and acts as the board's **"tech lead"**. Usually the founder-CEO is chair. The exceptions:
- **A founder who is no longer day-to-day.** This could be Dorsey after his first stint as CEO, or an active founder when a professional CEO is hired, which makes them **executive chair** (Jim Clark at SGI under McCracken). An executive chair is engaged day to day without being operational, and takes strategic areas (Eric Schmidt: government relations, corporate strategy). A founder-CEO with a different, operationally active founder as chair is weird and suggests a power struggle.
- **A VC or early investor** (Don Valentine; Oren Zeev at Houzz).

## Board diversity and evolution

You want shared purpose, calm in a storm, operating, financial and network skills, and high performers. Diversity of ethnicity, gender and sexual orientation helps recruiting, supplies role models and mentors, and broadens networks and perspectives. Boards start undiverse: VCs are mostly white men, and independents are often big-company execs. Gil's tactics:
1. **Recruit a diverse set of angels early,** as bridges to board candidates. Color had a dozen or more female investors, and Susan Wagner, a BlackRock cofounder, went from angel to its first external board member.
2. **Pitch women and minority VCs in every round.**
3. **Tell recruiters and investors** what you're looking for.
4. **Use theBoardlist** (Sukhinder Singh Cassidy).
5. **Mine "most powerful" lists** for intros.

Joelle Emerson's advice when you add a seat is to "just do it": define what the board looks like now, then look for someone with the needed skills who also adds diversity. It takes more time, so commit and don't waver.

**Board evolution.** The board of a young company seeking a meaningful product (product/market fit, the next round) needs different members from a high-growth company. The latter needs operating experience, networks for later-stage exec hires, and broader strategic insight. Each time the board changes, return to the hiring steps above.

## Removing board members

Bad members range from benign but useless (generic advice) to destructive:
- leaking to the press;
- fomenting factions under the CEO;
- derailing financings;
- pushing nonsensical strategy;
- poaching execs for other portfolio companies;
- advice that drives a cofounder out.

Investor members are harder to remove than independents.

**Removing VC board members.** VC seats are contractual rights in the financing documents, so you can usually remove one only at a **time of transition or leverage**: a financing, a major change of direction, or an IPO. "simply asking an investor board member to step down is unlikely to work." There are three tactics:
1. **Change the overall composition of the board to reflect the company's maturation.** Ask several early members to step off at once, so it isn't personal ("changing the slate"). Keep the helpful ones, or convert one to an independent. Holdouts come under pressure once the others step down. At a later round, create a seat for a later-stage investor, keep the board small, and plan toward an IPO if one is 18 months or less away. Before the IPO, add independents, operators and specialists (an ex-CFO for the audit committee).
2. **Buy them out.** Liquidity takes 5–10 years, and VCs raising new funds want to show LPs returns. Offer to buy a subset of their stake (as a secondary, or within a later primary round) in exchange for them stepping off. The arguments:
   - selling signals limited belief in the upside, so they should step down;
   - board seats reflect ownership, so a smaller stake means no seat;
   - their investment has been returned, so their governance need has decreased.
3. **Ask the firm to swap the partner.** This works only if you're doing really well and the partner doesn't control the firm (an insight Gil credits to Reid Hoffman). Naval calls going to the firm "going nuclear": a last resort that works only if the difficult person is very junior and exposed internally, and only through a board member who has a relationship with whoever runs the firm. Go to the very top. He compares it to telling a husband you dislike his wife.

These conversations get emotional, because VCs feel they contributed. Be "firm, calm, and consistent." For using a secondary sale to clean up the board, see [`secondaries-and-ipo.md`](secondaries-and-ipo.md).

**Removing independents** is easier, and you may control the seat. Explain why. If the member's primary relationship is with a VC (a crony), it's harder: negotiate with the VC directly, or let the wider board push.

**Seat structures** (set in the financing documents) for electing and removing an independent:
- a board vote, with each member's vote weighted equally;
- a stock vote on an as-converted basis, one vote per share;
- mutual agreement of the common as a class *and* the preferred as a class, by a majority of each.

Removing a crony may need one extra board member, a major preferred holder, or all of the preferred to agree, and may require trading something of value. Once it's agreed, have a lawyer draft the legal documents and board resolutions.

## Reid Hoffman on the board and the CEO

- **What the board is for.** The board holds "in-depth control of what is being set for the future of the company." The line that its only job is to hire, fire and compensate the CEO is partly right but incomplete. The board also sets parameters: the CEO has to talk to it before selling the company, buying a company, or deploying all the capital. A good board is a team in **"dynamic collaboration with the CEO"**.
- **Hiring a CEO** is "bringing on a later-stage cofounder". When replacing a founder CEO, "you're actually looking for a cofounder" with a different skill set. His tests:
  - Would they do this job if you paid them half? Would they refuse twice the pay elsewhere?
  - In the "valley of the shadow" moments, the professional manager says "it wasn't my idea"; the founder says "I'm going to make it work".

  Without the founder's mindset, a new CEO at best manages the asset (asset management). Changing the company's curve takes founder-like risk-taking, moral authority, and willingness to hear "You really screwed up". Deciding the CEO is wrong amounts to saying "We made a really bad investment decision." Bringing in an external expert is usually a bad play.
- **What game you're playing.** The board must know where the company sits between gambling everything and preserving the asset. A startup lives in **default mortality** ("throwing yourself off a cliff and assembling an airplane on the way down"). Once it has assets, the trade-off shifts. Public investors want asset preservation.
- **Red light, yellow light, green light.**
  - **Green:** "You're the CEO. Make the call. We're advisory." Very big things, such as selling the company, still get discussed first. The mistake here is a "young, idiot board member" dictating X, Y and Z.
  - **Red:** this CEO won't be CEO in the future. Succession is collaborative, up to "Bob, meet Sue. Sue is the CEO. She starts today."
  - **Yellow:** the board is questioning whether to stay green. Bad boards leave a CEO in yellow indefinitely, which hamstrings both the CEO and the company. A board in yellow must agree coherently among itself, and agree **exit conditions** and a limited time frame.
- **The board as a team.** Before every meeting, and maybe weekly, a member should ask: "Before I go into the board meeting, what's the thing that I can bring that's most helpful?" Phrase suggestions **as questions** ("the following thing strikes me as a risk. Do you think it's a risk or not?"), so the CEO can answer "That's interesting, but it's not on the short list right now." "do no harm" to the CEO's priorities. A good team is rarely surprised.
- **Selecting members.** Pick for the board as a team; one good player pulling in a different direction breaks it. If a problem member can't be removed, cast the next member as a **catalyst**: another VC with "throw weight" who shifts the dynamic. Every member should **amplify the CEO**. The test: would the CEO want to spend an hour or two a week with this person on the hardest problems? If yes, their area of expertise matters less. Alternatively, fill a gap the CEO has identified, such as the banking industry or enterprise sales. Look for expertise, networks or ways of thinking you **couldn't hire**; if you could hire it, hire it.

## Managing the board

A well-managed board gives you:
- strategic and operational feedback;
- help sourcing and closing candidates, especially execs;
- ongoing assessment of talent;
- help and alignment on fundraising ("This isn't always the case!");
- CEO coaching;
- confidence that the right CEO is in place.

Meetings exist to help the company and to provide proper governance for all classes of stock. Early meetings cover a handful of fundamental metrics ("Are we running out of money?", "is our product working?") plus strategy, org advice and hiring help. Later meetings take on advanced strategy such as M&A. A larger board is harder to run.

**Before the meeting:**
1. Send the deck and materials **48–72 hours** ahead.
2. With three or more non-founder members, call each one for a **30–60 minute 1:1 briefing** to gather input and let them vent.
3. With several members, hold a board dinner the night before or a meal after, which works best when they fly in.

**Agenda:**
1. Board business, kept short.
2. A big-picture summary.
3. A quick review of key metrics, especially those that bear on strategy (already in the pre-sent slides).
4. Follow-ups from the last meeting, here or after the strategy topics.
5. **Two or three key strategy topics**, which get the bulk of the time.

The pre-calls keep the meeting from rehashing. Which execs attend items 2–4 can become politicized, because attendance signals relative importance, so decide it thoughtfully.

**Board observers and random attendees.** Don't tolerate them unscoped. Negotiate the parameters with the VC partner: the junior person's role, whether they may speak, and which specific items they help with.

**Other interactions:** a board member with IPO or public-company CFO experience coaching your CFO; 1:1s on strategy and org; members interviewing and recruiting execs.

## Naval Ravikant on boards

- **Boards don't build.** The Romans kept a Senate with diffuse power but appointed a dictator for war. A company is a dictator-founder (risk-prone, visionary, able to pivot) reporting to a diffuse board. "No committee ever built anything great", and no board did either. Use the board as a sounding board, not to run the company. The larger it is, the more time goes into syncing it. "Your average VC is a retired entrepreneur; your average entrepreneur is not a retired VC."
- **Size.** Experienced board members "favor private company boards of five or six people or less." Rabois puts it at three to seven, typically five.
- **Seats.** **Don't give up more than one board seat per round.** A two-VC round with two seats leads to a board of 6–8 by series C to E.
  - Put in early term sheets that an investor gives up their seat when a later investor's seat comes in (First Round Capital does this).
  - Smart VCs step off as the IPO nears.
  - In a hot growth round, offer the series A partner the chance to step off while keeping protective provisions, board decks, and no monthly calls.

  Gil adds that a partial buyout is the primary lever, and that early investors want the board association for their brand. "I see too many late-stage entrepreneurs spending literally half their time just doing board management."
- **Cadence.** Hold a board meeting every three months plus a short monthly update call. Skip the fancy deck: bring "a sheet of paper with the big points on it and the big numbers on it", then talk.
- **Manage it or someone else will.** If you don't manage your board, "whoever the next most aggressive board member is will step in and fill that vacuum." The founder-CEO-as-chair model is the most successful. It's odd for a non-founder to chair.
- **A destructive series A member** brought in after a "shotgun process": usually buy them out for more than the stake is worth, and have the most senior good member help. Fragmented senior partnerships at VC firms have killed companies.
- **No permanent board seats.** Make every seat removable, including Naval's own, and negotiate this from the first round. "Otherwise it's marriage with no possibility of divorce." "Nobody is safe, and that forces everybody to behave."
