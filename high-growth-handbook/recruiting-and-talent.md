# Recruiting, onboarding and managing talent

Twitter grew from about 90 to about 1,500 people in two and a half years, so 93% of its employees were new. Adding 500 people a year forces changes to the recruiting org, onboarding and culture: you go "from hiring 10 people a year to 10 people a week".

## Interview practices

- **Write a job description for every role:** what the role does, the experience and background you want, and what you are *not* looking for or care less about. Circulate it to interviewers with a note on the hiring manager's priorities, and refer back to it to correct bad assumptions. An engineer interviewing a BD candidate may not know BD from sales.
- **Ask every candidate the same questions,** so you can calibrate.
- **Assign each interviewer a focus area** beforehand. For a PM, one interviewer takes product insight, another past accomplishments, another culture fit. Spreading 3–4 areas across 3–4 interviewers gets depth. In the second round, double down on the areas of concern.
- **Use work-product interviews:**
  - a coding exercise for an engineer;
  - wireframes or a workflow for a hypothetical product for a designer;
  - a product marketing plan for a hypothetical product for a marketer.

  They can be onsite or take-home. Base them on a hypothetical product, not on your real one, so the candidate doesn't feel they're doing free labor.
- **Score candidates** before interviewers talk to each other. That avoids bias and forces a written stance. Use 1–5 or "hire / no hire", define the scale clearly, and leave out a neutral "no opinion" option.
- **Move fast.** "one of the biggest determinants of candidate conversion is how quickly you interview them and how quickly you can make an offer." Track how long candidates spend at each step.
- **Check references** for everyone; they are "often the clearest signal." Businesspeople tend to name friends, who give glowing reviews, so widen their references to people in other functions. Engineering references are more direct.
- **Build diverse slates** (from Joelle Emerson's firm, Paradigm):
  1. source diverse candidates for each role, and check job-description language and how your website represents the company;
  2. remove interview biases, for example by blinding names and gender at resume review;
  3. offer benefits that matter to underrepresented employees, such as paid parental leave.

## Scaling the recruiting organization

**Early days (3 to 15 people).** At 3–10 people, a recruiter is less useful than founders and employees working their networks and LinkedIn. Founders and early employees should spend **30–50% of their time** on recruiting, and expect to grind through large numbers of candidates. A useful early hybrid hire is an office manager, social media manager or recruiting coordinator. They schedule referred candidates and reach out to passive ones, then hand off to the founder.

**The first in-house recruiter** comes once you add **15–20 or more people a year**. They source, run the process (scheduling, collating feedback, coordinating with the hiring manager), and sometimes deliver offers. A recruiter hires **1–4 engineers a month**, depending on their strength and the company's brand. If you hire fewer than 15 engineers a year, use a part-time or split-role recruiter, referrals, or external firms. A recruiter for non-engineering roles such as sales hires more: those hires are referral-heavy, and fewer high-growth companies compete for them, since everyone is chasing engineers and designers.

A recruiter's effectiveness depends on:
- the startup's brand;
- how good the hiring manager and exec team are at recruiting;
- the breadth of the employees' networks.

"The importance of the hiring manager and other executives being involved in the recruiting process… cannot be over-emphasized." Zuckerberg was known for taking candidates on closing walks.

**Roles at scale:**
1. **Sourcers** research passive candidates, cold call and email them, and hand them off. They rarely stay involved beyond the onsite interview.
2. **Recruiters** coordinate interviews, circle back with the hiring manager, and may extend the offer. Your first few in-house recruiters should have sourcing experience. That works better for specialized engineering roles and means fewer handoffs, less friction and fewer candidates falling through the cracks. Split sourcing from recruiting once you hire many of one type (50 back-end engineers, 30 front-end, 20 PMs).
3. **Candidate researchers** are added as you grow from 100+ toward 1,000+ people. They scrub LinkedIn (for example, for every Google engineer), build spreadsheets, and pass them to sourcers.
4. **Recruiting marketing** produces materials, ads, events, hackathons and the careers website, all to drive inbound candidates. At a startup, an engineering manager or the marketing team does it; it becomes a standalone role at a few hundred people.
5. **University programs** run the cadence for new grads and interns. At a small company, existing recruiters pivot to it for the few months it takes.

**Exec hires** go through a **retained recruiter**, paid an upfront fee or retainer. Use one for roles such as GC and CFO that fall outside the founders' networks. Execs are more willing to talk to a recruiter from a brand-name firm than to an unknown startup. For Rabois on exec recruiters, see [`executives.md`](executives.md).

## Onboarding

A common mistake is to spend months building a candidate pipeline and almost nothing on onboarding.
1. **A welcome letter** to the teams the new hire will work with, cc'ing them. It gives who the person is, their role, who they report to, their goals for the quarter, and one interesting fact as an icebreaker.
2. **A welcome package** that goes beyond the laptop: a management book the company aspires to, a T-shirt or hoodie, a onesie for a new parent, a handwritten signed note.
3. **A buddy** outside the reporting chain, for lunch, introductions and "stupid" questions, for 1–3 months.
4. **Real ownership.** The two biggest obstacles for new hires are a bad relationship with their manager and a lack of ownership. A prior owner who lingers to collect credit has to ramp down. If a launch is two weeks away, the original owner finishes it; over a longer window such as two months, hand over.
5. **Goals at 30, 60 and 90 days.**

## Old-timer syndrome

Early employees can become enormously valuable. Susan Wojcicki was Google employee number 16 and later became YouTube's CEO; Chris Cox joined Facebook as an engineer in 2005 and became its chief product officer. Others overstay: they get too rich and lose their hunger, or their skills and mindset don't scale. They cling to lunches with the CEO and to having a say in every decision.

**Early employees who scale** channel the founders' mindset, so they buy in quickly. They have the trust of execs and peers and know the processes, jargon and culture. They use their old-timer standing to challenge or contextualize convention and to remove rules. The CEO's trust lets them hire, manage and learn from experienced execs. They stay humble enough to learn from "fresh blood" and open to change. **The sign:** they accept that their role and influence will **shrink in the short to medium term** and expand later.

**Old-timers who should move on.** The temptation is to keep giving them chances, or to move them somewhere "less important", which ends in a bad fit. Often the employee wants to leave but feels loyal. The steps:
1. **Identify the problem and whether it can be solved.** The common forms:
   - **They don't evolve with the company.** They fight hiring a sales team, professionalization, or sunsetting a product.
   - **They can't scale into the role they want.** The first marketer is rarely the long-term VP marketing, and the first engineer is rarely the CTO. CEOs tolerate poor leads because of founder-era trust.
   - **They feel left out.** At 12 people they had lunch with the founders. Now they act out by blocking projects or escalating inappropriately to the founders.
   - **They exert power inappropriately.** Their title is a historical artifact, like a CTO from the 10-person days who is still CTO at 1,000 with no reports. Or a cofounder lobbies the exec team out of band.
   - **They get too rich.** Through secondaries or tender offers they now have tens of millions, and are distracted by travel, houses and cars.
2. **Put emotion aside and decide whether there is a problem.** Ignoring or deferring it creates a bad environment, so act quickly and decisively.
3. **Address the issues head on.** A frank conversation may fix it. If it's a mismatch, move them to a role that fits, which may mean a demotion or a departure. A large equity holder should act to maximize the value of that equity. CEOs try to find a place where "they won't do much harm, versus a place they will excel", but "99% of the time the right answer is to part ways." Let them go gracefully; they may be relieved.

## Diversity hiring

A common culture is not "people who look and act exactly like me". It is a common purpose, mission and outlook. Gil's tools:
1. **Recruiting.**
   - Source diverse candidates for every role.
   - Think through the biases in your process. Those biases also block other kinds of hires: a team heavy on back-end engineers may fail to hire a great front-end engineer because its questions are geared against them.
   - Use recruiters such as Jopwell and Triplebyte, and tell firms the gender and race mix you want.
   - Recruit at more diverse campuses.
   - Put women and people of color on interview panels.
2. **Role models:** diversity among investors, the board and execs; mentoring programs; press and speaking opportunities.
3. **Benefits:** a maternity policy, and pumping rooms that are actually reserved and available.

The hardest part is that startups recruit from the incumbents that feed them, and those lack diversity. Look to less common sources, knowing it is competitive and takes longer. On whether the problem is the "pipeline" or bias, Gil's answer is "both".

## Joelle Emerson: diversity is not a nice-to-have

**Know which of four reasons resonates** with your leadership, and design the strategy around it:
1. Diverse teams are stronger at analytical thinking and complex problem-solving. The causal research covers gender, ethnicity and even political affiliation. The correlational research on financial outcomes is less persuasive.
2. You build for diverse customers. When YouTube launched mobile upload in 2012, about 10% of videos came in upside down: left-handed people held their phones differently, and the team had no left-handers.
3. The talent pool is narrow, so be attractive to the widest possible set of people.
4. It is the right thing to do.

**Sourcing obstacles:**
- **Homogeneous networks** of race, ethnicity and education. Invest time outside them.
- **Waiting.** It gets exponentially harder: who wants to be the one person who looks different among 50 who look the same? It's much easier at 5–15 people.
- **Unconscious and conscious bias,** including pattern-matching to whoever held the role before.
- **A "culture of brilliance":** the myth of "the natural" or the rock star. A fixed-trait view lets stereotypes guide decisions; a growth mindset works against that.

**Early easy wins.** Some companies spend **90–100% of their outbound sourcing** on underrepresented candidates, since inbound applicants won't be diverse. Gusto committed to 100%. Post on diverse job boards and with diverse organizations. "you usually only need one or two key hires to make a really big difference", because their networks compound.

**Replace "culture fit."** As usually practiced it is defined inconsistently ("Who do I want to get stuck in an airport with for 12 hours?") and is a poor filter. Use values alignment, work-style alignment, or **"culture add"**: "Who's going to bring what we most need?" At Color, Gil defined culture fit as excellence and pragmatism.

**Structured interviewing, in four steps:**
1. Articulate the relevant qualifications for every role, technical and non-technical.
2. Design specific questions that assess each qualification, and align the team on them.
3. **Limit the domains each interviewer assesses.** Each one answers "Does this person meet what we need on these two things?", not "Should we hire?" A heavy cognitive load pushes interviewers toward shortcuts.
4. **Write rubrics** for answers and work samples. A great answer hits these three things, a medium one hits two, a bad one hits one or none. This limits confirmation bias and the halo from a resume.

**Job descriptions.** Watch for gendered language and for innate-ability language such as "brilliance". Women are less likely to apply to descriptions written in a fixed mindset than to ones about learning and growth. Also consider where the job is posted.

**Belonging.** People who question whether they belong perform worse, engage less and leave. Most cultures were built for the dominant group. **"Ambient belonging"** is built or broken by small signals: posters, social events (all happy hours excludes people with outside obligations and people who don't drink), office design, and benefit images on the website (a gym icon showing a man's arm). In about 30% of the offices Emerson visits, she feels she would not fit in.

**Feedback.** New managers are bad at feedback, so consider vendor coaching, such as required monthly 1:1 sessions. Give them templates contrasting good and bad feedback. Bad: "You're a good communicator. You did a good job on that project." Good is **process-oriented**: it names what they did ("keeping people up-to-date on the status of your project and being helpful to your coworkers").

**Warning signs.** At about **50 people**, start employee surveys. Asking whether people plan to be there in a year predicts who will leave. Once the numbers allow, cut results by demographic: women vs men on manager quality, African-American vs white engineers on advancement. Before then, use brown-bag lunches on culture, monthly or quarterly listening events with leaders, and monthly two-hour inclusion office hours.

**Performance process.** Companies wait too long because of bad past experiences, and without a process, outcomes default to bias. Plan, for example, to start a formal process at 50 people, with light rigor in leveling, promotion and comp before that ("ask these three questions"). Aim for fairness and *perceived* fairness.
