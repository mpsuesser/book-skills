# Secondary sales, 409A and RSUs, and going public

## Primary vs secondary

A **primary** sale means the company receives money for newly issued shares. A **secondary** sale means someone buys shares from a holder other than the company. The distinction is about who sells, not about which kind of share is sold (common or preferred).

**Who wants to sell, and why:**
- **Employees:** a hospital bill, a house, diversification.
- **Early investors:** returning money to LPs, especially when they are raising a new fund, and earning their **carry** (the share of a fund's returns a VC earns; no capital returned means no carry).
- **Founders:** diversification. Sales "tend to align founders to focus on the long-term", because they remove personal financial worries.

**A valuation of $500 million to $1 billion is the usual tipping point** for selling, for three reasons:
1. It takes two to five years to get there, and in that time people have life events.
2. 1% of the company may now be worth $5M–$10M, so diversification becomes meaningful.
3. Employees' belief in the remaining upside fades. Chaos and competition look like limits on the multiple, and small stock holdings are now worth real money.

**Founder sales** are increasingly acceptable. The norm is **up to 10% of holdings, or up to $5–10 million, whichever is lower**. Selling more than 10%, especially while operationally involved, sends a negative signal. Most founder sales happen at multi-hundred-million valuations. Earlier, at mid-to-high tens of millions, founders sometimes sell a few hundred thousand dollars' worth to clear school debt or build a cushion. Most founders are glad they took some money off the table.

**Naval Ravikant on founder secondaries.** Founder and investor incentives are diverging. A $100 million exit makes a founder happy but not an investor with an ever-larger fund. The industry is more hit-driven, so the odds of any one company working out have fallen: maybe 1 in 10 in 1999, perhaps 1 in 50 now. Incubator graduates come at you like "the locust swarm of startups". A founder gets only a couple of shots on goal, so good VCs who ask a founder to go for a billion-dollar exit let them take some off the table along the way. "Secondary markets are here to stay."
- **When it's legitimate:** you don't want to take substantial liquidity while your investors lose all their money. Once you are convinced, almost beyond a shadow of a doubt, that the company is worth more than the **stack of liquidation preferences**, it's legitimate to ask. That conviction comes from revenue or cash flow, or from acquisition offers above the preference value.
- **How it usually starts: an acquisition offer.** The investors want you to turn it down, and you're tempted. A smart investor responds by letting you take something off the table.
- **Timing:** generally not at seed, A or B. It's common from the C round up, and happens in B rounds when the company has made substantial progress.

## Regulate secondary sales early

Unmanaged secondaries backfire in several ways:
- Large transactions can affect your 409A valuation.
- Misbehaving investors end up on the cap table.
- The **"random dentist"** buys at a premium, then harasses the company for information. This really happens.
- Bad secondary funds cause trouble. An SEC inquiry into pre-IPO Facebook trading involved Felix Investments.

The remedies:
- charter or agreement changes that prevent secondary sales;
- a **right of first refusal (ROFR)** on all shares;
- a contractual requirement for board approval of sales;
- a preferred buyer program or tender offers.

Some standard documents leave out restrictions entirely. Gil says Fenwick & West is notorious for omitting the ROFR, so check yours.

Companies typically move through stages. First come one-off sales (an employee caring for a sick parent). Then random buyers enter the cap table and demand rises. Then the company sets up a preferred buyer program or tender offers.

## Four kinds of secondary sale

1. **One-off sales.** Steer the seller to a known buyer, either by simply asking or by making life difficult through exercising or delaying the ROFR. Steering can backfire, so establish **preferred buyers** (trusted existing investors) early, along with rules for how much employees and investors can sell and when. Promise a tender and most people will wait **6–12 months** for a company-sanctioned sale.
2. **Selling as part of a funding round.** An oversubscribed round lets investors blend preferred stock with common or early preferred. Some funds are limited in how much common they can buy, by their LP agreements or SEC filings.
   - **Price:** common is discounted **20–30%** from the preferred price. DST bought Facebook common at a $6.5B valuation, a 35% discount to the $10B preferred. Hot companies may get parity.
   - **409A:** the company stays at arm's length, so the sale doesn't force a 409A repricing of all common stock and future options. Talk to lawyers.
   - **Size:** secondary is usually **no more than 20% of a round**, because of VC risk tolerance and regulatory limits.
3. **Preferred buyer programs.** These can be informal (suggesting funds to sellers) or formal: a term sheet, LOI or binding agreement, dedicated funds, a pre-set price range (for example, a 10% discount to the last round), and an assigned ROFR.
   - **The ROFR delay:** the company typically has a 30-day ROFR at the negotiated price, and the assigned fund then gets a second 30 days. That **60-day-plus delay** makes buyers and sellers nervous, and the ROFR drives other buyers out, reducing secondary demand.
   - **Use in acquisitions:** founders and investors of companies you acquire can sell the stock they receive to the preferred buyer, or into a tender, without using company cash.
4. **Tender offers.** A large, coordinated event in which one or more buyers purchase at a preset price, typically institutions such as BlackRock, Goldman Sachs, DST and Fidelity. Sizes range from tens of millions to billions of dollars. The steps:
   1. **Estimate demand** to size the tender, with paperwork restricting further sales by participants.
   2. **Sign a term sheet** with the buyer or buyers at a single price.
   3. **Decide who can sell and in what order.** For example, in a $50M tender, current and past employees get first dibs on up to 20% of their holdings each. If they fill it, investors and founders don't sell.
   4. **Hire an administrator,** such as a bank like Deutsche Bank. The cost is partly covered by a transaction fee charged to sellers (about 1%).
   5. **Notify eligible sellers** of the price and the window (20 or 30 days).
   6. **Close** when the window ends.

**Information sharing.** Share basic financials with large buyers on your preferred list or running your tenders. They will sign an NDA, and some will be long-term shareholders. Restrict information for small, random additions to the cap table, except what you are legally obligated to give every shareholder. By a **$5 billion+** valuation, you'll need someone whose job, at least part-time, is regulating secondary transactions and the secondary ecosystem, unless you locked it down early.

## How much employees may sell

There are three models:
1. **A percentage limit,** for example 10–20% of each person's holdings. Most stock stays held, so incentives stay long-term. But at a company worth many billions, or for very early employees, even that can be tens of millions of dollars. That is a disincentive to keep working, and it creates a **two-class system** before a true liquidity event, which is culturally jarring.
2. **A dollar limit.** Facebook let employees sell up to $1 million, an amount that is life-changing but not distracting. It may push some early employees to leave if leaving unlocks larger sales, though people who leave solely to sell probably wouldn't have stayed much longer anyway.
3. **A hybrid, "whichever comes first":** 10% or 20% of holdings, or $1 million, whichever is reached first. Someone holding $20M can sell $1M. Someone holding $1M can sell 20%, which is $200,000.

Under any model, companies usually add two limits:
1. The employee must have been there at least a year and/or passed their vesting cliff, since cash is very hard to claw back.
2. Sales are capped at what has vested, or at a proportion of it ("no more than 20% of what has vested") in official programs such as a tender.

Refresher grants are usually a fraction of the initial grant, with exceptions for outliers and promotions such as IC to VP. So most of an employee's stock value comes from their earliest grant. "it is always striking how much the financials matter, even if people claim otherwise."

## Investor sales: an opportunity to renegotiate

When an early angel wants to diversify, or a fund wants to return money to its LPs before raising its next fund, you have leverage to revisit old terms:
- **Information rights.** As an investor's stake shrinks, argue that their information rights should end. In some rare cases the biggest leaks have come from early investors trading favors with TechCrunch, not from employees.
- **Board participation.** Board representation should reflect ownership, and an investor who was a great advisor at ten people may have no insight for a later-stage company. Ask their board member to step down as part of the sale, or convert the seat from a preferred seat to an independent one. A large secondary to a preferred buyer, or a tender, is a unique chance to clean up the board.
- **Cleaning up the cap table.** Offer small early angels an **"all or nothing"** sale of their entire stake, and consolidate their many line items into one buyer.

View investor secondaries as a chance "to claw back rights and clean up governance in a manner that is positive and mutually beneficial for all parties". **Lock up future sales** too. Whenever anyone sells, have them sign a contract that prevents future sales without the company's sanction, and update the bylaws and employee documents to allow employee sales without inviting bad actors onto the cap table.

## 409A and RSUs

The **409A** is the valuation analysis that sets the strike price of your stock options. If large, company-sanctioned common transactions happen at high prices, you'll have to reset the strike price upward, which weakens your ability to reward employees. Work out your approach to secondaries and the 409A with your lawyers.

**Move to RSUs once your valuation is over $1 billion and you are within 18–36 months of going public.** Early on, RSUs are less tax-efficient for employees than early-exercising options and holding for capital gains. Once the strike price is high, though, early exercise costs too much for most employees, or would be unwise. In the '90s, people exercised, paid a big tax bill, and then saw no upside, or lacked the cash for the tax. Within a few years of an IPO at a high valuation, RSUs are about as tax-efficient as options. They avoid the problem of covering the exercise price and the loss if the stock falls. RSUs are never "below water": they are effectively shares, so they always have value, while options at a very late-stage company can end up worthless.

## Selling your stock: the employee's seven steps

1. **Find out whether you can sell.** Check the option plan, the charter and other company documents, and ask the general counsel, the finance person handling secondaries, or the CFO. Most companies have a 30- or 60-day **ROFR**. Once you've agreed a price with a buyer, the company, and then perhaps its investors, can buy your shares at that price instead. Either way, you sell. Waiving the ROFR typically takes about 30 days. Before an IPO, companies often halt trading for a few months, and after it you are locked up for another six months.
2. **Decide how much to sell.** Consider:
   - **Employment status.** Most companies require you to exercise within **90 days** of leaving or lose your options. Decide whether to sell just enough to cover the taxes on exercising, or more.
   - **Diversification.** If 99% of your net worth is in company stock, protect yourself against a black-swan event. Gil knows Zynga employees whose net worth fell 70% with the stock price.
   - **Cash needs.** A house, a car, your kid's school. Filing for an IPO doesn't mean going public quickly, and the lockup adds half a year of uncertainty.
   - **Taxes.** Timing matters; people sold in 2012 to avoid the 2013 tax increases. Talk to an accountant.

   Many people sell **20–50%** of their stakes before an IPO. Selling everything is possible, but it trades away the upside for security.
3. **Find a legitimate buyer.** The market includes dedicated secondary funds, hedge funds, family offices, angels, dentists, and "a random assortment of yahoos". Choose a buyer who:
   - **has the funds:** ask for proof of funds on a large transaction, or use a well-known investor;
   - **will move quickly:** avoid layers of decision-makers, such as fund committees that meet only periodically;
   - **has bought private securities before,** if they're an individual, so they understand the process and risks;
   - **won't be a pain to the company.** A dentist from Ottawa may pay more, but may also sue the company for no good reason. That hurts the value of your remaining stock and your relationship with your employer. Deal with random buyers only if you don't mind burning bridges;
   - **the company will approve quickly.** Some funds have had SEC problems, and the company may not want them on the cap table.
4. **Set your price.** Private transactions are illiquid and volatile, and rumors about prices circulate constantly.
   - Ask colleagues what they got in transactions that actually **closed**. "Unclosed transactions are often meaningless."
   - Don't be greedy. Optimize for speed of closing at a price you're comfortable with; five cents a share doesn't matter at $18 a share.
   - **Common is discounted about 30% from the last preferred price,** because preferred gets paid first in a low exit. After a $240M round, expect a price based on a $160–200M valuation. If months have passed and the company has progressed, you can often get the preferred price. A super-hot company can command a premium; ask the company what it thinks it's worth now. The spread disappears as the company matures. When a VC buys founders' common alongside its preferred in a round, it may pay the same price for both.
   - **IPOs breed volatility.** Secondary prices run up sharply in the weeks before the company halts trading ahead of an IPO, and can exceed the post-IPO price (as in Facebook's first year as a public company). Don't hold out for more. After filing, a company may wait months or quarters to go public, and then you're locked up for six months.
   - Expect prices to move semi-randomly. A founder dumping a big block to diversify depresses prices for everyone.
   - Remember taxes, including any qualified small business benefits.
5. **See whether company counsel will run the transaction.** You'll need a **stock purchase agreement** (SPA), which the company may supply, or which a major Silicon Valley law firm can draft. In a large market with many buyers and sellers, you may also need a third-party legal opinion that you own the shares, because some people end up selling shares that don't exist.
6. **Include the basic terms:**
   - the buyer must fund within X days (for example, wire the money within a week of closing) or you can void the deal;
   - the seller can't back out;
   - if the company blocks the sale or exercises its ROFR, the contract is void.
7. **Consider more complex structures.** Some funds offer a loan against your shares with a split of the upside, or buy the shares outright with an upside split above a threshold. Example: you sell at $25 and split any appreciation above $30. If the stock reaches $32, you end up with $26 a share.

## Why go public

Many companies of the 2007–2012 era tried to stay private as long as reasonable. Gil lists the benefits of going public:
1. **Hiring, retention and conversion.** Comp packages can go down after an IPO while more candidates accept, because liquid stock looks de-risked. Retention rises among newer employees and may fall among old-timers, who are now liquid, but that group is small and likely to leave eventually anyway.
2. **M&A.** Liquid currency lets you buy companies without haggling over what your stock is worth.
3. **New capital.** Public markets can supply outsized funding, as Tesla shows. In tight markets this can save the company: Opsware went public in 2001 after private capital had dried up.
4. **Partnering and selling at scale.** Public companies are taken more seriously.
5. **Fiscal and business discipline.** At its IPO, Facebook treated monetization as a low priority. After the first serious post-earnings drop in its stock, Zuckerberg moved engineering into ads. The resulting valuable currency paid for Instagram and WhatsApp.

And the drawbacks:
1. **A larger, more complex board,** with committees to staff. Small boards are more nimble.
2. **Financial and other controls.** Some help the company, but many slow it down.
3. **The employee mix shifts.** Later-stage employees are more risk-averse, and a public company attracts people of the same caliber with a more conservative risk profile. Counter this by acquiring entrepreneurial companies and integrating them culturally, or by having leaders actively encourage risk-taking and rule-questioning.

**The IPO process.** Appoint an IPO team and a **directly responsible individual (DRI)** to project-manage it, with the CFO overseeing the whole IPO. Talk to CEOs and CFOs who have taken companies public. For timing relative to market cycles, see [`financing.md`](financing.md).

## Keith Rabois: go public as soon as you can

- **"companies should go public as soon as they can."** Transparency and accountability are always good, and preparing to go public creates discipline and focus. Once public, you have tools for incremental financing and M&A. Facebook, still private, couldn't settle the value of its own stock in a debate over a $500 million offer for Twitter, and the deal didn't happen.
- **The reasons not to go public are "basically excuses":**
  - *Innovation:* five of the six companies people name as most innovative (Google, Facebook, Tesla, SpaceX, Apple, maybe Amazon) are public.
  - *Distraction by the stock price:* employees get distracted by gossip, food and crypto anyway. With a public stock you at least see what they see, and can countersteer.
  - *Cost:* SOX compliance comes a year after the IPO, by which point you've raised hundreds of millions and can pay "four or five more accountants and some software".
  - *Profitability* is not a gating factor; most tech companies went public unprofitable.
  - PayPal's searing IPO (filed the day before 9/11, then attacked by a state regulator) taught some, including Peter Thiel, the wrong lesson.
- **Effects on people.** After Yelp went public, retention of engineers and other employees rose by double-digit percentages. Gil cites a recruiting firm's data showing that public companies close a higher share of candidates with lower offers. It gets easier to hire recent graduates who want a stable company for at least two years, partly because parents and partners see a public company as stable. Employees become more compensation-focused.
- **The surprise for first-time founders: board drag.** Board meetings get more process-oriented. Rabois advises a startup board of three to seven, typically five. Committee requirements (audit, nominating) enlarge it, which turns debate into presentations. Countermanage this by not making the formal board meeting your only strategy session.
- **Signs you're ready.** **Predictability**: you can easily forecast the next quarter and the next six months. That means you understand the levers of the business, its "X x Y x Z" equation, well enough to know how each part drives the result (say, contribution margin). The N must be large enough that variance is rare, at a scale of about **$50 million in revenue**.
- **Market cycles matter.** Ignoring them is "somewhat naïve". Most founders have grown up professionally in the long, hot market since 2008. Debt can be the oxygen of a business model (Opendoor, Affirm, Upstart), and its price can change radically overnight: Bill Me Later and Zappos had to sell while performing well because their access to debt dried up. "I think of money, of capital, like oxygen. Imagine if we all had to live our lives and pay for every breath." In a hot market everyone treats capital as free; having lived through cycles means always calculating its cost.
- **Process tactics.**
  - The earlier you have a CFO, the easier it is. Don't hire one prematurely, but hire a great one opportunistically.
  - It can go faster than the canonical year. Rabois has seen it done in three to four months, which takes intense focus and an experienced CFO or GC. Six to nine months is reasonable, and about a year of preparation is a good idea.
  - Name a **DRI**. At PayPal a corp dev director or VP "quarterbacked everything". Pick someone trusted and credible in the organization, who can stitch the work together and motivate people.
