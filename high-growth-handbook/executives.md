# Building the executive team

## When you need executives

Signs the company is breaking:
- communication gets gummed up;
- product teams lose coordination;
- there is no time to think;
- the hiring process falls apart, with weeks before candidates get a follow-up;
- the sales pipeline depends on you plus inexperienced ICs.

Promoting inexperienced existing employees into these roles usually fails. When the first seasoned exec works out, "All sorts of things will magically just get done." When one doesn't, you get a culture misfit, a hire too senior who spins their wheels, or your best people quitting.

**Hire for the next 12–18 months.** A 10-person engineering team that will be 30 in a year doesn't need an SVP who ran 1,500 at Salesforce; that person is bored and spins their wheels. For 20 engineers, hire someone who has led 50–100 and can scale, not a VP who ran 10,000 (Ben Horowitz's *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* covers this). Plan shorter and the person can't scale before you would have to replace them; plan longer and you over-hire into a bad fit.

## Six traits of a great executive

1. **Functional expertise** right for the current scale. They know the major issues and the common failure points, and the org respects them. You can over-hire as well as under-hire: Ruth Porat for a pre-revenue 10-person team would be wrong.
2. **Ability to build and manage a team.** They recruit exceptional people and build a recruiting culture. They know how to motivate their function (sales and PM respond to different incentives) and manage its kind of people (designers differ from support staff). They have run an org deep enough for the next 12–18 months.
3. **Collegiality.** They work well with peers, do right by the company over themselves, and fit the culture.
4. **Strong communication skills,** across the company, with the CEO and other execs (exec-to-founder communication is "its own magical art"), and with the board, partners and customers. They have **"cross-functional empathy"**, Mark Williamson's term.
5. **Owner mentality.** They own and solve problems and make their function a **"black box"**: the CEO can engage with it without managing it day to day.
6. **Smarts and strategic thinking.** They ask "What does a strategic X org look like?" and can make their function a competitive advantage. Most companies are great at only one or two things; Apple's are hardware design, supply chain and marketing. Prefer **first-principles thinkers** to people who re-implement their last job.

**Define the role and meet people who do it best.** Founders often don't know what a CFO, GC or VP sales actually does.
1. Through investors, advisors and founders who are ahead of you, meet 3–4 excellent people in the role, at companies a few years ahead or at excellent public companies.
2. Ask what to hire for at your size and on your 18-month road map: the traits that matter, interview questions, work projects and tests, and reference questions.
3. Write it down and share it with the interview team: what to look for, what to select against, and the culture you need.

**Expect to get it wrong once or twice.** Facebook turned over its first exec team before Sandberg arrived. Founders wait too long out of fear. Give yourself permission to make mistakes as long as you correct them quickly.

## Keith Rabois on hiring, managing and firing executives

**Calibrating outside your discipline.** Use Brian Chesky's technique: find the five best people in Silicon Valley in that role and have coffee with each. That benchmarks A+ against B+ and lets you triangulate candidates. Rope in board members and investors to interview early, since it's high-leverage for them. Have a trusted friend with a different background interview the finalists; Rabois had Max Levchin interview VP engineering finalists.

**Executive recruiters.** Use one for C-level and VP+ roles, not directors. They know who is on the market and what people's reputations and past references were, and they install a process (weekly meetings speed it up). At roughly **$100k** they are worth it for a great CFO or VP engineering. The talent partners at VC firms are free. The drawback: a recruiter's incentive to close biases them toward easier-to-close candidates over your dream candidate, who may take a year.

**The key criterion: a magnet for talent.** Can they immediately upgrade the org's talent? Reference-check execs thoroughly; there is "no reason ever" to skip it. Always ask: **"If this person joined my company, would you join?"** You want an "Absolutely, yes" with a spark. If the spark is missing, probe.

**Pull the trigger at about 70% confidence.** Zero-defect hiring is too conservative. Below 50% is reckless, and waiting for 100% means you take too long and lose candidates. Track your **false negative rate**.

**Know within 30 days, certainly by 60,** whether an exec is working. It takes longer in complex businesses such as Opendoor. Early struggle is a major red flag. Making them successful is the CEO's job: carve out **10, 15, maybe 20 percent** of your calendar for a new exec. Mistakes are rarely fatal; Zuckerberg replaced his entire management team starting in 2007.

**Early signs an exec isn't working:**
- They don't take ownership of decisions or are too passive. A direct conversation giving them license may fix it.
- People **circumnavigate** them and bring problems back to you. This can also be residue of the old regime after a political change.

The positive sign: "The people who are thriving—at any level, junior to senior—tend to have people approaching their desk all the time," even from outside their org.

**Six to twelve months ahead of the curve** (a lesson from Chesky). A great exec works 6–12 months ahead; a decent one delivers in real time or 1–3 months ahead. Five years ahead doesn't work. A great exec looks flawless and always has an answer, like a software architect with a ready plan for "If we went on Oprah today and got 10X the amount of traffic". Ask about lead times for the worst and best cases over the next 3–12 months. If the strategy needs fifty more engineers, a great VP of engineering sees it and starts recruiting a year in advance.

**Mistakes vs not scaling.** Tolerate about **one clear mistake in an 18-month to two-year frame**. With several, ask yourself "What am I doing wrong?" Distinguish:
- a **mistake**: with the same information, they would not make the same decision again;
- **someone who doesn't scale**: they would make the same decision again, because their learning curve is flatter than the company's growth slope.

**Who reports to the CEO** depends on the **"seams in the business."** A CEO tiebreak between two functions once a month or quarter is fine; more often, and you should unify those functions under one person. Compromise the org design for an exec's strengths: a product exec who is good at BD can take partnerships or studio negotiations under product. To decide, diagnose the key risks: the 2–3 levers that make or break the company should report to the CEO.

**Number of reports.** Andy Grove (*High Output Management*) put the maximum at five or seven. Rabois: **three to five is ideal, four to seven is scalable**, and a dozen is "totally crazy". He ran 11–13 temporarily at Square while urgently recruiting to unify functions. Such periods should be "measured in months, not years", and ending the violation becomes the number one priority.

**Layering.** Fear of flight risk is the biggest reason founders procrastinate on layering. Rabois's rules:
- Layer someone under another person only if "the person above them is clearly superior."
- Re-report people internally only when the performance gap is clear, both measured and perceived. With a "debatable delta", don't.
- For an external hire, keep your performers by hiring someone **they can learn from**. If you can't, they may need a steep learning curve elsewhere, but that isn't a reason to avoid the hire.
- The alternative is mentoring one high-potential person into the bigger role (VP finance to CFO). It takes a lot of energy, so you can't do it for several people at once.

**The exec team meeting** is "classic high-leverage activity", and more valuable to the execs than to the CEO, because it gives them a stage with peers and lateral understanding of the company.
- Cover about **three topics**, mixing information sharing with actionable discussion and debate.
- **One to three hours** is enough if KPIs and dashboards carry the metrics.
- Circulate notes the night before using the **three Ps: plans, progress, problems**, plus a couple of topics where the CEO wants input.
- Run controversial strategy and trade-off topics (led by the CEO) separately from an **operating review** of KPIs and progress (led by the COO). Gil later split out a separate revenue and metrics meeting.

## Do you need a COO?

VCs used to bring in a gray-haired "adult supervision" CEO. That has shifted to the founder hiring a COO to complement them, on the Sandberg model (Box, Facebook, Stripe, Square, Twitter, Yelp).

**Why hire one:** to complement, operationalize and execute the founder's vision. A COO's typical work:
1. adding executive bandwidth as a business partner;
2. scaling the company (recruiting infrastructure, governance processes);
3. building the exec team and the organizational scaffold (finance, accounting, sales), and helping screen product, engineering and marketing execs;
4. the areas founders lack time, suitability or interest for, typically the "business side": corporate development and M&A, BD, sales, HR, recruiting. Founders keep product, design and engineering, though a sales-focused CEO might hire a product COO instead;
5. shaping the culture for the next phase (Sandberg focused on people development and managerial excellence).

**Why not:**
- A well-rounded team can cover the same ground without the title (Polyvore's CFO owned several areas).
- The COO title sets a very high bar, and you can't hire anyone above a COO, so you lose flexibility as you grow from 100 to 5,000.
- A COO who is out of their depth won't accept a demotion and will leave.

To keep options open, hire under a "general manager" title and convert it to COO later. Another option is replacing yourself as CEO (see Hoffman in [`board.md`](board.md)).

**Who.** The candidate should be strong enough to be a CEO, or have solid GM or functional experience: Sandberg had interviewed for CEO roles, and Box's Dan Levin had been CEO or president of two companies and a GM at Intuit. They must also be excited enough to give up a GM's or CEO's upside. Gil's eight criteria:
1. maturity and lack of ego;
2. chemistry with the founders and CEO, a "mind-meld";
3. past experience **scaling**. Managing 1,000 people is not the same as growing from 20 to 1,000; Claire Hughes Johnson scaled at Google and then Stripe;
4. an entrepreneurial mindset;
5. functional expertise in the areas they will own;
6. the ability to hire, and to manage execs;
7. someone you can learn from (Gates hired senior people to learn from them);
8. a process focus: they install lightweight best-practice processes and craft new ones.

Be explicit about what you keep (say, design, product, marketing, engineering) and what you truly delegate (BD, sales, corporate development, finance, HR, operations). A COO need not run everything you don't. At Microsoft, Gates ran product, Ballmer ran sales, and COO Bob Herbold ran finance, HR, marketing and PR. Not every company needs a COO.

### Aaron Levie on hiring a COO

Box hired Dan Levin as COO in 2010, five years after its founding.

- **Don't frame it as "do we have the right CEO?"** That defines the CEO's job too strictly. "it was actually just my job to make sure that we got all of the problems solved, not that I had to personally solve them all." Firing the founder ("shoot the founder") and bringing in an experienced CEO is the mistake.
- **Every COO job is different.** The COO is "one of the most generalized jobs, but ultimately one of the most specific to the individual and to the company": five companies, five different COO jobs. At Box, the COO owns building the company and its orgs, and the CEO owns strategy, the long run and product.
- **When:** "If you as the CEO are uniquely strong in a couple of areas, and you want to supplement or complement those areas… consider a COO."
  - A sales-oriented founder adds product strength.
  - A product founder adds sales.
  - A founder strong in both adds operational infrastructure.

  Wait for a growth inflection, and don't hire a COO to absorb random busywork. The test: is your time going mostly to pure operations (hiring, goal-setting structures, performance reviews, org issues, process) and away from product strategy, design and customers? That can happen at the 12th employee or at a couple hundred. "Add the COO role when the rate of change is reaching some kind of escape velocity."
- **What background:**
  - Ask what you're not good at and where you're spending time you don't want to.
  - Make sure the **surface area** is large enough. If you only need legal and finance help, hire a GC or CFO, not a COO.
  - "go on a mountain and light some candles" and ask: What are you really good at? What do you want to get better at? What things aren't going to be a competitive advantage for you to personally get good at?

  At 40–50 people, Levie's list was management, org development and adding process. His spec: someone who had seen a couple thousand people and a few hundred million in revenue, had carried large revenue targets, and had run a multifaceted org (sales, marketing, customer success, product, engineering). Then he looked for cultural fit, someone he could divide and conquer with, and someone willing to do a "thankless job" with their ego aside: a person you'd want "in the trenches." Wait for the one or two who really fit. His search, including convincing Levin, took 6–9 months and hundreds of hours.
- **Ownership and onboarding:**
  - Make clear who owns what and communicate it widely. At Box, people, org and process issues go to the COO, with most execs reporting to him, and major strategic, product and brand calls go to the CEO.
  - In the first 30 days, a new COO (or any exec) should interview every employee possible: what's working, what isn't, what to improve, where to take the business.
  - Integrate them into the culture in the first 30–60 days.
  - Get **early wins in 60–90 days**, such as a new process that cuts wasted effort.

  The relationship breaks down "because of swim lanes more than anything", so set crystal-clear ownership plus a process for resolving overlap and gray areas.

## Executive titles

There are two philosophies. Google and Facebook gave **low titles** on joining: VPs from Yahoo or eBay became directors or managers, and startup VPs became ICs or junior managers. Others give big titles to compensate for early risk. Pick one and apply it consistently. Gil favors keeping titles low as long as possible, because that reduces hierarchy and stops VP opinions from being valued over everyone else's.

Candidates want a step up in title. Your tactics:
- use a vague title ("Head of Sales" rather than "VP of Sales"; "General Manager" rather than "COO");
- defer the title;
- agree they get it when titles are rolled out, if they're performing at that level.

It's hard to hire above a CXO title. You can put an SVP, EVP or COO above a VP of sales, but if a VP-level person becomes COO and doesn't work out, you must let them go or demote them, and they quit. It's easier to hire over a director than over a VP.

## Firing an executive

1. **Discuss it with the board.** Call each member 1:1 to cover why, how, the transition plan and severance, and hold a follow-up board call if needed. Some members may have worked with or referred the exec.
2. **Prepare the separation agreement** with lawyers, and decide severance up front. Use a script: **be firm** (it's a done deal), **be professional, be clear on the reasoning**. If you've managed well, it shouldn't be a surprise, because you've already discussed fit, responsibilities and alignment.
3. **Make a transition plan:** who manages the reports, and whether the arrangement is short-term, interim or permanent.
4. **Make a communication plan:** what gets said, when, and to whom. For a high-profile exec, prepare reactive press and agree a common story. "Don't be petty. Allow the executive to leave with dignity and reputation intact."

Gil's idealized timeline for firing a VP of marketing, Bob, all on one Tuesday:
- **9am:** meet Bob and agree on positioning.
- **10am:** tell his direct reports. Give them the background, guidelines for telling their own reports, how exceptions will be handled, and the transition plan. Sarah, who runs sales, will also run marketing permanently, already agreed with her.
- **11am:** Sarah and you meet the marketing team.
- **11:30am:** email the company with the transition plan and objectives, then meet concerned individuals.
- **Friday:** cover it at the all-hands with a well-prepared FAQ.

## Hiring great business development people

BD hires fail in two typical ways. One is the smart, charismatic person who can't get anything done. The other is the networked deal person who leaks value, misses details and signs terrible terms. It's also hard to tell real accomplishment from credit taken.

**Great BD people:**
- **have raw horsepower;**
- **are articulate** with engineers, PMs, lawyers and execs internally, and with the other side's legal, engineering and deal people;
- **are creative and fearless on terms.** They make the "crazy ask": "You never know what someone will give away until you ask";
- **get things done,** with a history of closing complex deals;
- **are structured and can run a deal process.** "Structure is underrated in deal people". The phases are **ideation, pitching, negotiating, structuring, closing, implementing** (Marc Leibowitz). They mine the prospect list, frame the negotiation aggressively, and hold internal prep meetings before external calls. Unstructured BD causes internal churn over every concession;
- **are detail-oriented.** Reid Hoffman reads every word of every contract, including the legal language. A good BD person is part lawyer;
- **put the company first** and work well across the org;
- **are pragmatic and big-picture.** They go 80:20 and know when to walk away: "Some of the best 'deals' are the ones that don't happen";
- **understand what the partner really wants,** as opposed to what it claims, and the market trends that shift leverage;
- **are tenacious,** where bad ones give up at the end to "just close the deal" and leak value;
- **are relentless** and keep knocking on doors;
- **have a moral compass.**

**Bad BD people:**
- are great at selling but bad at follow-through. Only references screen for this: "Raw charisma is drastically overrated by technical founders";
- are disorganized;
- **leak value.** They overthink what's "fair" for the other side, assume what matters to it, and close at all costs;
- don't think like owners ("it doesn't matter," "it's within 20%");
- don't think details matter;
- outsource too much ("that's Legal's problem"), leaving terms buried in legalese or "technical specs";
- optimize for themselves and their network. They make deals too easy so the partner likes them, and chase boondoggles and panels. Pick a small handful of events with goals, rather than assuming "all exposure is good exposure";
- have a **cowboy mentality,** committing to terms externally without internal approval and then getting defensive;
- are emotional, where you need an even keel;
- spin internally. They must "turn it off" inside the company.

**Screening:**
- Walk through their history of deals: what they negotiated, how complex it was, the ask no one believed they'd get, a clever deal hack, and the actual impact.
- Get references from people who worked directly on the deals, plus back channels. Ask about specific deals, relentlessness, creativity and impact, whether the terms worked out or backfired, edge cases that bit, and a radical position that paid off.
- Watch their follow-through and structure during the interview process, and how they negotiate their own comp.
- Ask what they're optimizing for: title, equity or growth.

**A great deal person is not usually a great partner manager.** You need both: one to close the deal, the other to manage the relationship afterward.

## The Naficy seniority framework

Gil's name for Mariam Naficy's rules on where to hire senior people and where junior hires are safe:
- **Engineering is the first function to scale to a senior level.** Never start with the "cheap solution" of an all-young engineering team.
- **Junior is fine where results are visible on the surface to the CEO,** such as design and merchandising.
- **Hidden, hard-to-see areas** such as architecture and systems need senior people.
- **Engineering founders struggle to judge business people** in marketing, finance, the business model and customer-acquisition metrics, where spending on acquisition can go wrong fast. Hire "a really, really good person" there, who is probably not the cheapest.
- **Existential specialist roles** need senior people who have done the job before. Gil's examples are building out a data center and Color's clinical lab; Naficy adds regulatory and legal. Don't go cheap and junior.

## Signs someone isn't scaling

These come from Shannon Stubo Brayton, and apply to any leader:
- exhaustion;
- tardiness, arriving late and discombobulated to meetings;
- intense **micromanaging**, because it's the only thing they can still control;
- getting tactical fast, crossing items off a list rather than taking on the big, chunky strategic issue;
- their team saying they are micromanaging, tired, late and hard to reach. They are "over their skis".

Coaching rarely improves things fast enough. Frame the conversation as not being a failure: they may thrive in a smaller company. Founders avoid the conversation, but when the person opens up, they are often unhappy too. For a temporary leader while you search, see the Wolf in [`org-and-culture.md`](org-and-culture.md). For long-tenured employees who have stopped scaling, see old-timer syndrome in [`recruiting-and-talent.md`](recruiting-and-talent.md).
