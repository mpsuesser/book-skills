# High Growth Handbook

Elad Gil's handbook for the stretch after product/market fit, when a startup grows from tens of people to thousands. Gil worked through hypergrowth at Google, then at Twitter, which bought his company Mixer Labs. He calls the book "an active reference": you go to the topic you face now. It is deliberately "painfully tactical", because "the only good generic startup advice is that there is no good generic startup advice." The first time through, hypergrowth feels to everyone like "a chaotic, scary, stressful shitshow". That is normal.

The book alternates Gil's chapters with interviews. Advice from an interviewee is attributed to them by name, and you should keep that attribution when you pass it on (for example, "Keith Rabois's test is…").

## Core lenses

These run through every chapter. Apply them before reaching for a domain file.

**The three things after product/market fit** (Marc Andreessen):
1. **Taking the market.** Get the product to the whole market and win dominant share, because most tech markets end with one company holding most of the share and the value.
2. **Getting to the next product.** Tech is a "product cycle business": every product gets obsoleted. Taking the market funds the R&D for the next product and gives you currency for M&A.
3. **"Everything else."** Build the company around the product and its distribution: finance, HR, legal, marketing, PR, investor relations and recruiting. This is the easiest category to defer, and the longer you defer it, the closer you come to "catastrophic failure through self-inflicted wounds." Past 50 people on the way to 150, relationships become necessarily impersonal, and without HR "something is going to go badly wrong." You also need a general counsel who can tell the CEO where the line is, and a finance function that keeps cost structure and pricing under control.

**Distribution beats product.** Successful tech companies become **distribution-centric rather than product-centric**: a channel through which many products flow (IBM, Microsoft, Cisco). Product/market fit is often fit with **early adopters**, who come looking for you. If you stay with them you hold about 5% of the market, and someone with a distribution function takes the other 95%. Whatever founders believe about their uniqueness, "six months later there are 20 venture-backed competitors". **Distribution moats** count at least as much as product leads: an enterprise sales team, or a state-of-the-art growth team, is itself a moat. Andreessen asks whether you would rather have a two years' lead on product or on a state-of-the-art growth effort; for many consumer products, it is the growth effort. Andreessen on pricing: "raise prices, raise prices, raise prices". In his words, "The definition of a moat is the ability to charge more". Move from the engineer's one-dimensional view (price equals value) to the "two-dimensional mindset, where higher prices equals faster growth", since margin funds both distribution and R&D. He calls network effects "a little overrated", because they unwind just as fast (MySpace). He sees a lot of claims of data network effects and very little evidence. For the operating version of this lens, see the product to distribution mindset in [`product.md`](product.md).

**A different company every 6–12 months.** At hypergrowth, which doubles headcount every 6–12 months, more than half the company joined within the last year. At Twitter, 90% of employees had not been there 2.5 years earlier. Three consequences follow, and the book applies them everywhere:
- **Hire for the next 12–18 months.** An executive sized for the company five years out is bored and spins their wheels. One sized for today can't scale before you would have to rehire.
- **Organize for the next 6–12 months.** Org structure "is an exercise in pragmatism": there is no "right" answer, only trade-offs, and mistakes can be undone. Tell people that constant change is normal and a sign of success.
- **What worked will stop working.** Celebrate being a "learning organism" (Claire Hughes Johnson).

**The CEO's job.** Sam Altman: "figure out and decide what the company should do and then make sure it does that." The split is about **5% deciding and 95% making sure it happens**, which means repeating what you're doing, why, and how. Aaron Levie: "the most important job of a CEO is just to make sure that the company succeeds". The company has to be good at everything, but you don't. Altman names the number one failure to scale in CEOs: failing to move from doing the work yourself to hiring people and working through them. Patrick Collison lists five CEO responsibilities that can't be delegated:
- steward and final arbiter of senior management;
- chief strategist;
- primary external face, early on;
- almost certainly chief product officer;
- responsibility and accountability for culture.

**Growth has a speed limit.** Collison argues that the ceiling on scaling is the experience and cohesion of the management team, which he calls leadership bandwidth. Growing headcount **more than 2X year over year** is especially hard, and you need a strong case for how you'll avoid "the default outcome of true chaos." Facebook, which he credits with the best culture scaling among megacaps, held to **about 60% a year**.

**Know which game you're playing** (Reid Hoffman). A startup lives in **default mortality**: "throwing yourself off a cliff and assembling an airplane on the way down". Once it has assets (a team, a market position, cash flow), the choice becomes preserving the asset versus risking it to grow. Unsustainable growth bought with a high valuation can flip a company from **default alive to default dead**.

## Thresholds the book attaches to scale

| Point | What the book says happens or should happen |
|---|---|
| 3 to 15 people | Founders and early employees spend **30–50%** of their time recruiting |
| 15–20+ hires a year | Bring in the first in-house recruiter (one recruiter hires **1–4 engineers a month**) |
| ~30 people | Start a weekly staff meeting |
| 50 on the way to 150 | Layer in HR (Andreessen); founding documents matter beyond 50–100 (Hughes Johnson); employee surveys and a performance process start around 50 (Joelle Emerson) |
| ~100 people | First internal comms hire, then **one per 100 people** (Shannon Stubo Brayton); by 100–200, internal comms becomes a function (Collison) |
| 1,000+ people | Consider an APM/RPM program, once a senior PM org exists |
| $500M–$1B valuation | Demand for secondary sales tips over |
| $1B+ valuation | M&A becomes a serious tool; move to RSUs once within 18–36 months of an IPO |
| $5B+ valuation | Someone's job (at least part-time) is regulating secondary trading; at $5–10B+, M&A becomes central to strategy |
| ~$50M revenue, predictable | Rabois's bar for being ready to go public |
| Downturn | Pad cash to **three years** of runway |

## Applying the book

1. **Place the company.** Establish headcount, growth rate, valuation and stage, and what is breaking (communication, coordination, hiring, the CEO's time). Check the thresholds table.
2. **Read the domain file** for each area the question touches. Many questions cross files. Hiring a COO, for example, involves both `executives.md` and `ceo-and-cofounders.md`, and a secondary sale tied to a board change involves both `secondaries-and-ipo.md` and `board.md`.
3. **Answer for the horizon the book uses**: 12–18 months for people and execs, 6–12 months for org structure. Use the book's procedure step by step, its questions and numbers, and its named failure modes. Attribute each interviewee's advice to them.
4. **State the trade-off.** Where the book says there is no right answer, say so, give the pragmatic choice for now, and note that it can be undone.

You are done when the advice names the procedure or test it comes from, the threshold or number that applies, and who in the book holds that view.
