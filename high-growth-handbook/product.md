# Product management and the product to distribution mindset

## What a PM is

A great PM org sets the vision and road map, sets goals and strategy, and drives execution through the product's whole lifecycle. A bad PM org does project management: schedules and tidied-up documents. The **PM** is the "single cross-functional owner directly responsible for the success of a product". Calling the PM the "general manager of the product" or "CEO of the product" overstates it, because the PM is a directly responsible individual without line authority.

**The four responsibilities:**
1. **Product strategy and vision.** This covers:
   - the goal and the customer;
   - the primary features and use cases;
   - success metrics;
   - competitive dynamics and positioning, and differentiation;
   - distribution channels, the business model and pricing.

   The PM is the voice of the customer.
2. **Prioritization and problem solving.** The PM owns the road map and its trade-offs, writes PRDs, and runs road-mapping sessions driven by data and customers. The metrics are agreed and tracked. The PM solves problems such as a legal or regulatory tweak, or a sales concern about competitors or pricing. They trade off design's "pristine, platonic ideal of beauty", engineering's "technical pizzazz", sales' "just give me some shit I can sell", and legal's "this may be risky".
3. **Execution.** The PM manages timelines, resources and obstacles. They:
   - lobby for resources;
   - cut or reprioritize features and keep the road map clear;
   - ask "stupid" questions that cut timelines and work;
   - push back on extraneous internal and external requests.

   Execution continues after launch with maintenance, iteration and **sunsetting**. Deprecation is an art: transitioning customers, handling pricing, managing backlash.
4. **Communication and coordination,** which overlays the other three: status, progress, obstacles and sequencing, through weekly status, leadership product reviews and launch timelines. The hardest part is communicating the **"why"**: a framework for prioritization that every function buys into.

There is natural collaborative tension. Engineering wants the decision power, design thinks PM is redundant, and sales wonders why things are slow and why PM keeps them away from engineers. The PM acts as a **buffer or shield** for engineering and design, for example by consolidating input into one weekly meeting, or by being sales' single point of contact. Sometimes, though, put an engineer in front of a customer.

**Diagnosing a PM stuck in checklists.** If a PM spends their time solely on checklists and project management, one of four things is wrong:
- they are covering for a weak engineering-management counterpart;
- they aren't empowered;
- they don't understand the job;
- they aren't respected.

Most of their time should go to defining the product, prioritizing trade-offs, customers, and launch, iteration and communication. The hard part is telling the right person in the wrong role from a company that doesn't empower its PMs.

## Six traits of great PMs

1. **Product taste:** insight and intuition into customer needs, and the ability to learn a new industry.
2. **Prioritization.** Weigh value against engineering work, a sales-requested product against a customer feature, consumer against SMB pricing. "What is the 80% product… and what singular customer problem does it solve?"
3. **Execution:** the ability to convince and cajole.
4. **Strategic sensibility,** including the end run around the competition. In the 1970s, Intel priced new chips below their cost of goods, anticipating the cost reductions that scale would bring. Customers then bought in volumes projected two years out, which made the low price self-fulfilling.
5. **Communication skills in the top 10%.**
6. **Metrics and data.** "You build what you measure", and even the right metrics can drive the wrong behavior.

## Four types of PM

1. **Business PM.** Synthesizes external customer requests into the road map. Found in enterprise software and in the partner-facing parts of consumer companies. Works with sales, presents to customers, handles pricing and segmentation.
2. **Technical PM.** Works on infrastructure, search quality, ML and other inward-facing products.
3. **Design PM.** Works on consumer apps and UX-centric products. Designers converted to PM may lack training in business trade-offs and want everything pixel-perfect, which is slow. Retrain them toward pragmatic trade-offs between beauty and what the market needs.
4. **Growth PM.** Quantitative, creative and aggressive. They (i) determine the critical levers for adoption and use and (ii) manipulate them. Facebook gained tens of millions of users through email loops, funnel optimization and multivariate testing. Sometimes growth marketing plays this role from under marketing.

The more technical or back-end a product is, the fewer PMs it needs: Google's search infrastructure had few or none, while mobile had many.

**PMs are not project managers.** Don't hire project managers as PMs: they lack prioritization and strategy skills. A high-functioning software org doesn't need project managers, because the engineering manager and the PM cover the work. They are useful for hardware, partner implementations and vendor hardware integrations.

**APM/RPM programs.** Google ran two 12-month rotations and Facebook three six-month ones. Consider a program at **1,000+ people**, and only after you have a solid senior PM org.

## Interviewing PMs

Tailor the interview to the PM type, add generic questions, and add culture questions.
1. **Product insight.** Ask:
   - what products they use daily, and how they'd change one;
   - to design X for a set of users;
   - which features they'd add or drop;
   - what product they'd start a company around;
   - to "design a mobile phone for children".
2. **Contributions to past successful products.** Google had terrible PMs who were "at the right place at the right time", so dig. What was their role in defining and launching the product? Who came up with the features? Who drove pricing?
3. **Prioritization.** Look for their framework more than the trade-offs themselves. Give a scenario with multiple paths, factors and data. Ask about an exec-requested feature they pushed back on.
4. **Communication and team conflict.** Ask:
   - how they sold a vision to leadership;
   - about conflicts with engineering or design and how they were resolved;
   - how they build relationships;
   - for an example of a miscommunication and the process change that followed.
5. **Metrics and data.** Ask:
   - which metrics they tracked and why;
   - what bad behavior those metrics could drive, and how they avoided it;
   - which metrics they'd set for your product;
   - how often they reviewed them;
   - how they evaluate whether a launch succeeded.

**Reference-check every product hire,** PMs above all. PM competence isn't easily tested, so past work is the strongest indicator; use back channels. The best PMs launched things that were otherwise stuck, negotiated trade-offs with engineering and design, and created a big strategic viewpoint.

**Product, design and engineering.** Design owns the optimal visual and user experience. Engineering builds, and its technical road map drives the product as much as the product drives it. Product owns the vision and road map, builds what the user needs, and makes the trade-offs between design, engineering and the business. The PM sits in the middle as the voice of the user and needs trust; one bad PM ruins the perception of the whole role.

## The VP product

The CEO is often the first VP product. Hiring a "process person" to execute the CEO's vision is tempting, but it becomes a mistake as more gets delegated. You want process skills plus a vision that complements or matches yours. The VP product's job:
1. **Drive product strategy, road-mapping and execution,** with the CEO keeping final authority. They set the vision and road map and articulate the strategy:
   - (i) who the customers really are;
   - (ii) what it means to win;
   - (iii) how to differentiate;
   - (iv) how to build compelling, remarkable products.

   They make the strategic trade-offs across functions. The CEO delegates planning and prioritization, then **blesses or modifies** the result.
2. **Create and empower a professional PM discipline:**
   - recruit experienced PMs who have shipped at scale across the lifecycle;
   - represent PM at the exec level as the "central spoke";
   - empower PMs and help them navigate politics;
   - define PM roles and get the whole company to buy in;
   - train and mentor new PMs, including university hires and conversions.
3. **Run cross-company PM processes:** a multi-functional launch calendar, simple PRDs, and cross-functional input.

**Empowering the VP product.** Delegate; don't abdicate. The road map is blessed or modified by the CEO. Support their new processes, team turnover and re-orgs, and a stronger PM role, even when that creates tension with influential teams. **Be patient: three months to come up to speed, and another three months to start being valuable.** That holds for any senior hire. Still expect quick, low-hanging wins in the first weeks and months. Once the CEO sees a great product org at work, PM becomes one of the most valued functions.

## PM processes

1. **PRD templates and road maps.** Engineering owns the technical design doc. PM owns the requirements:
   - who it is for;
   - the use cases;
   - what it solves, and explicitly what it won't solve;
   - the main features;
   - dependencies;
   - wireframes of the user journey.
2. **Product reviews,** held weekly with a common set of key execs to check progress, strategy and launch readiness. Some reviews happen at staffing, to set a baseline of objectives, use cases and road map; others cover major products underway. They resolve uncertainty about direction and trade-offs, gather cross-functional input, and review post-launch metrics and user feedback. Attendees:
   - the PM, who organizes it;
   - the design lead, the tech lead and key engineers;
   - others as needed: sales, BD, support, legal.
3. **A launch process and calendar.** At scale this becomes a standalone forum. Each product has an internal page with its launch date. Each function gives a binary **"ready to launch"** plus its issues (legal: "not ready"), and execs and functional leads weigh in.
4. **Retrospectives after each launch:** what went well or badly, and why. Codify the best practices, and let people air praise, pressure and disagreement in a non-emotional forum.

## Converting people into PMs

Twitter's early PMs came from design, sales, bizops, engineering and partner services. Some thrived; others flailed and left when the org was upgraded. Before you convert anyone, have four things in place:
1. an interview or trial process;
2. the core PM processes, as guardrails;
3. a VP product to manage and train them;
4. seasoned senior PMs to mentor them.

**The common early evolution of a product team,** especially under founders without big-tech experience:
1. The founder acts as PM.
2. The founder delegates to existing employees (designers, bizops, marketers, engineers) converted to PM.
3. With no process and no senior PMs, the role defaults to project management and gets discounted.
4. A VP product is hired, restarts the team and sets up processes, which takes a year or more.

Google's early PMs came from engineering (Marissa Mayer, Georges Harik), marketing (Susan Wojcicki) and general operations (Salar Kamangar), plus senior hires. VP product Jonathan Rosenberg then built the processes and the APM program.

## The product to distribution mindset

A compelling, differentiated product wins the first customers. The customer base then becomes the major asset, for cross-selling and for share of time or wallet. Founders believe their primary competency is product; by this point it is really **the distribution channel and customer base**. "The smartest companies realize they are also in the distribution business".

Examples:
- **Microsoft** pushed Office products (Word, PowerPoint and Excel were each once standalone companies or market segments) and Internet Explorer down its channels.
- **Cisco** resold dozens of acquisitions to enterprises and telecoms. SAP and Oracle did the same.
- **Google** bought homepage placement in Firefox, paid hundreds of millions a year to bundle its toolbar, and paid laptop makers to make it the default search. It bootstrapped Maps (Where2), Gmail, Chrome and Docs (Writely) through that distribution.
- **Facebook** acquired Octazen (email scraping for invites) and Snaptu (100 million feature-phone users) for growth, and accelerated Instagram globally.

**The steps:**
1. Build a product good enough that customers choose you over the incumbent, and build a large user base.
2. Be aggressive, not complacent, about customer growth early, as Google, Facebook and Uber were with their metrics. "Too many companies get complacent about distribution if their core product 'just works.'"
3. Recognize that your customer channels are your primary asset. Build or buy products and push them down the channel (Uber Eats, Jump).
4. Recognize that you can't build everything, so buy more companies. Overcome the internal arguments that it would be easy to build in house, or that integration will be too hard. "most companies buy too few, rather than too many". For how to buy, see [`m-and-a.md`](m-and-a.md).

**Product pickers and architects** (Marc Andreessen). He dislikes numeric 70-20-10 resource rules. "give me a great product picker and a great architect, and I'll give you a great product." The **product picker** is the person who conceptualizes the product, the product originator or PM; the **architect** builds it. Take inventory of how many of each you have; even giant companies may have only 10–20 of each. That number is how many products you can work on. Organize R&D as flat, autonomous teams, each guaranteed a great product person and a great architect. If the founder is the product picker, build the org so the founder has time for it, which raises the outside CEO or COO question. "matrixed is death", and "hierarchies kill innovation". Use Bezos's **two-pizza team** of 5–7 people.

## Hemant Taneja: persona focus, growth rate and building responsibly

Taneja is a General Catalyst investor in Snap, Stripe, Gusto and Livongo, and author of *UNSCALED*.

**Start from a precise persona.** His breakout companies attack markets of **$100 billion or more**, and the ones that get it right could become $100 billion market-cap companies. What they share is that they "identified their target customer persona precisely at the beginning":
- **Stripe:** the developer doing online commerce who needs an API for accepting payments.
- **Gusto:** the modern HR person at a small business who is on social media and can be reached in new ways.
- **Livongo:** a diabetic who checks their blood sugar and works for a self-insured employer.

Narrowing a massive market to one persona is a core reason these companies got it right. Livongo rethought diabetes care from first principles. The incumbents' approach had been apps that captured data more efficiently, while prevalence and costs kept rising. Livongo took the consumer's view and lets people "disengage" from their disease: a connected glucometer, machine-learning monitoring, and interventions on diet or exercise when needed.

**Scale the core persona before diversifying.** Stripe built an "AWS for commerce" stack, but the traction of its core payments API dwarfs everything else. It focused on making sure most new companies start with Stripe, and moved upmarket only once it was great at that. Livongo deepened its offering early, into comorbidities such as hypertension and obesity, because its customers needed it. The rule: "if a company has a large market to go after with your initial target persona, it should maximize scaling there before diversifying and diluting the organizational focus."

**Most of these markets are not winner-take-all.** Large regulated markets that are just opening up make "market leadership" a hard frame. Instead, chart "aggressive but manageable growth rates over a long period of time." There is an **optimal growth rate unique to each company**, set by the "physics of the operations":
- the pace of hiring needed to serve the initial customer versus the next segment;
- how complex the logistics of delivering the service are;
- how capital-intensive it is to diversify.

Companies that diversified too much on cheap capital struggled to raise money or grow into their valuations, because they lost focus on unit economics. Carve out your niche (Gusto had to choose between small businesses of 1–10, 10–100 or 100–1,000 employees), become great at it, and don't worry about other companies' share.

**Building responsibly.** Growth at any cost, from the hacker-entrepreneur who moves fast and breaks things, is ill-suited to products that shape daily life in health, education and finance. Taneja wants the **empathetic entrepreneur**, who understands customers deeply and builds the responsible way of serving them into the MVP, even at the expense of some growth. Startups need "their own startup social responsibility":
- be transparent about how algorithms and machine learning deliver the service;
- build measurement systems showing that success doesn't rest on bias or discrimination;
- measure the product's impact on society in its own dimension (social-emotional learning, chronic care, financial literacy);
- build **"algorithmic canaries"** into products and metrics dashboards to catch unintended negative outcomes before they spread. Examples are a sales optimization that keeps groups of people from learning about the product, managers reviewing older workers with different language, or the product being used in ways you never intended.

Zenefits, Theranos and Uber took shortcuts to grow and put stakeholders at risk. Say up front that weaponizing technology is off the table. With regulators, help design the right policies and stay out of politics. Show self-regulation through **algorithmic accountability**, and invest in a trusting relationship that brings regulators along on data and AI, because "less regulation implies more innovation." Taneja believes we are "a decade into a thirty-year cycle" of digitizing these sectors. Pay less attention to talk of bubbles and more to that shift, and think systemically about what your products add up to. On philanthropy, start early with mentoring time (he cofounded TUGG to have the tech community mentor social entrepreneurs) and add money as success comes.
