# The CEO and the cofounders

## What the CEO does

Textbook duties: set and communicate direction and strategy; hire, train and allocate people against that direction and maintain the culture; raise and allocate capital; serve as **chief psychologist**. In practice, a CEO in hypergrowth has little time for strategy until they have a strong exec team and control of their own time. Gil reduces the job to three tactical duties: **manage yourself, your reports and your board** (for the board, see [`board.md`](board.md)).

## Managing yourself

**Learn to delegate.** There are four ways to learn:
1. Hire an experienced manager and watch how they work. Give your most independent people the most rope, and hold regular 1:1s. The best execs are three things:
   - a **router**, who sends items to others and ends meetings with few or no action items for themselves;
   - a strategist;
   - a problem solver, who spots work going off track and dives in.
2. Trial and error. Learn to tell a report who is flailing (overworked, rumpled, late to every meeting) from one with slack, and resize their responsibilities to match.
3. A formal or informal mentor, or a dinner group of CEOs at the same stage.
4. An executive coach. Most are bad and some are good. Find one through other entrepreneurs, or use a semi-retired exec who has run large teams.

**Signs you aren't delegating:**
- You leave meetings with many action items for yourself.
- Someone "owns" an area, yet after 4–8 weeks you still do most of the work or weigh in on every decision.
- You feel you must jump on every email thread or attend every meeting.

Over-delegation also happens: abdication, avoidance, or chasing the external spotlight. It is rarer.

CEOs fail to delegate for three reasons: they don't know how, they have no trusted lieutenants with the skills for this scale, or they are stuck in small-company work habits. Before each task, ask "Do I really need to do this? Or can someone on my team do it instead?" When in doubt, force delegation, and set a weekly goal for the number or share of meetings dropped and items handed off.

**Audit your calendar.** Do it weekly at first, then monthly, then quarterly once you're good at it. Add up where the time goes, and offload anything where your involvement isn't uniquely crucial or core to your personal life. Meetings to skip 90% of the time:
- First-round interviews. Do the later rounds and the final sell. Exec hiring is the exception.
- Sales and partnership meetings. The exception: a technical founder selling to enterprises must meet customers regularly.
- Every internal engineering, product or sales meeting. Replace point meetings with a process that brings decisions to you, such as a "Weekly product synch with the CEO."
- Random external meetings.

Delegate recurring time sinks, or hire for them. After 4–8 weeks of self-auditing, time opens up for strategy.

**Learn to say no.** Say no to:
- whatever the calendar audit flags (the nuances of a database schema);
- 6am customer calls (a 9am call on the East Coast is noon there);
- every press opportunity ("Dog Life Monthly Webzine");
- every event (pick one or two of the highest impact each quarter);
- excessive networking (batch it into consolidated blocks);
- unnecessary fundraising ("a VC approached us, so we figured we may as well talk"). Raise only when it supports your objectives.

**Let old patterns go.** With 50 or 500 engineers, your code is unlikely to be your best contribution. Let go of parts of the job you enjoyed.

**Protect your energy, because the team runs on it.** Gil spent an anniversary trip at Color on his phone and counts it as a mistake. His cadence:
- a real vacation of 1–2 weeks a year;
- a three-day weekend each quarter;
- a weekly day without work, if you otherwise work every day;
- a weekly date night;
- morning exercise three times a week, with a trainer or friend for peer pressure.

"A common trigger of founder burnout is finding yourself working on things that you hate". Hire execs or a COO for those things, as Zuckerberg did with Sandberg. The company has to be excellent collectively; you don't have to be excellent at everything. Aaron Levie separates two kinds of burnout. One is being tired of, or out of passion for, the problem space. The other is spending your days on work you dislike that you haven't offloaded, such as 90% of your time in performance reviews and comp meetings. Don't mistake the second for the first. His advice is to "try a COO first" (see [`executives.md`](executives.md)).

## Managing your reports

- **Regular 1:1s.**
- **A weekly staff meeting from about 30 people.** Hold it at a regular weekly time:
  - review key metrics;
  - discuss key topics on company or product strategy, or one functional area's issue, with a different staff member presenting each week.

  Keep in-depth status updates out of it. The meeting serves your reports more than you: peers can't see into each other's orgs, so this is where they share knowledge, raise issues, build relationships and work out strategy together.
- **Skip-level meetings.** Middle managers and big-company hires filter information, "shielding the CEO from 'unimportant' information". Bright junior people often have the pulse of the market. Skip-levels keep lines of communication open, help you spot and nurture talent, and surface ideas from the front lines. So your reports don't feel threatened, tell them you hold skip-levels with many people. "If a person's manager shows up to what is explicitly a skip-level meeting, there is likely something wrong with that manager."

## Claire Hughes Johnson on operating the company

Claire Hughes Johnson was COO of Stripe from 165 people to over 1,000.

**A guide to working with you.** Write a user guide to yourself. Her example is "Working with Claire", and the practice spread through Stripe, where even ICs wrote them. A founder's guide answers:
- What do I want to be involved in?
- When do I want to hear from you?
- What are my preferred communication modes?
- What makes me impatient?
- Don't surprise me with X.

Without a guide, people learn these things in the moment, too late. Her own guide covers four areas:
- **Operating cadence:**
  - weekly 1:1s at consistent times, with a shared 1:1 doc;
  - a weekly team meeting split into updates and decision or work review;
  - quarterly planning with pre-work;
  - a career conversation in the first months;
  - quarterly review of each person's top 3–5 goals.
- **Management style:** collaborative and slow to decide (tell her if you need a fast decision); hands-off unless work is off track ("What do you want to do?"); accountable (renegotiate a deadline before it passes, not the day after); data-driven (no "torturing the numbers"); strategic. Her questions:
  - "What's the big lever here?"
  - "What problem are we trying to solve?"
  - "Why do we need to solve it?"
  - "When do we need to solve it?"
  - "What information do we need and when will we get it?"
- **Communication rules:**
  - she reads all email within 18 hours and replies only if asked;
  - "FYI = no response required";
  - ping her for anything urgent;
  - take long, non-urgent items to the 1:1.
- **Feedback:** "Anyone who vents to me about you is going to get my help to tell you directly."

**Operating structures.** Document the operating principles ("This is what we expect of ourselves, in terms of how we work") apart from any one process. Add only a few mandatory processes, one at each level:
- one for performance and feedback;
- one for planning;
- a few day-to-day tactical ones, such as a launch review (if it isn't on the launch calendar, it isn't happening).

The danger is becoming too process-heavy. The aim is to "provide more context versus trying to exert more control". As the company outgrows the single room, decide what everyone must read, where the source of truth lives, and how all-hands and leadership emails are used. Say outright: "This is about context and communication, not about control." Gil required meeting notes for every meeting at Color. People resisted it as micromanagement, then came back to say he'd been right.

**Decision clarity.** There is "no such thing as an optimal org structure", and structure is a proxy for decision rights only up to a point. State explicitly how each type of decision gets made ("major pricing change, everyone on the leadership team needs to agree"). Otherwise every decision defaults to the founder, which is unhealthy. Write **roles and responsibilities** documents that list each person's primary decision areas and where to go for everything else.

At the start of a meeting, separate **facilitating a group discussion** from **making a group decision**:
- "I want all of your opinions, but I'm going to be the one who ultimately makes the decision."
- Or: "I don't know if I'm the right decision-maker… I will let you know how we're going to make the decision."

The goal is to hash the question out, have one decision-maker, and then have everyone commit. Failing to set this expectation is a common mistake.

Some decisions must come from the leader:
- hiring a significant new leader;
- **decisions to stop, retrench or rebuild**, such as paying down tech debt or killing a project. Plans, incentives and metrics aren't built to stop things, because "the org is always oriented toward making it work."

Gil names a failure mode: the founder hires an impressive senior exec and then **abdicates** the areas the founder is uniquely good at. That signals to the org that the founder no longer cares about them. Re-orgs every 3–6 months feel chaotic, so prepare people for change as inevitable.

**Planning.**
- **Before product/market fit:** stay agile and set milestones that prove fit.
- **After traction:** set key metrics and short, medium and long-term goals, written as **two documents**:
  1. a **charter**: the long-term reason the team, product or company exists, its overarching strategy, and what success looks like over 3–5 years;
  2. a shorter-term plan: results-based or **OKR**s, the quarter's focus, "move X and Y metrics".
- **A new product inside a mature company** stays in milestone mode. Planning must fit each product's stage in its life cycle.
- **Cadence:**
  - revisit charters about yearly;
  - set goals or OKRs quarterly or twice a year;
  - set company metric targets annually and adjust plans quarterly.

On planning frameworks, Hughes Johnson says "you need them, and you need them sooner than you realize". Her analogy is a field covered in random equipment with no rules: people get hurt and don't know how to score. Constraints plus objectives let people decide independently. Stripe started with six company goals pushed down into teams. Leaders own planning, it waterfalls down with accountability, and the CEO participates visibly. Gil recalls that OKRs at Twitter, at 500 people, needed Dick Costolo to pound the table; the execs who resisted later wished they had always done them.

**Founding documents.** These matter especially beyond 50–100 people:
- the mission;
- the vision;
- overarching long-term goals (Stripe's were unchanged three years later);
- operating principles or values ("codify a set of principles and behaviors and then cohere to them, culturally").

Refresh them yearly, though they rarely change. Product projections reach at most about three years out, and so does the finance plan.

**Founder involvement.** Identify, openly with the leadership team, what only the founder or CEO can do that is existential: often recruiting leaders, articulating the product vision and setting cultural standards. Then run a self-awareness exercise across the leadership team (skills, experience, strengths), deploy the group against the company's needs, and document what each person wants to be involved in versus has to be. Avoid opportunistic role allocation ("They have this background so we'll have them do this"); tie roles to the strategy.

## Sam Altman on CEO growing pains

- **Only the CEO can do certain things:** recruit and evangelize to key hires, major customers and investors, and fundraise, because those people only want to talk to the CEO. "the only universal job description of CEO is making sure the company wins." By time spent, communicating and evangelizing are the biggest part of the job.
- **Telling which tactical work matters** is the hard part, because some work looks wasteful but is important and some looks important but is waste. **Compensation structures** are an example: sales comp sets what the company actually measures and pays for, and CEOs should spend time on it, though most don't. "you have to be willing to let some things fall apart." One failing founder with **74 investors** was proud of answering every annual audit request. His company was on fire, and those investors would be far happier with a big return. Getting users and revenue means building things and talking to users.
- **Disconnection from product is bad.** Bringing in a COO is a good idea, but then no longer going to the executive team meeting is a very bad one. Several strong founders dividing up the CEO's roles can work well, as long as it is clear who does what.
- **In a downturn:** "People always say that the CEO's job is not to run out of money, and what they usually mean by that is fundraising. But the other way you can do that is to make money."
- **Boards:** "boards hate surprises." Overcommunicate, and deliver bad news before the meeting. Keep open-ended brainstorming ("Hey, I'm really struggling with this idea. What should we do?") for individual conversations beforehand, because the meeting itself wants confident leadership.
- **Press:** "almost all founders—overweight the importance of press"; "press feels great and delivers nothing". The exception is a product whose customers are the press, as at Twitter. Keep the speaking circuit to very small amounts; "Less is more."
- **Early employees** are worth "more effort than you would normally spend on an employee" to evolve with the company. Few companies get this right. If you're too busy to hire, take a big step back and let some things go undone. A board member can serve as your CEO coach: Altman's own question at a monthly dinner was "Will you teach me how to be a CEO?" Gil adds that founders chase big-name or highest-price investors and later wish they had allocated the round to fewer people who would put in real time.

## Changing cofounder dynamics

Early on, cofounders weigh in on everything. At scale, define decision boundaries and roles, for two reasons:
- the company needs a single strategic direction and viewpoint;
- a cofounder may be out of their depth for the company's scale.

People need to know who makes final decisions. A cofounder ends up in one of three places:
- an IC role they are happy in (Wozniak);
- a key exec role (CTO, president, VP product);
- leaving (not enough influence, wanting the CEO role, a skills mismatch, family).

**Steps:**
1. Work out the optimal role for the cofounder over the next 12–18 months:
   - functional: CTO vs VP engineering vs IC;
   - cultural: do they interview every candidate?;
   - other involvement: public speaking, deals and partnerships;
   - which inputs and decisions they own.
2. Ask the cofounder what they want, and have them write a job spec for themselves.
3. Discuss it. Expect a series of conversations.
4. Enlist a trusted advisor, investor or board member to mediate.
5. Once you agree, help them succeed, perhaps with a management coach.

Remember that they made the early bet, are a major equity holder, and may sit on the board. If you can't agree, negotiate an exit, which may be contentious or may come as a relief. Over time, a non-CEO cofounder may come to feel disempowered.

## Unequal cofounders

Gil calls the idea that cofounders should be equal a myth. Many top companies had a **dominant cofounder**:
- Amazon (Bezos), Apple (Jobs, with unequal equity with Wozniak), Facebook ("A Mark Zuckerberg production");
- Instagram (Systrom), Intel (Noyce, then Moore), Intuit (Scott Cook), LinkedIn (Hoffman);
- Microsoft (Gates, after Allen left), Netflix (Hastings took over from Randolph), Oracle (Ellison), Pinterest (Silbermann);
- Salesforce (Benioff), Square (Dorsey), Uber (Kalanick), WhatsApp (Koum).

In Gil's experience, "equal power sharing yields worse outcomes than having a dominant cofounder (or at least one who emerges as dominant once a company starts working)". What matters is clear decision-making and a single CEO who sets the path. Google (Page and Brin, plus Schmidt) is the counterexample.
