# Org structure, re-orgs and culture

## Organizational structure is all about pragmatism

The questions come fast. Should you hire a COO? Who does the VP marketing report to? Should product and engineering split? Should international offices run their own functions or be matrixed to HQ? Gil says the fear of picking the wrong answer is misplaced, because the right structure is whatever fits the **talent available, the set of initiatives, and a 12- to 18-month horizon**.

- **If you're growing fast, you're a different company every 6–12 months.** Google went from 1,500 to 15,000 people in three and a half years, and Twitter from 90 to 1,500 in under three. Choose a structure for the next 6–12 months, not for the long term. The exec team eventually stabilizes, while the teams below it keep re-organizing.
- **There is no "right" answer.** Every structure has trade-offs. Two structures can be equally good or equally bad, and mistakes can be undone. Tell the team that shifting structure is normal and a sign of success.
- **Bandwidth can matter more than a perfect fit.** At Twitter, GC Alex Macgillivray had user support, trust and safety, and corporate development reporting to him. Each moved away as execs were hired for it. Assign by who has the time and the skill, and treat it as temporary. There are limits: a VP of engineering shouldn't run sales, but could run design or product for a while.
- **Org structure is often about tie-breaking.** Reporting chains are decision chains. Whoever both functions report to becomes the tiebreaker, for example in tension between engineering and PM.
- **Hire execs for the next 12–18 months,** not for eternity (see [`executives.md`](executives.md)). "A company is a living, breathing thing."

## Doing a re-org

Hypergrowth means doubling every 6–12 months: 20 people become about 300 in two years, and 500 or 1,000 in four. Most people joined within the last 12 months. Early re-orgs happen at the exec level and cascade down; hiring a CMO or another CXO consolidates roles under them. Beyond 500–1,000 people there are fewer company-wide re-orgs and more within functions. Sales re-orgs often, while product and engineering stay more stable as headcount shifts toward go-to-market.

The big later re-orgs that cross the whole company come from four things:
- changing product, engineering and go-to-market together;
- new product areas or acquisitions;
- flipping between a matrix and business units;
- flipping international between centralized and decentralized. Oracle reportedly flips its international structure every few years.

Early on, the CEO must be good at re-orgs. Later, the CEO must make sure the leadership team is. "Most companies and new managers screw up their first re-org or two."

**The seven steps:**
1. **Decide why.** Is the aim renewed focus, fixing collaboration problems, giving a growing team the management it needs, or re-aligning priorities after a market change? Spell out the logic first, then the leadership and structure.
2. **Find the most pragmatic structure.** Who is overloaded and who has bandwidth? Who is building a great management layer? What fits together? Choose between cross-functional and verticalized product units, and between distributed and centralized international. Since reporting lines settle ties, have the people most likely to disagree report to a single tiebreaker. It won't be 100% perfect, and that is OK.
3. **Get buy-in from the right people before implementing.** Consult the handful of execs most affected. **Never open the conversation with the whole company or function** about what form the re-org should take. That invites lobbying, politicking, land grabs and prolonged angst.
4. **Announce and implement it soup-to-nuts within 24 hours.** Discuss it with your reports in 1:1s, and make sure each exec has a plan to communicate it. Meet the key unhappy people right before or right after. Never drag it out or pre-announce it ("this week we will re-organize product, and next month we will change engineering"). A team that knows it is next stops working and turns to gossip, rumors and lobbying.
5. **Brief every member of the leadership team** so they can answer questions, and prepare an internal FAQ.
6. **Remove ambiguity: know where roughly 100% of people are going.** Don't do a partial re-org. List the people likely to be unhappy, reach out to them quickly, and stay accessible.
7. **Communicate directly, clearly and compassionately.** Listen, but stay firm. Keep backtracking to a minimum. Exceptions made for the "squeakiest wheels" undercut the reason for the re-org and show that you can be politicked.

## Ruchi Sanghvi: bringing in the Wolf

**The Band-Aid, gap filler or "the Wolf"** (after the fixer in *Pulp Fiction*) is a trusted person who steps into a function that lacks a leader until a permanent exec is hired. Examples are Matt Cohler at Facebook, and Gil and Ali Rowghani at Twitter. The CEO delegates the function to them; they build it, hire the exec, and move on. Gap fillers have a large impact in the short and medium term, but the arrangement isn't sustainable for the person or the company.

**How Sanghvi did it.** She joined Dropbox through its acquisition of Cove and interviewed about half the company, asking each person three questions:
- "What's working well?"
- "How do you think you could accelerate the company's efforts?"
- "And what are the one or two priorities you think need to be addressed immediately?"

Everyone named a lack of resources. As VP of Operations she took on, in turn: recruiting (goals, plus systems to integrate new hires); marketing (built the team and hired its leader); communications; a third of product; and growth and internationalization. The key is to hire quickly, so that the Band-Aids give way to people who grow scalable solutions.

**Doing the job well:**
- Scale the team and make decisions, but don't push it so far in one direction that you leave no room for the incoming exec's own direction.
- You need the trust and respect of the CEO and the exec team. Other execs lend you people, you set expectations on how the team fits company goals, and the execs recognize your contributions.
- Shape the hire. The replacement should fit the present and the **next two years**, or you'll be "chasing unicorns": someone who both gets their hands dirty now and can manage 100 people five years out. Such unicorns don't exist. The gap filler convinces the CEO to hire different types at different times, sources candidates, sets the interview slate and moderates debriefs.
- It works only when the gap filler is part of the exec team and reports to the CEO, COO or a founder. From there they can surface constraints, ask for support, highlight successes, and influence or expedite hiring. They must be deeply respected and have "cred" with early employees and execs, or the culture will reject them.

**Failure modes:**
- **The search gets deprioritized.** Non-core functions such as comms, people ops and marketing get gap-filled, and if the gap filler does a reasonable job, the company stops prioritizing the search.
- **Expectations aren't set.** This is the biggest failure, both with the CEO and with the people the new exec will manage. The gap filler ran on trust, and the new exec will draw org lines differently. Settle up front the role definition, the extent of its responsibility, the experience needed, and whether it reports to the CEO.

**How long.** More than two or two and a half years is "way too long". A good Band-Aid is hard to replace, and keeping one is "locally maximizing and not globally maximizing"; by then you should have a functional exec team in every area. The functions most often Band-Aided are recruiting, HR, comms and marketing, sometimes customer support, and occasionally product (VP product is hard to fill, and usually the CEO fills it). Gap fillers work only at the top level. Every company in exponential growth needs one, because from outside it looks great while "internally it's chaos." The CEO should delegate the gap filling rather than take it on, since that isn't the CEO's most leveraged work.

**Rip off the Band-Aid.** A good Band-Aid deters the permanent hire. If someone has been subbing for the head of product or the VP of comms for **more than a year**, remove them to force building the org. Gil's arithmetic for building the exec "organizational scaffolding" early, thinking 6–18 months ahead: **3–6 months to find a good exec, a couple of months to build their team, and a couple more to ramp, so you need a year of runway.** A Wolf usually comes from inside, because outsiders are hard to hire for it. It takes the org's trust plus a strong relationship with the CEO or founder, which means someone very early, or an acquired person the founders already knew. If you find someone you trust that much, consider making them COO. Gil also explains why the role sits unfilled: before product/market fit it rightly wasn't a priority, then "the company itself becomes more of the product", and the org doesn't know how to value or search for the role.

**The employee's side of rapid scaling** (Sanghvi):
1. Everyone's workload grows **2–5X**.
2. Hiring fast grows it further, because you now take on the projects you couldn't get to before.
3. As the org reaches equilibrium, your role and scope suddenly become **narrow**. This is hard for early employees. Those who transition through the narrowing grow with the company. Narrower doesn't mean less impact; it means a "larger impact in a more focused area".

Gil's advice to employees: don't sweat it. If the company is doubling, a peer who became your manager may later be your report. Keep your head down, do good work, and be ready to redo processes every six months. On process, Sanghvi says be cautious early, but "when you need it, don't be afraid to implement it." At Facebook she was one of the kids pushing back on the "adults" hired to manage them; at Dropbox she became the adult. Look for people who are perfect for the **next three years**, not a 10-year unicorn.

## Mariam Naficy: scaling is more than a numbers game

Naficy founded Minted, which had about 200 employees after nine years.

**Complexity is a dimension of scale.** Beyond headcount there is "the differentiation or complexity of the business". Reid Hoffman contrasts a village with a city. A basic, unchanging model scales more easily; entering a new vertical complicates everything. Michael Porter's "What Is Strategy?" shows that different **activity maps** underlie different businesses. Ask: "How many businesses are we actually in?" One customer base and one acquisition team repeating one motion is the better scaling model. Gil frames this as **repeatable vs nonrepeatable scaling**, using Google search versus Gmail as the example. Ask whether the new business needs the same skill sets and the same acquisition, and whether you have the right infrastructure for innovation.

**A new vertical is "completely new entrepreneurship within a company."** Find the overlapping core competency; for Minted it was its design community and the same customer. Still expect differences in operations and acquisition: acquiring an art customer is not like acquiring a stationery customer, and even moving existing customers across takes entrepreneurship. Hire inventive, entrepreneurial people for it. Mature companies run a portfolio of roughly 15% exploratory work, 20% past the exploratory gate and commercializing, and 60–65% incremental improvement of the core.

**When to expand.**
1. Look at the core business's year-over-year growth curve.
2. Project when its inevitable decline will come.
3. Estimate how many years a second business needs before it is ready to add to the "growth cake". That depends on how strong the core is, how long it can run, and how fragile or self-generating its acquisition model is.

The problem is **the mix**: 500% growth a year on a tiny base still blends down to a low overall rate. Because it takes years, "start a little earlier than you think," as Google did. Minted started in 2008 and launched art in 2012, and only in the fourth year did art move the blended growth rate. "The complexity or difficulty you add to your company significantly affects scaling, much more than just the number of people you hire."

**International vs a new market.** Photo cards differ culturally from country to country. Naficy positioned the core as design rather than stationery, to avoid **"the Zappos problem"** (you can't get them "out of the shoebox"), and so expanded the brand early. Overseas complexity, with exec travel and time, would "break us"; a new US business was easier.

**Starting a new business inside the company:**
- Launch an **MVP** with very little money, clamped onto existing people's day jobs (merchants ran art challenges; supply chain figured out framing).
- When it works, make the first hire an early-career, "I'll do it all" entrepreneurial person. That got to a couple of million in revenue with one hire plus leverage.
- Then hire a director of marketing for the line.
- Minted used a matrix org, not a GM structure. Naficy was warned that GMs risk politics and a siloed brand.

**Choosing a GM.** You want a collaborative problem-solver, not someone who will "run their own island". Ask:
- "What would you want from me as your manager? What kind of interactions do you want?" A red flag is the big-company answer, such as "I will communicate just to make sure that you have absolutely no negative surprises."
- "How do you communicate with your manager?": formal or informal, how often, in what style.
- "What do you want from your peers?" This reveals whether they think a manager and peers add value.

GMs are hard to find. They must be very good generalists, they want to run their own companies, they need equal caliber across divisions, and divisions with different core competencies (merchandising, marketing, PM) make shared processes hard. Running multiple businesses is among the most complicated parts of scaling.

**Technical debt.**
- Set a yearly growth road map, and **piggyback debt pay-down projects on it**, in the order the road map dictates. Minted rebuilt its product details page from about six versions into one modular code base because the old pages blocked the growth road map.
- Watch team productivity. When engineers slow down and are afraid to touch the code, use a **balanced scorecard** with measurable impact, and watch for tipping points in pure happiness.
- Let the capital environment set the horizon. Cheap capital allows long-payoff projects. Expensive capital means shorter-horizon, lower-beta projects that pay off within a year, which is a tall order.
- Rank revenue growth first, then brand, customer satisfaction and community satisfaction. Don't run massive tech debt projects that aren't connected to the business plan.

**Engineering and business tension:**
- Engineering and product meet to rank work against the company goals.
- The recruiting process states what you value (the key metric was revenue), so people self-select.
- Communicate strategy and goals "many, many, many times".
- Minted's VP of finance taught engineers **ROI classes** (an idea from its head of PM), and financial language ("ROI", "net present value of the revenue") went into the ranking documents.

For the Naficy seniority framework, see [`executives.md`](executives.md).

## Company culture and its evolution

Culture changes with scale. You add broader functions and execs, the company tolerates less risk and more process, it grows 10–100x, and an IPO brings controls. The founder decides what to keep, morph or drop, and tells veterans that change is needed. **The biggest levers are who you hire, which behaviors you emphasize and reward, and who you let go.**

**Never, ever compromise: hiring for culture.** You want diversity of background together with cohesion in purpose, intent and baseline culture. Culture is the "unwritten set of rules and values". Cohesive cultures survive shocks such as competition, bad press or a failed product, and they motivate (Palantir engineers who slept under their desks; Google's "don't be evil"). Compromising to "get someone effective" or to "fill a need" backfires within months. "Every single founder I know who has compromised on culture when hiring has regretted it". The cost is firing bad actors, a crappy environment, good people quitting, eroding trust, the product going the wrong way, and misaligned incentives.

**How to build a strong culture:**
1. Use strong hiring filters for common values, without filtering out diverse populations.
2. Emphasize the values constantly, day to day. "Repeat them until you are blue in the face". Once you are sick of saying them, people start repeating them back.
3. Reward performance *and* culture, in promotions and pay.
4. **Fire bad culture fits even faster than low performers.**

**Hiring for culture and values.** A high-growth company is fragile, and people pulling in different directions or having "pointless philosophical arguments" is lethal. Cohesion is not cloning or groupthink.
1. **Determine your values and culture,** with broad employee input:
   - What are the cornerstones?
   - Which values do you want in hires?
   - What will you compromise on, and what won't you? "if you are willing to compromise on it, it is not important to you."
   - How will you screen at each stage? If you want proactive problem-solving outside someone's responsibilities, ask for past examples.
   - Do your values, questions and filters still attract diverse candidates?
2. **Watch for red flags:**
   - The sole motivation is financial. Mercenaries leave for the highest bidder and make short-term decisions.
   - **Arrogance.** The smartest people write the question down and work through it; people who think they're the smartest do it in their heads and get it wrong.
   - They make a bad environment for the team: low energy, negative, needlessly argumentative, valuing philosophy over pragmatism.

   Reject someone who is technically great but a bad culture fit.
3. **Optimize for the long term.** Facing a big hole and a borderline-culture candidate, don't hire. **"If there is a doubt, there is no doubt"**.

## Patrick Collison: you can't delegate culture

**The main mistakes** are being "too precious," "too apologetic," and not treating culture as dynamic and subject to revision.

**Be specific.** If the company is working, some parts of the culture empirically work, so say them plainly. "We believe in the importance of commitment" is oblique. "we expect this to be the singular focus of your working life" is specific. For attention to detail, say you must be "okay with your work being repeatedly designated as inadequate". Some people will decide it isn't for them. Not being explicit costs three ways:
- the wrong people join;
- it is unfair to those who join and are surprised by the friction;
- less obviously, people's disposition is a function of what they feel they signed up for, so being explicit at the outset changes the outcome.

Be explicit **before the offer is accepted**; onboarding is only OK-ish.

**Don't be overly wedded to it either.** The judgment call is whether outcomes are poor because people aren't following the culture, or because of the culture itself: **"Is it the implementation or is it the spec?"** The data is sparse and noisy. A common misjudgment is crediting early success to lightweight or no management. Another is crediting it correctly but not seeing that what worked at seven people is wrong at 40 or 70.

**Revising culture:**
- "you do not want to preserve culture; you want to collectively steer the right evolution of the culture". Push back against misty-eyed talk of the halcyon days ("In so many ways we were derpy back then"), and point out the stupid things done early. It's easy to learn the wrong lessons from early success.
- When you add a senior external leader or a new function, **say explicitly that it will change the culture**. "If this person has no effect on the culture, they've probably failed." A new head of sales should make the culture better at selling.

**Naysayers.** Their complaints often point to real problems, so give them a sympathetic ear and fix what you can, which won't be quick. If they disagree with a decided direction (it used to be X, now it's Y), be explicit: they have to decide whether they can enthusiastically sign up for Y, or the work may not be fulfilling long-term. An investment in what the company *is* can turn into an investment in what it *was*; these are not bad people. The mistake is not being honest, which drags on for a year or two and ends with departures on bad terms. **Front-load** a non-acrimonious conversation: "Look, we're going to do Y. Can you set aside your perhaps underlying desire for X and sign up for it? Or not? We're not going to judge you if you don't want to do that." Gil adds that turnover in a subset of early employees is natural and good. Collison says an org that fires no one, or keeps every early employee five years in, is statistically extraordinarily unlikely, and you should question it.

**Reinforcing culture.** A rapidly scaling human organization is unnatural; schools, families and churches don't double every year. That justifies putting explicit values into performance reviews and all-hands meetings, even if it seems contrived. Most companies encode their principles **too late**. Write a provisional version when you're a handful of people, keep updating it, and weave it into product development, communication and decisions, even the choice of series A investors.

**No culture czar.** Handing culture to someone other than the CEO is bad: the likely candidates are emotionally invested in what the company is, so they impede its evolution. Culture is one of the CEO's five responsibilities (see [`SKILL.md`](SKILL.md)).

**Distributed offices** (Stripe had about 10):
- Pick the right site lead and the initial **"seed crystals"**: 2–4 people, steeped in HQ, around whom the office's culture forms.
- Start every new office employee at HQ for weeks or months. Dublin's 70 people each spent their first couple of weeks in San Francisco. Interview at HQ too.
- Get the communication mechanics right: good videoconferencing, wired rooms, easy recording and broadcast. Move the all-hands from a Friday 5pm beer to a time that works for Europe and Asia.
- At about **100–200 people**, make internal communications a function rather than everyone's "1% job". People are surprised how valuable it is, and start it late.

**Role models.** Choose them carefully. Contemporaries of a similar size aren't proven enough, and some are making major cultural and org errors. Study the greats with known outcomes: Intel, Microsoft, early Google, Jobs's return to Apple. "Most great products with strong initial traction fail to endure or become any kind of durable organization." Gil adds that product/market fit doesn't mean you have a strong, adaptable culture.

**Entitlement** (free haircuts and the like) is a challenge for the whole era: wealth created by predecessors spills over into distraction and lost determination. Collison notes that the early semiconductor and software companies had nothing to feel entitled about, and that Silicon Valley is also "how densely populated a graveyard it is". Survivorship bias warps intuition. It is harder today to build a focused, determined, disciplined, noncomplacent mindset, and he points to Chinese companies (JD, Tencent, Alibaba) for their lack of entitlement.

## Things to just say no to

Gil's list:
1. **Envelopes full of cash.** Google once handed out Christmas bonuses as $1,000 in cash in plain envelopes, all on one day. Legend has it that thieves mugged the shuttle riders.
2. **China.** Uber came closest to succeeding, through its 20% of Didi. Almost every other tech giant was blocked and then cloned. For most companies, a China strategy is "a painful, money-burning fail."
3. **Giant chrome pandas.** Dropbox bought one at its funding peak. When it later focused on frugality, the panda became a reminder of wanton spending. You can teach the same lesson cheaply; one company keeps a Juicero machine in its office as its "chrome panda."
4. **Pool tables.** A startup Gil joined at 120 people grew to 150 and then shrank to 12 through four to five rounds of layoffs. After the first round it bought a pool table to "help employee morale", and anyone seen playing tended to be laid off in the next round.
