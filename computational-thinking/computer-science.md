# Computer science: how academic CT developed

The computing-education dimension in the universities. Academic CT always had a strong design component and was shaped by what employers said they needed. It was also shaped by departments that fiercely guarded their identities, budgets, enrollments and research domains and decided by consensus. Founders of computer science had to argue what was new about computing before they got departments: 1 in 1962 (Purdue), about 120 in the US by 1980. Early on CT split into a *hardware flavor* (computer engineers, engineering schools) and a *software flavor* (software designers and theorists, science schools).

## The four eras

1. **Phenomena surrounding computers (1950s–1970s).** Critics held that computing lacked unique intellectual content and theory, that artifacts are not natural phenomena, and that computers would not last. The 1950s field looked like "an impenetrable and anarchistic thicket of idiosyncratic technology tricks." Machine designers were mostly unaware of the theory: Turing on computable numbers, Church on lambda calculus, Post on string manipulation, Kleene on regular expressions, Rabin and Scott on nondeterministic machines, Chomsky on grammars and automata. In 1967 Newell, Perlis and Simon answered in *Science*: "Phenomena breed sciences. ... There are computers. Ergo, computer science is the study of computers." Simon's *Sciences of the Artificial* gave time-sharing as the example. No theory could guide its early development, and predictions were "astonishingly inaccurate"; the theory came only from empirical study of systems once they were built. So CT "could not approach problems from one direction only": engineering and science evolved together into a science that was not purely natural. "Algorithmic thinking" named the new kind of thinking. The pioneers worked out named variables, control structures, data structures, data types, formal programming languages, subroutines, compilers, input-output protocols, instruction pipelines, interrupt systems, computing processes, memory hierarchies, caches, virtual memory, peripherals and interfaces.
2. **Programming as art and science (1970s).** In the 1960s CT acquired a *systems attitude* (operating systems), an *engineering attitude* (software engineering) and a *networking attitude* (ARPANET, 1967). Then attention turned to programs. *Programs* are "expressions of algorithms in a formal language that, when compiled to machine-executable form, control the actions of a machine." Assembly language ("ADD R1,R2,R3") was tedious and error-prone. Languages let a compiler translate precise higher-level expressions unambiguously: FORTRAN (1957) for scientific data and models, LISP (1958) for logical deduction, COBOL (1959) for business records, ALGOL (1958) for communicating precise specifications of algorithms. Interest grew in formal verification and computational complexity. By 1980 most computer scientists said CT is the skills and knowledge related to algorithms and software development.
3. **Computing as automation (1980s).** Algorithms alone could not answer how to design a language, raise programmer productivity, build secure operating systems and fault-tolerant systems, transmit reliably over packet networks, stop intruders, find bottlenecks and response times, or replace human operators. Algorithm study rarely addressed interactions with humans or effects on other users. Automation became "the soul of computational thinking": "the control of processes by mechanical means with minimal human intervention, or the carrying out of a process by a machine." NSF's COSERS study produced *What Can Be Automated?* (1980), and the question became computing's slogan. It undid itself, because the boundary of what can be automated is ambiguous and moves with new algorithms and faster hardware (see *Gauging what can be computed* in SKILL.md). AI became the focal point for automating cognitive tasks, adding heuristic search of game solution spaces, deduction, and machine learning that generalizes from examples.
4. **Computing as pervasive information processes (1990s to present).** Scientists running simulations were not automating anything. Around 2000 they began saying they worked with *naturally occurring information processes*: DNA transcription is computational, and there is nothing to automate, only a process to understand and modify. Cognitive scientists, chemists, drug designers and physicists said the same of their fields. Blogging, image recognition, encryption, machine learning, natural language processing and blockchains each created an altogether new process rather than automating an existing one. The field once attacked for not studying natural processes now studies them directly.

Computer science also resisted others twice. Computer scientists protested computational science branches in physics and biology as a hijack, and science-minded departments kept software engineering's project practices at arm's length.

## What algorithmic thinking is

- **Programming paradigms**: languages cater to particular ways of thinking about problems. *Imperative* programs are series of modules ("procedures") that command the machine (FORTRAN, COBOL, ALGOL). *Object-oriented* programs are collections of relatively self-sufficient "objects" that exchange messages (Smalltalk, Java). *Functional* programs are sets of mathematical functions generating output data from input data (LISP). In the 1970s these were seen as styles of algorithmic thinking, all seeking programs clear for humans to read that run correctly and efficiently. Knuth (*The Art of Computer Programming*, *Literate Programming*) and Dijkstra (structured programming) epitomized it.
- **Knuth**: algorithmic thinking differs from mathematical thinking in how it reduces complex problems to interconnected simple ones, emphasizes information structures, attends to how actions alter the states of data, and formulates symbolic representations of reality.
- **Dijkstra**: computer scientists differ from mathematicians in expressing algorithms in natural as well as formal languages, devising notations that simplify computations, mastering complexity, shifting between abstraction levels, and inventing concepts, objects, notations and theories when necessary.
- **The toolbox it added.** From the engineering side: compilers, parsing, code optimization, operating systems, and empirical testing and debugging. From the math side: algorithms analysis with O-notation, models of computation, and proofs of program correctness.
- **Learned or innate?** Some pioneers held computing's ways of thinking to be a generic tool for everyone, on a par with mathematics and language; others held algorithmic thinking to be a rare innate ability, in about one person in fifty. Educators back the first: "CT is a skill to be learned and not an ability that one is born with." Proposals to add programming to the three Rs as a fourth have not been accepted.

## Strong and weak views

- **Strong AI**: suitably programmed machines can be literally intelligent. It dominated AI from 1950 to the late 1990s and produced very little insight into intelligence. **Weak AI**: smart programming simulates mental activities so well that machines appear intelligent without being so. Siri and Alexa act on commands without "understanding" them. Weak AI has ruled since the 1990s and produced the neural network and big data advances. The *Turing test* (1950) was meant to settle whether a machine can think. An observer holds two text conversations, one with a computer and one with a human, without knowing which is which; if over a long period the observer cannot definitely identify the human, the machine is deemed intelligent.
- **The strong computational view of the universe**: the universe and every living being is a digital computer, every dimension of space and time discrete, every movement a computation. The **weak** view claims only that computational interpretations are very useful for modeling, simulating and studying phenomena. The strong view is "highly speculative" with little evidence. It continues an old habit of explaining the world by the latest technology: Enlightenment clockwork, then the brain as mill, telegraph, hydraulic system, electromagnetic system, computer, and now quantum computer. Its appeal is to those who believe computing is pervasive without limits.
