# Methods and machines

Two of CT's six dimensions. *Methods*: procedures non-experts could follow to compute and reason. *Machines*: devices that automate those procedures, chasing speed and the elimination of human error. "Modern computer science is the last 1 percent of the historical timeline of computational thinking"; "CT is not about how computer scientists think."

## Methods

**Unambiguous steps.** A method captures as a set of steps what has to be done in a complex calculation. Refined enough, it works for anyone who follows directions, even without knowing why it works. Magicians' "automatic tricks" run on this: think of a number, double it, add 8, divide by 2, subtract the original, and the answer is always 4, half the number added, because (2X+8)÷2−X = 4. "The less the ambiguity, the more trustworthy the method was in the hands of non-experts." The practice was to replace fuzzy steps with precise chains of arithmetic and logic operations.

Classic methods:
- **Euclid's GCD** (about 300 BCE): the GCD of two numbers divides their difference, so repeatedly replace the larger with the difference until both are equal: GCD(48,18) = GCD(30,18) = GCD(12,18) = GCD(12,6) = GCD(6,6) = 6. It reduced fractions and today underlies cryptography.
- **Sieve of Eratosthenes**: list the integers from 2 to the limit, cross out the multiples of 2, then 3, then 5; each round reveals a new prime whose multiples go next. It is adapted to find large primes for cryptographic keys.
- **Calculus** (Newton and Leibniz, about 1680): represent forms and curves by tiny locally interacting components, then sum them; the local rule is a derivative, the summation an integral. Dividing time and space into a grid and computing the function at each grid point made physics' differential equations computable.
- **Gaussian elimination** for linear systems (Gauss, mid-1800s; known to Chinese mathematicians in 179 BCE), now used to render 3-D graphics in real time.

**The quest to eliminate intuition.** *Intuition* is "An aspect of embodied expertise where the expert is able to know immediately how to deal with a situation, based on extensive past experience. The expert may know what to do but cannot explain why." It lets experts find the essential core, skip steps and switch approaches. Mathematics and logic nonetheless worked for centuries to eliminate it from routine calculation and inference, so that results come out the same whoever calculates. "Eliminating intuition from routine jobs did not mean eliminating experts, but rather making their expertise available to a large number of non-expert people." The inheritance of those centuries: symbolic information representation, symbol processing, unambiguous computational steps, basic arithmetic, algorithms, synchronization of computations and systematic error checking.

**Representation.** "A representation is an arrangement of symbols in an agreed format; each representation stands for something": numbers, equations (a matrix for rotation, a differential equation for planetary motion), mechanical models (wind tunnels, orreries), and non-numeric symbols (a program, a bitmap, a soundtrack). *Digitization* is constructing a binary coded representation of almost any information so a computer can process it. Speech, for example, is sampled 20,000 times a second with each amplitude encoded as a 16-bit value. Representations of data and of logic steps (a conditional jump, a loop) are core elements of CT.

**Accumulating error.** Machines have limited precision (32 or 64 bits), and round-off errors accumulate in poorly designed algorithms. Designers of numerical methods find constraints that keep accumulated error within bounds; calculus gave systematic ways to limit errors in finite-difference calculations.

**Decomposing computing tasks.** Around 1940 teams of women human computers at Aberdeen Proving Grounds computed artillery firing tables on assembly lines, with mechanical calculators and programs their managers set. CT still follows their pattern:
1. Break the entire computation into pieces done by separate, interacting computers.
2. Arrange the computers to optimize their communication and messaging patterns, for example an assembly line or a massive parallel fan-out and join.
3. Include error checks so recipients can verify that their inputs and outputs are correct.

These are distributed computing, parallelization and error checking, developed for human computers before machines. With ENIAC, decomposition into unambiguous steps that pass data between them moved from a management principle into a design principle for automatic computers.

**Where errors arise.** Errors can happen at any stage: describing the problem, preparing the formulas, setting the constants, communicating data, recording and retrieving data, carrying out the steps, or displaying the results. Human computers err constantly however simple the steps: they forget, misunderstand, get distracted and bored. One study of 40 volumes of old tables found 3,700 errors; another found 40 errors on one page. Machines removed some kinds of error, but "a whole horizon of new errors has appeared."

**Rules for reasoning.** Unambiguous steps are not enough: at every stage the plan must say unambiguously what the next step is, and deciding that is an exercise in logic.
- Descartes and Leibniz sought a "language of thought" that would settle every dispute by calculation.
- Boole's *Laws of Thought* (1854) gave "an algebra of thought": variables that are true or false, combined by and, or, not. Shannon (1937) showed it describes relay circuits. *Boolean algebra* became the fixture of circuit design, finding the smallest circuit for a logic function and avoiding race conditions.
- Boole could not express sets ("everybody with a gray hair") or the quantifiers all and some. Frege's predicate logic (1879) added them, with mechanical symbol rules requiring no intuition. It resembles a programming language: an artificial formal language with unambiguous, deterministic, mechanical rules.
- The *decision problem*: is there an algorithm that decides whether a proposition in a logical system is true? In the 1930s several mathematicians independently defined "algorithm" and showed there is no general solution.

**Mechanizing computation.** Turing modeled a human computer as a linear sequence of boxes, each holding one symbol, and a mind in one of finitely many states. The basic actions: in a given state, move left or right one box, read the symbol, change the symbol, move to the next state. "five actions and a finite number of states are enough for any computation." One such machine can simulate all others. He proved no machine can solve the decision problem. He called them a-machines, for "automatic"; early computers were named "-AC" for "automatic computer". Automation means the machine does the work without human intervention: "The automatic computer is the ultimate realization of the old dream of making calculation available for the masses." Its other half is knowing what automatic computers cannot do.

## The machines

The motivation was always speed and the removal of human error. The slide rule (about 1620) multiplied by adding logarithms but could not add or subtract. Pascal's calculator (1642) added and subtracted but could not multiply or divide. Both had two limits: they could not run long chains of calculation, and each served a single purpose. The electronic computer's radical idea overcame both: software stored inside the machine's memory. Jacquard's punched-card loom was external, changeable software. Hollerith's tabulators processed the 63 million records of the 1890 census in one year.

Babbage's Difference Engine (about 1820) aimed to print error-free tables. Errors in navigation tables, he argued, wrecked ships. His Analytical Engine (about 1840) was a general-purpose computer: punched-card programs, selection, looping, separate units for input, processing, memory and output, and instructions composed from microprograms. Ada Lovelace designed algorithms for it, one computing the Bernoulli numbers. She saw it as a processor of any information that can be encoded in symbols, "the science of operations": "Lovelace saw the computer as an information machine."

In 1945 the ENIAC team, joined by von Neumann, split the machine into CPU, memory and input-output, with the CPU taking its instructions from memory: the *stored program computer*, misnamed the "von Neumann architecture" after the note taker. "The notion that a CPU traces out an instruction sequence among instructions stored in memory has become a central tenet of computational thinking." After the 1940s, design concepts for programs and computers "quickly diverged from mathematics and logic. It was a profound change."

## Precepts the machines gave CT

- **Digital representation.** "There is no information without representation": data exist as signals or measurable disturbances in storage media. Engineers chose binary because binary arithmetic needs far fewer components (4 tubes per digit against a ring of 10) and two-voltage circuits are far more reliable. Internally the computer processes only voltages, currents, switches and malleable materials; the 0s and 1s are designers' abstractions. Syntax rules let a machine tell valid patterns from invalid ones without knowing what they mean. Every dataset, program and circuit layout is a "strategic arrangement of stuff."
- **Boolean algebra is an abstraction.** It hides physical *race conditions*: when signals take paths of different speed to the same output, the output fluctuates with arrival order and downstream circuits malfunction. The same occurs in an operating system when two users write a file at once and the last writer wins. Programmers who know only the abstraction cannot find such errors by studying their programs.
- **The clocked CPU cycle.** Logic circuits need time to settle; at each clock tick their outputs are stored in *registers* (sets of flip-flops, each holding a 0 or 1). A "3.8 GHz processor" ticks 3.8 billion times a second. The clock gives "algorithmic steps" a precise physical meaning: each step finishes before the next begins.
- **Control flow.** The program counter (PC) holds the next instruction's address, by default PC+1; "if C then set PC to X" branches. In languages this is the *if-then-else* construct.
- **Loops: small programs making big computations.** Without loops a computation cannot be longer than its program. A loop repeats until a stopping condition holds; a faulty condition gives an *infinite loop*. Since no algorithm can detect infinite loops, debugging cannot be automated. Service processes loop forever on purpose: wait at a homing position, serve a request, return. Proving they always return remains a challenge.
- **The address-contents distinction.** In a program a variable names a memory location. "X=3" is a command, store 3 in location X; in algebra it is a fact, the unknown X is 3. Novices who miss this write programs that do not work.
- **Subprograms.** "CALL X" saves the PC and jumps to X; "RETURN" restores it. Subroutines, functions and procedures follow, and it is taken for granted that programs are divided into modules.
- **Universal machines.** Turing's universal machine (1936) simulates any other computer given its program; one base machine runs many programs. Universal is not the same as stored program: the Analytical Engine read cards and ENIAC used patch boards. A stored program lets the same bits be data (bits that represent things) or instructions (bits that do things); a compiler's output data become executable instructions. Operating systems made machine code read-only so it could be shared but not changed. Self-modification survives in malware that rewrites itself to escape antivirus detection.
- **Fault tolerance and data protection.** Hardware faults come from conflicting signals, clocks too fast for components to settle, and deterioration; circuit engineers made hardware reliable enough that users ignore it. Multi-user systems (from the 1950s) confine each program to a private memory region, set by a base address and length in a CPU register. This *isolation principle* protects users from one another and from untrusted software in a "sandbox". Many 1980s chips dropped the memory-bound checks for speed, which security specialists now regret.

## Beyond the von Neumann architecture

The architecture is reaching its limits. Moore's law (component counts doubling every two years) is ending as components approach too few atoms to function. And separating processor from memory creates massive traffic between them. Remedies: caches that keep data in the CPU, cellular arrays interspersing processors and memory, and special-purpose chips such as GPUs.

- **Neural networks** map large bit patterns to other bit patterns (a photograph to labeled faces) through weighted layers. An external algorithm trains them on many input-output pairs, which takes hours to days; a trained network answers almost instantly. They are not universal computers. "there is no mechanism for verifying that a neural network will give the proper output when presented with an input not in its training set," and being unable to "explain" a conclusion jars computational thinking.
- **Quantum computers** represent bits with electron spins and connections with entanglement. *Qubits* can be 0 and 1 at once (superposition). They factor large numbers fast, threatening RSA, whose security rests on factoring being intractable on von Neumann machines.

"each kind of machine has an associated style of computational thinking and is quite good at particular kinds of problems." An advanced computational thinker knows the architectures and picks the best one as part of the design. Each machine type can also induce "blindness": designers schooled in von Neumann machines think in instructions and struggle with quantum computers.
