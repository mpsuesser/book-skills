# Owning a word: laws 5, 6, 14 and 15

The laws of focus, exclusivity, attributes and candor: how to choose the word or attribute a brand will own, and how to get it into the mind.

## 5. The Law of Focus

"The most powerful concept in marketing is owning a word in the prospect's mind."

- **The word.** Not complicated, not invented: "The simple words are best, words taken right out of the dictionary." You "burn" your way into the mind by narrowing the focus to a single word or concept, "the ultimate marketing sacrifice." Federal Express put *overnight* into the mind by sacrificing its product line and doing overnight package delivery only.
- **The leader owns the category word.** IBM owns *computer*; the brand becomes the generic name ("We need an IBM machine"), a word so simple it is invisible. **Word association test** for a leadership claim: given *computer, copier, chocolate bar, cola*, people answer IBM, Xerox, Hershey's, Coke.
- **An astute leader goes one step further** and preempts the category's most important attribute. Heinz owns *ketchup*, and with "Slowest ketchup in the West" took *slow* (thickness), which helps hold a 50 percent share.
- **A non-leader's word** must have a narrow focus and must be **"available"** in the category: no one else can have a lock on it. Prego took *thicker* from Heinz's playbook and won 27 percent of spaghetti sauce against Ragu.
- **Simple and benefit oriented, and only one.** However complicated the product or the market's needs, focus on one word or benefit rather than two, three or four.
- **The halo effect.** Establish one benefit strongly and the prospect grants you others: a "thicker" sauce implies quality, nourishing ingredients and value; a "safer" car implies better design and engineering.
- **Owned words:** Crest, cavities. Mercedes, engineering. BMW, driving. Volvo, safety. Domino's, home delivery. Pepsi-Cola, youth. Nordstrom, service. Some words aren't worth owning (Volkswagen's *fahrvergnugen*).
- **Kinds of word:** benefit related (cavity prevention), service related (home delivery), audience related (younger people), sales related (preferred brand).
- **The opposite-proponents test.** You can't narrow the focus with *quality* or any idea nobody holds the opposite of. You can't run as the honest politician, because nobody will take the other side, but you can be the pro-business or the pro-labor candidate. GM ("Putting quality on the road", the "Mark of Excellence"), Ford ("Quality is Job 1") and Chrysler (Iacocca: "We don't want to be the biggest, we just want to be the best") all claim quality. It plays well inside the corporation and at dealer meetings, and falls apart outside: "everybody stands for quality. As a result, nobody does." Focusing on "the quality end of the market" is self-defeating unless you restrict yourself to high-priced products only, as Mercedes and BMW do.
- **Changing words** is hard but sometimes necessary, since nothing lasts forever. Lotus owned *spreadsheet*, which offered limited growth. After some conventional line extension (buying Ami Pro), it regrouped around *groupware*, software for networked PCs, and was first with a successful product. That is plausible because no one else owned the word and a large trend (more than half of business computers networked) carried it. Many companies see the value of a single concept, "the corporate vision," and neglect to be first to preempt the word.
- **Never leave your word for someone else's.** Atari owned *video games*, found the business faddish, and in 1982 set out to mean computers; CEO James Morgan said Atari must "broaden its business definition to electronic consumer products." Apple and IBM owned that word, the diversification was a disaster, and in 1986 Nintendo took over the concept Atari walked away from, and now has 75 percent of a multibillion-dollar market.
- **Get others to use your word.** Lawyers want to trademark everything; the trick is the reverse. "To be a leader you have to have followers." Other companies entering groupware would make the category, and Lotus's leadership, more impressive.
- **Protect the word.** BMW was the ultimate *driving* machine, then chased Mercedes with large 700-series sedans. A living room on wheels can't be the ultimate driving machine, and things went downhill until a new small BMW brought back *driving*.
- **Unselling works the same way.** The antidrug campaign has no single word and is all over the map, while both sides of the abortion issue focused on one word each: *pro-life* and *pro-choice*. The authors' proposal: make drugs socially unacceptable, as cigarettes became, with the ultimate down word, *loser*: "Drugs are for losers." Drugs cause losses of job, family, self-esteem, freedom and life, and the recreational user cares more about social status than getting high.

## 6. The Law of Exclusivity

"Two companies cannot own the same word in the prospect's mind."

- When a competitor owns a word or position, trying to own it too is futile, and usually reinforces the competitor by making its concept more important.
- **Volvo owns safety.** Mercedes-Benz, General Motors and others have run safety campaigns; none got into the mind with it. Atari couldn't take *home computer* from Apple and Commodore; *game computer* might have worked, because it built on Atari's existing perception.
- **Federal Express walked away from overnight** ("Overnight Letter" became "FedEx Letter"; "When it absolutely, positively has to be there overnight" was dropped) to chase *worldwide*, a word DHL Worldwide Express already owns ("Faster to more of the world"). FedEx must narrow its focus against DHL rather than try to own DHL's word.
- **Energizer and Duracell.** No number of pink bunnies will take *long-lasting* from Duracell, which got there first and has *Dura* in its name.
- **The research trap.** Research returns "a three-pound report" listing the attributes users want, and the conclusion that you should give it to them. Batteries die at bad moments, so the top attribute is long-lasting, so advertise long-lasting. "Right? Wrong." Researchers never mention that another company already owns the idea, and the theory that enough money can buy it is also wrong.
- **Burger King** found that the most popular fast-food attribute was *fast*, ignored that McDonald's owned it, and launched "Best food for fast times." It was a disaster close to its "Herb" campaign: agency fired, management fired, company sold.

## 14. The Law of Attributes

"For every attribute, there is an opposite, effective attribute."

- You can't own your competitor's word, so seek another attribute. Emulating the leader ("They must know what works," goes the rationale, "so let's do something similar") is "Not good thinking." Search for an opposite attribute that lets you play off against the leader: "The key word here is opposite—similar won't do."
- Coca-Cola was the original and so the choice of older people; Pepsi became the choice of the younger generation. Crest owned *cavities*, so other toothpastes took taste, whitening, breath protection and baking soda.
- "Marketing is a battle of ideas." You need an idea or attribute of your own to focus around. "Without one, you had better have a low price. A very low price."
- **Attributes are not equal.** Try to own the most important one (cavity prevention in toothpaste). Once a competitor has taken it, it's gone: move to a lesser attribute and accept a smaller share. Your job is then to seize a different attribute, dramatize its value, and so increase your share.
- **The opposite can outgrow the original.** IBM owned *big* and *powerful*; RCA, GE, UNIVAC, Burroughs, Honeywell, NCR and Control Data lost a lot of money attacking it. "An upstart from Boston" took *small*, the minicomputer was born, and *small* grew until IBM's mainframe empire was in serious trouble.
- **Never laugh at an opposite attribute.** Gillette, whose dominance rested on high-technology razors and cartridges, could have dismissed the French upstart's *disposable* razor with research showing America wanted hefty, expensive razors. It launched Good News instead, spent heavily, and won the disposables, which grew to dominate the razor blade business. "You can't predict the size of a new attribute's share, so never laugh."
- **When the exact opposite won't do, find the leader's other attribute.** Burger King can't take *fast* from McDonald's, and *slow* is no position for fast food. But McDonald's also owns *kids*. As Pepsi did against Coke, Burger King could position for the older crowd: anyone over about 10 who doesn't want to be seen as a kid. That means invoking the law of sacrifice (give the little kids to McDonald's), hanging "kiddie land" on McDonald's (law 9), and driving the idea in with a term such as *grow up*: "Grow up to the flame-broiled taste of Burger King." A program that would strike fear in the competitor's boardroom is a good sign.

## 15. The Law of Candor

"When you admit a negative, the prospect will give you a positive."

- It goes against corporate and human nature and the "think positive" creed, but one of the most effective ways into a mind is to admit a negative first and then twist it into a positive.
- **The book's lines:** "Avis is only No. 2 in rent-a-cars." "With a name like Smucker's, it has to be good." "The 1970 VW will stay ugly longer." "Joy. The most expensive perfume in the world." "The taste you hate twice a day" (Listerine).
- **Why it works.** Candor is disarming. A negative statement about yourself is instantly accepted as truth; a positive one is dubious, especially in an ad, and has to be proved. A negative needs no proof. A car that ugly must be reliable; a perfume people pay $375 an ounce for must be sensational; Avis must try harder. Communications growth has made people defensive about anyone selling them anything, and a message that starts with a problem makes them open their minds almost instinctively, as you lean in when someone brings you a problem. With the mind open, drive in the positive: your selling idea.
- **Marketing is often a search for the obvious.** Since minds can't be changed, use ideas already installed in the brain and "rub it in." Everyone knew Avis was second.
- **A bad name:** change it or make fun of it, as the Smucker family did, which is one reason Smucker's is the No. 1 jam and jelly. You can't ignore a bad name; Gablinger's, Grolsch and Gresedieck are not on supermarket shelves.
- **Listerine's answer to Scope.** When Scope launched a "good-tasting" mouthwash, Listerine couldn't say its taste wasn't all that bad; that would raise a red flag and reinforce the negative. It admitted people hated the taste, which set up "kills a lot of germs": anything that tastes like disinfectant must kill germs. General Foods called Grape-Nuts a "learned pleasure" and told people to "try it for a week"; sales rose 23 percent.
- **Use it with skill.** First, the negative must be widely perceived as a negative and trigger instant agreement; a negative that doesn't register leaves the prospect wondering "What's this all about?" Second, shift quickly to the positive. "The purpose of candor isn't to apologize. The purpose of candor is to set up a benefit that will convince your prospect."
