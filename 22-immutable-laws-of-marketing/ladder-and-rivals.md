# Competing by position: laws 7, 8, 9, 10 and 16

The laws of the ladder, duality, the opposite, division and singularity: how your rung sets your strategy, how categories mature and split, and how to find the one move that works.

## 7. The Law of the Ladder

"The strategy to use depends on which rung you occupy on the ladder."

- Being first into the mind should be the primary objective, but there are strategies for No. 2 and No. 3. Prospects keep a hierarchy: **for each category, a product ladder in the mind**, with a brand name on each rung. Car rental: Hertz on top, Avis second, National third. Your strategy depends on how soon you got into the mind, and so which rung you hold.
- **Acknowledge your rung.** Avis advertised itself as "Finest in rent-a-cars," and readers wondered how that could be when Avis wasn't on the top rung of their ladder. Then: "Avis is only No. 2 in rent-a-cars. So why go with us? We try harder." After 13 straight years of losses Avis began making a lot of money. ITT bought it and ran "Avis is going to be No. 1"; prospects said no, called Hertz, and the campaign was a disaster.
- **The misread lesson.** Avis did not win because it tried harder (had better service). It won because it related itself to Hertz's position in the mind. If trying harder were the secret, Harold Stassen would have been president many times over.
- **Compare only with what's on the prospect's ladder.** Adelphi University compares itself favorably with Harvard; the high school senior's ladder has no Adelphi rung, and Adelphi fails to attract top students.
- **The mind is selective.** It accepts only new data consistent with its product ladder in that category, and ignores everything else. Chrysler's ad reporting that 58 of 100 people preferred a used Dodge Spirit with 70,000 miles to a new Honda Accord moved very few Honda owners. "Ridiculous. (But not necessarily untrue.)"
- **How many rungs.** It depends on interest:
  - High-interest, used every day (cigarettes, cola, beer, toothpaste, cereal): many rungs.
  - Bought infrequently (furniture, lawn mowers, luggage): few rungs.
  - High personal pride (automobiles, watches, cameras): many rungs, though bought infrequently.
  - Bought infrequently and unpleasant (automobile batteries, tires, life insurance): very few.
  - Least pleasure, bought once in a lifetime: no rungs. Batesville caskets has almost 50 percent of the market, and nobody has heard of it.
- **Share follows the rung.** You tend to have twice the market share of the brand below you and half the share of the brand above. Acura, the first Japanese luxury car, sold 143,708 in a recent year; Lexus, second, 71,206; Infiniti, third, 34,890: almost exactly 4-2-1. (That is the early, high-interest stage; later the law of duality takes over.)
- **The "three leading brands" are never equals.** The leader dominates No. 2 and No. 2 smothers No. 3: Gerber, Beech-Nut, Heinz in baby food; Budweiser, Miller, Coors in beer; AT&T, MCI, Sprint in long distance.
- **The rule of seven.** Asked to name every brand they remember in a category, people rarely name more than seven, even in a high-interest category. George A. Miller found the average mind can't handle more than seven units at a time (seven-digit phone numbers, seven wonders, seven dwarfs).
- **Small fish, big pond.** Sometimes it's better to be No. 3 on a big ladder than No. 1 on a small one. 7-Up was top of the lemon-lime ladder (Sprite second), but almost two of three soft drinks in America are colas. "The Uncola" put 7-Up on the cola ladder as the alternative (as tea is to coffee), and it became the third largest-selling soft drink, until line extension cost it that place.
- **Before any program, ask:** Where are we on the ladder in the prospect's mind? On the top rung? The second? Not on the ladder at all? Then make the program deal realistically with that position.

## 8. The Law of Duality

"In the long run, every market becomes a two-horse race."

- A new category starts as a ladder of many rungs and gradually becomes a two-rung affair: Eveready and Duracell, Kodak and Fuji, Hertz and Avis, Listerine and Scope, McDonald's and Burger King, Nike and Reebok, Crest and Colgate. It is usually **the old reliable brand against the upstart**.
- **Shares are unstable; the law predicts the leader loses and No. 2 gains.** In 1969 the three cola brands held about 60, 25 and 6 percent; 22 years later Coca-Cola had 45, Pepsi-Cola 40, Royal Crown 3.
- **Third place in a maturing market is a hard place.** In 1969 Royal Crown revitalized 350 bottlers, hired the former president of Rival Pet Foods and a veteran of both Coke and Pepsi, and retained the agency Wells, Rich, Greene, whose head promised to go "for the jugular." The only brand killed was Royal Crown. Sprint, third in long distance at about 9 to 10 percent ($6 billion a year), is comfortable now and in serious long-term trouble; a betting person would back MCI, which won the battle for second and should become the upstart alternative to old reliable AT&T. Chrysler, despite Iacocca, is in trouble. In video games Nintendo had 75 percent in the late eighties with Sega and NEC behind; now Nintendo and Sega are neck and neck and NEC is far back. In airlines American (20 percent) will probably be the Coca-Cola of the skies, while Delta and United (18 percent each) fight over who becomes Pepsi and who goes down with Royal Crown.
- **Time frames vary:** video games played out in two or three seasons; long distance may take two or three decades.
- **Not preordained.** Other laws and well-tuned programs affect the result. A weak No. 3 makes little progress attacking the two strong leaders; what Royal Crown could have done is carve out a profitable niche (law 5). Knowing the long-run race has two horses helps you plan the short run.
- **When there's no clear-cut No. 2,** skill decides who takes the place. Laptops: Toshiba first at 21 percent, with Zenith, Compaq, NEC, Tandy and Sharp each at 8 to 10 percent, six horses coming round a turn with room for two. With 130 laptop brands, the law of duality will leave very few. The car industry ran the same course: 195 cars from 60 companies in 1904; in the next 10 years 531 companies formed and 346 died; 108 makers by 1923, 44 by 1927; now Ford and GM dominate.
- **Concentrate on the top two rungs.** Jack Welch at GE: "Only businesses that are No. 1 or No. 2 in their markets could win in the increasingly competitive global arena. Those that could not were fixed, closed, or sold." Procter & Gamble holds No. 1 or No. 2 in 32 of its 44 U.S. categories.
- **Why No. 3 and No. 4 look good early, then fade.** In a developing market new, unsophisticated customers pick brands that look interesting, often No. 3 or No. 4. As they get educated they want the leader, on the naive assumption that the leader must be better. The customer believes marketing is a battle of products: "They must be the best, they're the leaders."

## 9. The Law of the Opposite

"If you're shooting for second place, your strategy is determined by the leader."

- **"In strength there is weakness."** Wherever the leader is strong, a would-be No. 2 can turn the tables, as a wrestler uses an opponent's strength against him. Study the firm above you: where is it strong, and how do you turn that strength into a weakness?
- **Discover the essence of the leader and present the opposite.** "Don't try to be better, try to be different." It is often the upstart versus old reliable. Coca-Cola is the 100-year-old product with a formula known to seven people and locked in an Atlanta safe; Pepsi reversed that essence and became the choice of a new generation, the Pepsi Generation.
- **Two kinds of customer:** those who want to buy from the leader and those who don't. A potential No. 2 appeals to the second group, and by positioning against the leader takes business from all the other alternatives to No. 1. "If old people drink Coke and young people drink Pepsi, there's nobody left to drink Royal Crown cola."
- **Emulating the leader is usually an error;** present yourself as the alternative. Time built its name on colorful writing; Newsweek went straightforward: "We separate facts from opinions." Johnson & Johnson's Micrin (1961) stressed its "scientific" virtues and reached No. 2 within months, but Listerine's germ-fighting was scientific too. Procter & Gamble's Scope (1965) had the opposite position to itself and became No. 2; Micrin had 1 percent when it was withdrawn in 1978.
- **The two-edged sword.** Sometimes be brutal: Scope, the good-tasting mouthwash, hung "medicine breath" on Listerine. Don't simply knock the competitor. Hone in on a weakness the prospect will quickly acknowledge (one whiff of Listerine and you know your mouth would smell like a hospital), then quickly twist the sword (Scope is the good-tasting mouthwash that kills germs).
- **Repositioning the competitor.** Beck's could be neither the first imported beer (Heineken) nor the first German import (Lowenbrau). It repositioned Lowenbrau: "You've tasted the German beer that's the most popular in America. Now taste the German beer that's the most popular in Germany." It became the second largest-selling European beer in America, a rare case of overturning the law of leadership.
- **Old products accrue negative baggage,** especially in medicine. Aspirin dates from 1899; thousands of studies found stomach bleeding just in time for Tylenol's 1955 launch: "For the millions who should not take aspirin." Tylenol now outsells aspirin and is the largest-selling single product in American drugstores.
- **The negative needs a ring of truth.** Stolichnaya called Smirnoff, Samovar and Wolfschmidt fake Russian vodka by naming their real origins (Hartford, Connecticut; Schenley, Pennsylvania; Lawrenceburg, Indiana) against its own Leningrad. Royal Doulton ran "Royal Doulton, the china of Stoke-on-Trent, England vs. Lenox, the china of Pomona, New Jersey," exploiting the common belief that Lenox was imported, and became the "real English china."
- **"Marketing is often a battle for legitimacy."** The first brand to capture a concept can often portray competitors as illegitimate pretenders.
- **A No. 2 can't afford to be timid.** Give up focusing on No. 1 and you are exposed to the leader and the whole pack. Burger King's best years came on the attack: "Have it your way" (against McDonald's mass manufacturing), "Broiling, not frying," "The Whopper beats the big Mac." Then it went timid with "Herb the nerd," "The best food for fast times," "We do it the way you do it," "You've got to break the rules," and a push for little kids, McDonald's mainstay. Sales per unit fell and never came back.

## 10. The Law of Division

"Over time, a category will divide and become two or more categories."

- Like an amoeba in a petri dish, the market is an ever-expanding sea of categories. Computers split into mainframes, minicomputers, workstations, personal computers, laptops, notebooks and pen computers. Cars, once dominated by Chevrolet, Ford and Plymouth, split into luxury, moderate and inexpensive; full-size, intermediate and compact; sports cars, four-wheel drives, RVs and minivans. Television went from ABC, CBS and NBC with 90 percent of viewers to network, independent, cable, pay and public. Beer split into imported and domestic, premium and popular-priced, light, draft, dry and nonalcoholic. Music lost its single Top 40: Billboard now keeps 11 hit lists with 11 leaders. Even countries divide: about 35 in 1776, some 190 now.
- **Each segment is its own entity,** with its own reason for existence and its own leader, which is rarely the leader of the original category: IBM in mainframes, DEC in minis, Sun in workstations.
- **Categories are dividing, not combining.** Boardrooms believe in synergy, corporate alliances and convergence (IBM poised for the merging of television, music, publishing and computing). "It won't happen." The "financial services" trap caught Prudential, American Express and others: customers don't buy financial services; they buy stocks or life insurance or bank accounts, and prefer to buy each from a different company.
- **A leader keeps its dominance by meeting each emerging category with a different brand name,** as General Motors did with Chevrolet, Pontiac, Oldsmobile, Buick and Cadillac (and later Geo and Saturn).
- **Taking a well-known name into another category is the mistake.** Volkswagen introduced the small car to America; the Beetle took 67 percent of the imported-car market. Wanting to be like GM, VW sold bigger, faster, sportier cars under the same name ("Different Volks for different folks": the Beetle, 412 Sedan, Dasher, Thing, a station wagon), and only the Beetle sold. It then dropped the Beetle for big, fast, expensive Volkswagens (Vanagon, Sirocco, Jetta, Golf GL, Cabriolet) and built a Pennsylvania plant. The small-car category kept growing, buyers went to Toyota, Honda and Nissan, and VW's share fell below 4 percent. VW is the largest-selling car brand in Europe with the same cars; in America it means small and ugly, and nobody wants a big, beautiful Volkswagen.
- **Honda did it right:** it went up-market as Acura, with separate Acura dealerships to avoid confusion, made the first Japanese luxury car in America, and leads two categories.
- **Fear for existing brands is what stops leaders.** GM was slow to answer Mercedes and BMW's super premium category partly because a brand above Cadillac would enrage Cadillac dealers. It took Cadillac up-market with the $54,000 Allante instead, and it bombed: the neighbors would think you paid $30,000 or so, so there was no prestige. A new brand (perhaps the classic LaSalle) would have been better.
- **Timing.** You can be too early: the Nash Rambler was America's first small car in the fifties, and American Motors lacked the courage and money to wait for the category to develop. Still, "It's better to be early than late." You can't get into the mind first unless you're prepared to wait.

## 16. The Law of Singularity

"In each situation, only one move will produce substantial results."

- **The false model** is success as the sum of many small efforts beautifully executed. Leaders fritter resources across programs (the **puppy approach**: get into everything). Non-leaders copy the leader and try to do it a little better. "Trying harder is not the secret of marketing success": the difference between trying hard and easy is marginal, and in a big company the law of averages wipes it out.
- **The single, bold stroke** is the only thing that works, and in any situation there is only one move that produces substantial results. Generals look for the stroke the enemy least expects; finding one is difficult, finding more than one usually impossible. B.H. Liddell Hart calls it **"the line of least expectation"**: Normandy, whose tides and rocky shore the Germans thought unlikely for a landing; Hannibal over the Alps; the panzers through the Ardennes, around the Maginot Line, twice. Usually there is only one place a competitor is vulnerable, "and that place should be the focus of the entire invading force."
- **GM's Maginot Line.** GM's strength was the middle of the line, and it beat back frontal assaults by Ford, Chrysler and American Motors (the Edsel). The two strong moves against it were flanks: the Japanese at the low end with small cars, the Germans at the high end with super premium cars. Shoring up both ends while saving money, GM gave its midrange cars one body style, so no one could tell a Chevrolet from a Pontiac, Oldsmobile or Buick. That weakened the middle, opening it for Ford's European-styled Taurus and Sable and then Acura, Lexus and Infiniti. GM became weak across the board.
- **Coke's one move.** Coca-Cola fights on two fronts with Classic and New Coke ("an Edsel from Atlanta"), and a parade of slogans ("We have a taste for you," "The real choice," "Catch the Wave," "Red, white, and you," "You can't beat the feeling," "You can't beat the real thing") hasn't moved the needle. Generating every idea you can think of might stumble onto the right one, but it is inefficient. Coke's single move has two parts, a step back and a step forward: drop New Coke, which blocks the only weapon Coke has, then invoke the law of focus with the words Coke owns, the Real Thing, against Pepsi: "All right kids, we're not going to push you. When you're ready for the Real Thing, we've got it for you."
- **To find the singular move, be at the front.** Managers have to be "down at the front in the mud of the battle," knowing what works and what doesn't. Mistakes cost too much to delegate important marketing decisions. When GM's financial people took over, they cared about the numbers, not the brands, and the numbers went south along with the brands. The single move is hard to find from headquarters.
