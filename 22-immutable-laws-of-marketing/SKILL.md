# The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing

Al Ries and Jack Trout hold that marketing has laws as binding as gravity on an airplane. A well-designed, well-executed, well-financed program still fails if it violates one: "Violate them at your own risk." Programs that work are "in tune with some fundamental force in the marketplace." What looks like a competition or quality problem is usually a marketing problem: GM made its brands look alike and priced them alike, and ten share points, about $10 billion a year, evaporated. The laws apply to any product or service and anywhere in a company, and the authors' examples run from beer, cola, cars, computers, airlines and retailers to colleges, magazines, politics and an antidrug campaign. Work in that full range, and speak with the book's certainty: the authors state the laws as laws.

The laws rest on two models from the authors' earlier work: **positioning**, a physical model of the human mind, and **marketing warfare**, a military model of the market that puts each company or brand in a **defensive, offensive, flanking or guerrilla** mode.

## The premise every law derives from

- **Marketing is a battle of perceptions, not products.** "There is no objective reality. There are no facts. There are no best products." All that exists is perception in the mind of the customer or **prospect**. "The perception is the reality." Most marketing mistakes come from assuming a product battle rooted in reality, with the product as hero; so "the natural, logical way to market a product is invariably wrong."
- **The mind, not the marketplace, is the battlefield.** Being first in the mind is everything. Once a mind is made up it rarely if ever changes: "The single most wasteful thing you can do in marketing is try to change a mind." You blast your way into a mind; you can't worm your way in.
- **Narrowing the focus is the essence of marketing.** "You can't stand for something if you chase after everything."

The book's vocabulary, used throughout: **prospect** (the mind you are trying to get into), **category** (what the prospect files products under), **the word** (the single concept a brand owns in the mind), **the ladder** (the ranking of brands in a category in the mind), **line extension** (putting a successful brand name on another product), **the leader**, **No. 2**, **old reliable** versus **the upstart**.

## The 22 laws

Each law's statement is the authors' own. The reference files hold each law's mechanisms, tests and cases.

**Getting into the mind** (depth: [mind-and-category.md](mind-and-category.md))

1. **Leadership.** "It's better to be first than it is to be better." The leading brand in a category is almost always the first brand into the prospect's mind. The **Bert Hinkler route** (wait for the market to develop, then enter with a better product) is the losing one.
2. **Category.** "If you can't be first in a category, set up a new category you can be first in." Ask "First what?", not how the product is better When first, promote the category, not the brand.
3. **Mind.** "It's better to be first in the mind than it is to be first in the marketplace." Being first in the marketplace matters only insofar as it gets you into the mind first.
4. **Perception.** "Marketing is not a battle of products, it's a battle of perception." Buyers often decide on second-hand perception: the **"everybody knows" principle**.

**Owning a word** (depth: [words-and-attributes.md](words-and-attributes.md))

5. **Focus.** "The most powerful concept in marketing is owning a word in the prospect's mind." A simple dictionary word, benefit oriented, available, and one with proponents for its opposite.
6. **Exclusivity.** "Two companies cannot own the same word in the prospect's mind." Attacking a competitor's word only makes its concept more important.
14. **Attributes.** "For every attribute, there is an opposite, effective attribute." Seek the opposite of the leader's attribute: "similar won't do." No attribute of your own means you need a very low price.
15. **Candor.** "When you admit a negative, the prospect will give you a positive." The negative must be one everyone already accepts, and you must twist it at once into the selling benefit.

**Competing by position** (depth: [ladder-and-rivals.md](ladder-and-rivals.md))

7. **Ladder.** "The strategy to use depends on which rung you occupy on the ladder." Each rung tends to have twice the share of the one below and half the share of the one above.
8. **Duality.** "In the long run, every market becomes a two-horse race." Old reliable versus the upstart, with the leader losing share and No. 2 gaining.
9. **Opposite.** "If you're shooting for second place, your strategy is determined by the leader." Find the leader's essence and present the opposite: "don't try to be better, try to be different."
10. **Division.** "Over time, a category will divide and become two or more categories." Each new segment has its own leader. Categories divide; they don't converge.
16. **Singularity.** "In each situation, only one move will produce substantial results." The single bold stroke on Liddell Hart's **line of least expectation**.

**Keeping the brand narrow** (depth: [extension-and-sacrifice.md](extension-and-sacrifice.md))

11. **Perspective.** "Marketing effects take place over an extended period of time." Long-term effects are often the exact opposite of short-term ones: sales, coupons and line extensions lift business now and shrink it later.
12. **Line extension.** "There's an irresistible pressure to extend the equity of a brand." This is the most violated law. "In the long run and in the presence of serious competition, line extensions almost never work."
13. **Sacrifice.** "You have to give up something in order to get something." Sacrifice three things: product line, target market, constant change.

**Time, money and the organization** (depth: [future-money-and-management.md](future-money-and-management.md))

17. **Unpredictability.** "Unless you write your competitors' plans, you can't predict the future." Set a long-term direction, not a long-term plan. Ride trends without extrapolating them.
18. **Success.** "Success often leads to arrogance, and arrogance to failure." "Ego is the enemy of successful marketing." Keep the decision maker at the front.
19. **Failure.** "Failure is to be expected and accepted." Drop things instead of fixing them. Bring the **personal agenda** into the open.
20. **Hype.** "The situation is often the opposite of the way it appears in the press." "When you need the hype, it usually means you're in trouble."
21. **Acceleration.** "Successful programs are not built on fads, they're built on trends." Dampen a fad to stretch it into a trend.
22. **Resources.** "Without adequate funding an idea won't get off the ground." Get the idea first, then the money. A rich company must "spend enough."

## Applying the laws

Use the same pass to build a strategy and to diagnose a failing one. Run the steps in order; each ends on its criterion.

1. **Map the mind as it is.** For the category in question, write down what the prospect already believes: which brand is first, which word each brand owns (run the **word association test**: name the category and see which brand comes back), what "everybody knows", and where the product sits on the ladder. Count the rungs: rarely more than seven, and few or none if the product is bought rarely and unpleasantly. Judge this from the prospect's perception, never from product facts or specs. *Done when* you can name the leader, the word it owns, and your rung, or state that you are not on the ladder.
2. **Choose the move your rung allows.**
   - *Not first and not on the ladder:* set up a new category you can be first in (law 2). Promote the category, give it a new brand name that can work generically, and be ready to wait for it to develop.
   - *Leader:* own the category word, then preempt the category's most important attribute. Meet each new category with a new brand, as GM once did, and stay flexible enough to attack yourself with a new idea.
   - *Would-be No. 2:* study the leader, find the strength at its essence and turn it into a weakness (law 9). Appeal to the people who don't want to buy from the leader. Stay on the attack; a timid No. 2 loses ground to the pack as well as the leader.
   - *Weak No. 3 or below in a maturing category:* don't attack the two leaders head-on. Carve a profitable niche with its own word, or climb onto a bigger ladder as an alternative to it (7-Up as "The Uncola"): No. 3 on a big ladder can beat No. 1 on a small one.

   *Done when* the move follows from the position found in step 1, and is not a plan to be "better."
3. **Pick the one word.** It is simple, from the dictionary, benefit oriented, not locked up by anyone else in the category, and has proponents for its opposite (no "quality," no "honest politician"). Where a leader owns an attribute, take the opposite one. Where a real weakness is common knowledge, consider admitting it and twisting it into the benefit (law 15). *Done when* the word passes all of those tests and is a single word or concept, not a list of benefits.
4. **Decide what to sacrifice.** Name what goes from the product line, which part of the market you will not target (the target is not the market: Marlboro aims at the cowboy and sells to everybody), and which strategy you will keep unchanged at the next budget review. *Done when* each of the three sacrifices is named, and the brand stands for the word alone.
5. **Find the single move.** Of the possible programs, pick the one bold stroke that exploits the one point where the competitor is vulnerable, and put the entire force behind it. It can come in two parts, as Coke's does: a step back that drops whatever blocks your only weapon, then a step forward that uses it. *Done when* there is one move. A slate of slogans or a portfolio of small efforts means the step is not done.
6. **Run the violation check.** Hold the plan or the failing program against every law, looking for:
   - Trying to change a made-up mind, or claiming a better product (laws 3, 4).
   - Taking a word someone else owns, emulating the leader, or chasing a research report's wish list of attributes (laws 6, 14).
   - Calling yourself the finest when you aren't on the top rung, or comparing yourself to a brand that isn't on the prospect's ladder (law 7).
   - Putting the brand name on a new category, adding flavors or forms, or building a strategy "tent" big enough to hold everything (laws 10, 12).
   - Leaning on sales, rebates, coupons or yo-yo pricing (law 11).
   - Gearing up for a fad as if it were a trend, or fanning it by putting the hot name on everything (law 21).
   - Basing the plan on forecasts, extrapolated trends, the press's story, or research about an idea people have no frame of reference for (laws 17, 20).
   - Assuming the famous name caused the success, delegating marketing to an underling or a finance department, or keeping a failure alive by reorganizing (laws 18, 19).
   - Launching without the money to get in and stay in the mind (law 22).

   *Done when* all 22 laws have been held against the plan and each violation is named with its law and its remedy.
7. **Set a long-term direction.** Good short-term planning finds the angle or word; the long-term direction builds a program to maximize it (Domino's: "home delivery" now, the first nationwide home delivery chain over time). Front-load the money and expect effects to take years. *Done when* the direction names the word and how the program will drive it into the mind, with no forecast standing in for it.

When you report, name the laws in play by their names ("this violates the law of exclusivity"), cite the book's case that fits the situation, and give the move, not a list of options.

## Selling the laws inside a company

The laws fly in the face of corporate ego, conventional wisdom and the quality movement, so expect resistance and meet it law by law:

- **Perception and leadership** upset a culture of benchmarking the leader and setting out to "beat their specs." People want to believe they reached the top by being better, not by being first.
- **Sacrifice** runs into the urge to offer everything for everybody, and into bright young marketers who must tinker to make their mark.
- **Focus** forces pruning. Ask "What word does your company own in the minds of your prospects?"; "We make a variety of products for many different industries" is the answer that shows the problem.
- **Perspective** frustrates the accountants, who want results this quarter.
- **Line extension** is the hardest. It means demolishing the boardroom belief that big brands have an equity that can stretch over different products, and hardly a director in a dozen will challenge it. Sometimes you must wait management out: "Management is mutable, but the laws of marketing are not."

Violate the laws and you risk failure; apply them and you risk being bad-mouthed, ignored or ostracized. The authors' counsel is patience: "success is the best revenge of all."
