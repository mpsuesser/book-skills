# Time, money and the organization: laws 17 to 22

The laws of unpredictability, success, failure, hype, acceleration and resources: planning without forecasts, keeping ego and personal agendas out of marketing decisions, reading the press and fads, and funding the idea.

## 17. The Law of Unpredictability

"Unless you write your competitors' plans, you can't predict the future."

- Most marketing plans assume something about the future, and plans built on what will happen are usually wrong. Hundreds of computers and an army of meteorologists can't predict the weather three days out; you can't predict your market three years out.
- **Failure to forecast competitive reaction** is a major reason marketing fails. IBM's OfficeVision, a massive plan to hook every PC to its mainframes, was dead in the water thanks to Sun, Microsoft and others: it "foresaw everything but the competition." Asked who was responsible for Gettysburg, Pickett said he'd always thought the Yankees had something to do with it.
- **Short-term financial thinking is the real problem,** not short-term marketing thinking. Companies living from quarterly report to quarterly report "live by the numbers, die by the numbers." Harold Geneen drove ITT's managers for ever-rising earnings and built a house of cards: "Good accounting, bad marketing." GM did fine until financial people let Alfred P. Sloan's plan of differentiated brands fall apart, with every division head chasing the middle of the market to make short-term numbers.
- **Plan short, direct long.** Good short-term planning is finding the angle or word that differentiates your product or company. Then set "a coherent long-term marketing direction" that builds a program to maximize that idea or angle. "It's not a long-term plan, it's a long-term direction." Tom Monaghan's short-term angle was the *home delivery* idea and a system that delivered pizzas fast; his long-term direction was the first nationwide home delivery chain, built as fast as possible. He couldn't own *home delivery* until he had enough franchisees to afford national advertising. Domino's became a $2.65 billion company without a complex 10-year plan.
- **Ride trends.** You can't predict the future but you can get a handle on trends, and so take advantage of change. America's growing health orientation opened the door to healthier foods; ConAgra's Healthy Choice (March 1989) was the first to use a simple name and concept for it, while earlier low-sodium, low-fat and light brands were buried under line extension names. ConAgra is now extending Healthy Choice far past entrées, violating the law of sacrifice.
- **Two errors with trends.** Extrapolation: jumping to conclusions about how far a trend will go (by the forecasts, everyone should be eating broiled fish and mesquite chicken; hamburgers are doing fine). And its twin: assuming the future replays the present, which is as much a prediction as assuming change. **Peter's Law:** "The unexpected always happens."
- **Market research measures the past.** New ideas are almost impossible to measure, because nobody has a frame of reference and people don't know what they'll do until they face an actual decision. Research before Xerox's plain-paper copier concluded nobody would pay five cents a copy when Thermofax charged a cent and a half. Xerox ignored it.
- **Build in flexibility,** and be willing to change quickly when change sweeps through your category. GM was slow on small cars and paid dearly; IBM is slow to acknowledge the trend away from mainframes. The authors' suggestion for IBM against workstations: a new generic, "PMs," for "personal mainframe," two words IBM already owns. It would terrify IBM's mainframe and PC divisions, and might undermine both, but "a company must be flexible enough to attack itself with a new idea."
- **Predicting the future differs from taking a chance on it.** Orville Redenbacher's Gourmet Popping Corn took a chance that people would pay twice as much for a high-end popcorn. "No one can predict the future with any degree of certainty. Nor should marketing plans try to."

## 18. The Law of Success

"Success often leads to arrogance, and arrogance to failure."

- "Ego is the enemy of successful marketing. Objectivity is what's needed." Successful people grow less objective and substitute their own judgment for what the market wants. Donald Trump and Robert Maxwell were blinded by early success.
- **Success breeds line extension.** When a brand succeeds, the company assumes the name was the reason and plasters it on other products. It's the opposite: the name didn't make the brand famous (though a bad name might have kept it from fame); the right moves did. You got into the mind first, narrowed the focus and preempted a powerful attribute. The more you identify with your brand or corporate name, the more likely the line extension trap ("It can't be the name," you think when things go wrong; "We have a great name."). Monaghan admitted his foray into frozen pizzas, sold in bars and restaurants, was a disaster that cost Domino's stores.
- **Ego belongs in building the business, not in the marketing.** Ego can drive a business. What hurts is injecting it into the marketing process. Brilliant marketers think the way a prospect thinks, put themselves in their customers' shoes, and don't impose their own view of the world, which is all perception anyway.
- **Arrogance at the top.** GM, Sears and IBM came to feel they could do anything in the market. DEC's Ken Olsen built a $14 billion company and then pooh-poohed the personal computer, open systems and RISC, three of the biggest developments in computing. "A trend is like the tide—you don't fight it." Olsen is out.
- **Distance from the front.** The bigger the company, the more likely the CEO has lost touch with the front lines, perhaps the single most important limit on a corporation's growth. Every other factor favors size: "Marketing is war, and the first principle of warfare is the principle of force." The bigger army has the advantage, and gives some of it up if it can't stay focused on the battle in the customer's mind. Ross Perot, on GM's board, spent weekends visiting dealers and buying cars, criticized Roger Smith for not doing the same, and wanted to "nuke the GM system": heated garages, chauffeur-driven limousines (for a company trying to sell cars), executive dining rooms.
- **Getting objective news, bad as well as good, past a middle management that tells you what you want to hear:** go **"in disguise"** or unannounced, especially at the distributor or retailer level, like the king who dresses as a commoner to hear honest opinions. Chief executives, like kings, rarely get them from their ministers.
- **Reclaim the time.** By one survey the average CEO works 61 hours a week: 18 on outside activities, 17 in corporate meetings and 6 preparing for them, leaving 20 for everything else. So they delegate marketing, which is a mistake: "Marketing is too important to be turned over to an underling." Delegate the chairmanship of the next fund-raising drive instead (the vice president, not the president, attends state funerals), cut the meetings, and go see for yourself. Gorbachev to Reagan: "It is better to see once than to hear a hundred times."
- Small companies are mentally closer to the front, one reason they grew faster.

## 19. The Law of Failure

"Failure is to be expected and accepted."

- Too many companies try to fix things rather than drop them; "Let's reorganize to save the situation" is a way of life. Admitting a mistake and doing nothing about it is bad for your career. Recognize failure early and cut your losses. American Motors should have dropped passenger cars and focused on Jeep. IBM should have dropped copiers, and Xerox computers, years before they did.
- **The Japanese** admit mistakes early and change. Consensus management removes the ego: with many people holding a small piece of a big decision, "We were all wrong" carries no career damage, unlike "I was wrong." They admit, fix, and keep coming, which makes them relentless marketers.
- **Wal-Mart's "ready, fire, aim,"** from Sam Walton's constant tinkering: nobody hits the target every time, and failed experiments aren't punished. You get credit for learning and trying, "But woe to the person who makes the same mistake twice."
- **The personal agenda** is the insidious disease: marketing decisions made first with the decision maker's career in mind and only second with the competition in mind. The built-in conflict between personal and corporate agendas kills risk-taking, and it's hard to be first in a new category without sticking your neck out. A senior executive with a high salary and little time to retirement won't make a bold move; junior executives make "safe" decisions to protect their climb. "Nobody has ever been fired for a bold move they didn't make." Sound ideas get rejected because no one in top management will personally benefit.
- **Bring the agenda into the open.** 3M's **"champion"** system publicly names the person who will benefit from a new product's success; Art Fry championed Post-it Notes through almost a dozen years to market. Ideally managers would judge a concept on its merits, not on whom it benefits, which takes teamwork, esprit de corps and a self-sacrificing leader. Patton's Third Army took more territory and prisoners faster than any army in history; Eisenhower fired him.

## 20. The Law of Hype

"The situation is often the opposite of the way it appears in the press."

- When IBM was succeeding it said very little; now it holds lots of press conferences. "When things are going well, a company doesn't need the hype. When you need the hype, it usually means you're in trouble." Young reporters are more impressed by other publications than by their own reporting, so hype keeps rolling once it starts.
- **Hyped failures.** New Coke got more than $1 billion of free publicity plus hundreds of millions in launch spending; within 60 days the original formula was back, and Classic outsells New about 15 to 1. USA Today launched in 1982 with the president, the speaker of the House and the Senate majority leader, and the residue of that hype is so strong people can't believe it's a loser. Steve Jobs's Next drew so many press requests he printed tickets, and IBM, Ross Perot and Canon invested $130 million. "Will Next be a winner? Of course not. Where is the opening? Next is the first in a new category of what?" Also: the Tucker 48 (51 built), the U.S. Football League, Videotext, the automated factory, the personal helicopter, the manufactured home, the picturephone, polyester suits.
- **The tell: hype says existing products will become obsolete.** Polyester would replace wool, Videotext newspapers, the personal helicopter roads. In the "office of the future" everything would merge into one machine; offices still have separate computers, laser printers, fax machines, copiers and postal meters. The greatest hype goes to developments that promise to change an entire industry single-handedly, preferably one vital to the economy: the post-war helicopter in every garage, the manufactured home off the assembly line, no-frills food that would make brands obsolete, the pen computer that would put computers in reach of people who can't type. The videophone story is really about the travel industry ("An Alternative To Travel"); AT&T has failed with it at $100 a month in the seventies and $2,300 an hour in the eighties, and is selling $1,500 units now. Who wants to get dressed up to make a phone call? Each hyped story has a grain of truth (a small market for helicopters, pen computers for traveling salespeople), but hype is hype.
- **"Capturing the imagination of the public is not the same as revolutionizing a market."** Such predictions violate the law of unpredictability. "The only revolutions you can predict are the ones that have already started." The press picked up the collapse of communism only after it began.
- **Real revolutions arrive quietly.** The press covered Toyopet's little cars falling apart on American roads, not a coming shake-up (renamed Toyota, it became a big winner). It ignored MCI's first microwave link between Chicago and St. Louis and Sun's first workstation. The personal computer appeared in 1974, IBM answered six years later, and the PC boomed only a year and a half after that, with Lotus 1-2-3. "Forget the front page." Look for clues to the future in the innocuous little stories in the back of the paper. "Real revolutions arrive unannounced in the middle of the night and kind of sneak up on you."

## 21. The Law of Acceleration

"Successful programs are not built on fads, they're built on trends."

- **"A fad is a wave in the ocean, and a trend is the tide."** A fad gets a lot of hype, is very visible, and rises and falls in a hurry. A trend gets very little hype, is almost invisible, and is very powerful over the long term. A **fashion** is a fad that repeats itself: short skirts, double-breasted suits, Halley's Comet.
- **The danger is gearing up for a fad as if it were a trend,** then being stuck with staff, factories and distribution when it goes. Atari is typical. Coleco milked the Cabbage Patch Kids (1983) with hundreds of novelties, reached $776 million in sales and $83 million in profit two years later, and was in Chapter 11 by 1988. Hasbro bought the Kids in 1989, handled them conservatively, and they're doing well.
- **The paradox: dampen the fad.** Faced with a rapidly rising business that has every mark of a fad, the best thing to do is dampen it, which stretches it out into something more like a trend. Owners of hot toys who put the name on everything fan the fad into a collapse: "When everybody has a Ninja turtle, nobody wants one anymore." Barbie was never heavily merchandised into other areas and became a long-term trend.
- **Control appearances.** The most successful entertainers don't overextend or wear out their welcome. Colonel Parker deliberately restricted Elvis Presley's appearances and records, so each appearance was an event.
- "One way to maintain a long-term demand for your product is to never totally satisfy the demand." The best, most profitable thing to ride is a long-term trend.

## 22. The Law of Resources

"Without adequate funding an idea won't get off the ground."

- **Marketing help is not what a good idea needs first.** Marketing is fought in the prospect's mind, and you need money to get into a mind and money to stay there. "You'll get further with a mediocre idea and a million dollars than with a great idea alone." Jobs and Wozniak had the idea; Mike Markkula's $91,000 (for a third of Apple) put it on the map.
- **Use the idea to find the money,** not marketing help; the marketing can come later. "An idea without money is worthless. Be prepared to give away a lot for the funding."
- **The routes and their costs.** Advertising is expensive: a one-minute Super Bowl commercial costs $1.5 million. Publicity isn't free advertising; the **5-10-20 rule of thumb** puts a small public relations agency at $5,000 a month, a medium one at $10,000 and a big-time one at $20,000. Only a tiny share of entrepreneurs get funded by venture capital. Large companies accept very few outside ideas; the real hope is persuading a smaller company.
- **Shortcuts to the money:** marry it (Georgette Mosbacher raised $31.5 million from many sources to buy La Prairie, lifted sales 30 percent in a year, and sold at a hefty profit); divorce it (Frances Lear, at 61 and freshly divorced, was prepared to spend $25 million of her expected $112 million settlement on Lear's, a magazine for women over 40, which had 350,000 readers by its fifth issue); find it at home (Trump's father's millions); or "share" the idea by franchising (Domino's).
- **The rich get richer** because they can drive ideas into the mind. Procter & Gamble and Philip Morris each spend more than $2 billion a year on advertising, GM $1.5 billion. Their problem is telling good ideas from bad and not spending on too many products and programs (law 5). A small company can lose its own breakthrough to money: A&M Pet Products invented clumping cat litter, Scoop Away; Golden Cat, owner of No. 1 Tidy Cat, launched Tidy Scoop and borrowed the "Scoop," and money will probably decide who wins.
- **Technical and business products** need less money (shorter prospect lists, cheaper media) but still need adequate funding for brochures, sales presentations, trade shows and advertising.
- **For a rich company the answer is to spend enough.** The military always errs on the high side; plenty of rations were left after Desert Storm. "You can't save your way to success." The more successful marketers **front load** their investment: no profit for two or three years while all earnings go back into marketing.
- Bottom line: "First get the idea, then go get the money to exploit it."
