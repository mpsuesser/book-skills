# Getting into the mind: laws 1 to 4

The laws of leadership, category, mind and perception, with their tests and cases. Laws 3 and 4 modify law 1: being first matters because of what happens in the mind.

## 1. The Law of Leadership

"It's better to be first than it is to be better."

- **The basic issue in marketing is creating a category you can be first in**, not convincing prospects you have a better product. If you hold a small share against bigger, better-financed competitors, your strategy was probably faulty from the start. It is much easier to get into the mind first than to convince someone you are better than the brand that got there first.
- **Lindbergh and Hinkler.** Charles Lindbergh was the first to fly the Atlantic solo. Bert Hinkler was second, and a better pilot (faster, less fuel), and nobody has heard of him. Most companies still take the **Bert Hinkler route**: wait until a market develops, then jump in with a better product, often under the corporate name. A me-too product with a line extension name "has little hope of becoming a big, profitable brand."
- **The first brand leads, in hard categories and soft.** Hertz in rent-a-cars, IBM in computers, Coca-Cola in cola. Heineken was the first imported beer to make a name in America after World War II; four decades and 425 imported brands later it is still No. 1, with 30 percent. Miller Lite was the first domestic light beer. Jeep (four-wheel-drive off-road vehicles), Acura (luxury Japanese cars), IBM (mainframes), Sun Microsystems (workstations), Gillette (safety razor), Tide (laundry detergent), Hayes (computer modem) all lead. Chrysler introduced the minivan: 10 percent of the car market, 50 percent of minivans. Hewlett-Packard introduced the desktop laser printer: 5 percent of personal computers, 45 percent of laser printers. Magazines too: Time leads Newsweek, People leads Us, Playboy leads Penthouse. TV Guide had only a minuscule head start, and the powerful Curtis Publishing Company's rival listings magazine never got off the ground.
- **The "leading for first" test.** You can find the first brand in a category by asking for the leading one. Harvard is both the leading college and the first founded in America. (The second was the College of William and Mary.)
- **Why the first stays ahead.** People stick with what they've got: it isn't worth switching to someone a little better than your spouse. Twins say that whichever one a person meets first stays the favorite.
- **The name goes generic.** Xerox, Kleenex, Coke, Scotch tape, Band-Aid, Fiberglas, Formica, Gore-Tex, Jello, Krazy Glue, Q-tips, Saran Wrap, Velcro; "FedEx this package to the Coast." Rule: when you introduce the first brand in a new category, always try to select a name that can work generically. Lawyers advise the opposite.
- **Followers often finish in their order of entry.** In ibuprofen, Advil was first (51 percent), Nuprin second (10 percent), Medipren third (1 percent). Motrin IB came fourth and has 15 percent despite owning the prescription name; Advil had launched with "Same as the prescription drug Motrin." Doctors say "Take two Advil." Tylenol, the first acetaminophen, is so far ahead that No. 2 is hard to identify.
- **Benchmarking doesn't work.** Benchmarking, touted as the "ultimate competitive strategy" and part of "total quality management", is the better-product strategy. "Regardless of reality, people perceive the first product into the mind as superior."
- **Limits.** Not every first succeeds. A first can come too late: USA Today, the first national newspaper, has lost $800 million without a profitable year, perhaps because a national newspaper is too late in a television era. Some firsts are bad ideas: Frosty Paws, the first ice cream for dogs. The dogs love it, but the owners buy the groceries and think dogs don't need their own ice cream.
- Charles Schwab, "America's largest discount broker," is the Lindbergh of discount brokerage. Second is Buzz Aldrin, John Landy, John Adams, and some unknown English muffin after Thomas' and sports drink after Gatorade. Being second isn't fatal: the other laws apply.

## 2. The Law of the Category

"If you can't be first in a category, set up a new category you can be first in."

- **Amelia Earhart** was the third person to fly the Atlantic solo, and is known as the first woman to do it.
- **Don't follow the success into its category; open the next one.** After Heineken, Anheuser-Busch reasoned that a market for a high-priced imported beer might mean one for a high-priced domestic beer: Michelob, which outsells Heineken two to one. Its me-too import, Carlsberg, went nowhere. An importer took five years to ask the same question of Miller Lite, and Amstel Light became the largest-selling imported light beer.
- **Computers, category by category.** After IBM came "Snow White and the seven dwarfs" (Burroughs, Control Data, General Electric, Honeywell, NCR, RCA, Sperry), and none grew into the No. 2. Digital Equipment Corporation did, by being first in minicomputers: 126,000 employees and $14 billion in sales. Tandem was first in fault-tolerant computers ($1.9 billion); Stratus "stepped down" with the first fault-tolerant minicomputer ($500 million). Cray was first in supercomputers ($800 million); Convex launched the first mini supercomputer ($200 million). Commodore was going nowhere until it positioned the Amiga as the first multimedia computer (more than $500 million a year). Dell entered a crowded field as the first to sell computers by phone ($900 million).
- **There are many ways to be first.** Schwab opened the first discount broker, not a better broker. Lear's was the first magazine for the mature woman ("The magazine for the woman who wasn't born yesterday").
- **The launch question.** For any new product, the first question is not "How is this new product better than the competition?" but "First what?": what category is it first in?
- **Forget the brand. Think categories.** Classic marketing is brand oriented: how do I get people to prefer my brand? Prospects are on the defensive about brands, because everyone says theirs is better, but open-minded about categories. "Everyone is interested in what's new. Few people are interested in what's better."
- **When first in a new category, promote the category.** You have no competition. DEC told prospects why to buy a minicomputer, not a DEC minicomputer. In the early days Hertz sold rent-a-car service and Coca-Cola sold refreshment, and their marketing worked better then.
- The laws are simple; working them out in practice is another matter.

## 3. The Law of the Mind

"It's better to be first in the mind than it is to be first in the marketplace"

- **Firsts in the marketplace that vanished:** the MITS Altair 8800, the first personal computer (an unfortunate choice of names); Du Mont (first commercial television set), Duryea (first automobile), Hurley (first washing machine). IBM was not first with the mainframe; Remington Rand's UNIVAC was. A massive marketing effort got IBM into the mind first.
- The law follows from the law of perception: if marketing is a battle of perception, the mind takes precedence over the marketplace.
- **The entrepreneur's problem** is not the idea but getting it into the prospect's mind. The conventional answer is money (law 22), but more money is wasted in marketing than in any other human activity, outside government.
- **You can't change a mind once it's made up.** Going at one is a head-on charge against an entrenched enemy: the Light Brigade at Balaclava, Pickett at Gettysburg. Wang was first in word processors and, after millions spent promoting its personal computers and minicomputers, is still seen as a word processor company. Xerox spent 25 years and $2 billion and is nowhere in computers.
- **Blast, don't worm.** To make a big impression you can't slowly build a favorable opinion over time. People file you away as a certain kind of person once they perceive you one way, and you can't become a different person in their minds.
- **Money works on open minds.** A few dollars can work a miracle where the mind is open; millions can't save a company where it isn't. Apple got off the ground with Mike Markkula's $91,000, helped by a simple, easy-to-remember name. Against it on the launching pad stood the Commodore Pet, IMSAI 8080, MITS Altair 8800 and Radio Shack TRS-80.

## 4. The Law of Perception

"Marketing is not a battle of products, it's a battle of perception."

- **The illusion.** Marketers research, "getting the facts," make sure truth is on their side, and sail in sure the best product will win. "There is no objective reality. There are no facts. There are no best products." "The perception is the reality. Everything else is an illusion." Saying "I'm right and the next person is wrong" only says you think you are a better perceiver. Truth is "one expert's perception", and an expert is someone perceived as one by someone else. "Marketing is a manipulation of those perceptions."
- **Why marketers cling to facts:** they believe in objective reality, and if you think you need the best product to win, it takes only a minor modification of your own perceptions to believe you have it. Customers, meanwhile, assume they are right after a modicum of experience with a category; to them a perception reads as universal truth.
- **The laws invert the instinct.** Most marketing mistakes stem from assuming a product battle rooted in reality. Only by studying how perceptions form and aiming the program at them can you overcome your incorrect marketing instincts.
- **Same product, different perception.** The same Honda, Toyota and Nissan cars sell in Japan and the United States. In America Honda leads; in Japan it is third, and Toyota sells more than four times as many cars, because there Honda got into the mind as a motorcycle maker. Tell a friend in Tokyo you bought a Honda and they ask what kind of motorcycle. By the same logic a Harley-Davidson car would fail however good it was. Campbell's soup is No. 1 in the United States and nowhere in the United Kingdom; Heinz soup is the reverse.
- **Taste is perception.** The Coca-Cola Company ran 200,000 taste tests showing New Coke beat Pepsi and Pepsi beat the original formula. New Coke is in third place; Coca-Cola Classic, which tested worst, is first. "You believe what you want to believe. You taste what you want to taste."
- **The "everybody knows" principle.** Buyers often decide on someone else's perception of reality. Everybody knows the Japanese make higher-quality cars, so a bad Japanese car is bad luck and a good American one is good luck; own experience is twisted to fit the perception. Audi: after CBS's "60 Minutes" segment "Out of Control" (November 23, 1986) on "unintended acceleration," U.S. sales fell from 60,000 in 1986 to 12,000 in 1991, though no automobile expert could duplicate the complaint. When Audi's ads cite German experts rating it above Mercedes and BMW: "Do you believe that? Probably not. Is it true? Does it matter?"
