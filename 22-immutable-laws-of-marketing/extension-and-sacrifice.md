# Keeping the brand narrow: laws 11, 12 and 13

The laws of perspective, line extension and sacrifice: why moves that lift sales now erode the brand later, and what to give up instead.

## 11. The Law of Perspective

"Marketing effects take place over an extended period of time."

- **Alcohol** is chemically a strong depressant, but in the short term, by depressing inhibitions, it acts like a stimulant. Many marketing moves behave the same: the long-term effects are often the exact opposite of the short-term ones.
- **Sales.** A sale lifts business now and lowers it later by educating customers not to buy at "regular" prices. It also tells prospects your regular prices are too high, and customers avoid a store with a "sale" reputation once the sale ends, so stores end up running near-continuous sales. Auto rebates rose as U.S. vehicle sales fell five years running. Seamans, the largest furniture company in the New York area, ran a sale every week and went bankrupt.
- **Couponing.** No evidence shows it increases sales in the long run. Companies need a quarterly dose to keep sales level, and sales drop when they stop: you keep couponing not to raise sales but to keep them from falling. "Couponing is a drug." Coupons, discounts and sales all teach consumers to buy only on a deal. The retail winners practice **"everyday low prices"** (Wal-Mart, K Mart, the warehouse outlets), against the **yo-yo pricing** of airlines and supermarkets; Procter & Gamble's move to uniform pricing may start a trend.
- **Line extension** invariably raises sales in the short term. Miller High Life was growing about 27 percent a year on the blue-collar "Miller Time" campaign when Miller introduced Miller Lite in 1974, "a brilliant concept" (law 2) buried under a line-extended name. High Life's sales nearly tripled in the next five years, from 8.6 to 23.6 million barrels by 1979: the short-term effect. Then it fell 13 years in a row to 5.8 million in 1991. Miller Genuine Draft (1986) caught on as first in a new category, but carried the Miller name, and five years later Miller Lite peaked and began to decline. "Once started, the decline is almost impossible to stop."
- **The same pattern elsewhere:** three years after Michelob Light, regular Michelob peaked and fell 11 years running; today all four Michelobs (regular, light, dry, classic dark) sell 25 percent fewer barrels than Michelob alone did in 1978. Coors Light collapsed Coors regular to a quarter of its old sales. Budweiser, up every year since Prohibition, has slipped three years running because of Bud Light. The excuse that light beer took over the market fails: 18 years after Miller Lite, light beer is 31 percent of the market.
- **Faster cycles.** Murjani introduced Coca-Cola clothes in 1985; two years later wholesale volume reached $250 million, and the next year the line dried up almost overnight, leaving millions in inventory. Donald Trump put his name on a hotel, three casinos, two condominiums, an airline and a shopping center, was celebrated by Fortune, Time and Newsweek, and ended $1.4 billion in debt. "What made him successful in the short term is exactly what caused him to fail in the long term. Line extension."
- **Why it's hard to see:** managers focused on the next quarterly report can't see effects that land years later. If a bullet took five years to reach its target, few criminals would be convicted of homicide. "It looks easy, but marketing is not a game for amateurs."

## 12. The Law of Line Extension

"There's an irresistible pressure to extend the equity of a brand."

- **The most violated law,** and it happens continuously with almost no conscious effort, like a closet or desk drawer filling up. One day a company is tightly focused on one highly profitable product; the next it is spread thin over many and losing money.
- **IBM** made a fortune focused on mainframes. Now it is in everything (personal computers, pen computers, workstations, midrange, software, networks, telephones, the PCjr) and lost $2.8 billion on $65 billion of revenue in 1991, almost $8 million a day. It lost millions on copiers (sold to Kodak), Rolm (sold to Siemens), Satellite Business Systems (shut down), Prodigy, SAA, TopView, OfficeVision and OS/2.
- **Success plants the seeds of future problems.** Microsoft's strategy is, in a word, "more": a dominant share, maybe 70 percent, in every major PC applications category. That is setting itself up as the next IBM. Microsoft leads operating systems and trails in spreadsheets (Lotus), word processing (WordPerfect) and business graphics (Harvard Graphics). "When you try to be all things to all people, you inevitably wind up in trouble." One manager: "I'd rather be strong somewhere than weak everywhere."
- **Line extension in the narrow sense:** taking the name of a successful product (A-1 steak sauce) and putting it on a new one (A-1 poultry sauce). The logic sounds airtight: people are moving from beef to chicken, and the name tells them it comes from the makers of that great steak sauce. But in the mind A-1 is not a brand name; it is the steak sauce itself. Nobody answers "Would you pass me the A-1?" with "A-1 what?" The poultry launch failed despite an $18 million advertising budget.
- **The verdict:** there are as many ways to line extend as galaxies, and new ones daily, and "In the long run and in the presence of serious competition, line extensions almost never work."
- **Flavors** look like a way to more share and aren't. 7-Up had 5.7 percent of soft drinks in 1978 as the lemon-lime uncola; after 7-Up Gold, Cherry 7-Up and assorted diets it has 2.5 percent. Line extensions are one reason stores are choked: 1,300 shampoos, 200 cereals, 250 soft drinks.
- **The leader is the brand that isn't line extended.** Gerber has 72 percent of baby food; Beech-Nut and Heinz, both line extended, trail far behind.
- **Extensions companies keep making:** Ivory shampoo, Life Savers gum, Bic pantyhose, Chanel for men, Tanqueray vodka, Coors water, Heinz baby food, "USA Today on TV", Adidas cologne, Pierre Cardin wine, Levi's shoes. Executives at Colgate-Palmolive, Campbell Soup, Del Monte and Ultra Slim-Fast each publicly committed to extending their core names into new categories.
- **Why management believes in it anyway:** it wins in the short term (law 11), and management is blinded by intense loyalty to the company or brand. How else did PepsiCo launch Crystal Pepsi after Pepsi Light and Pepsi AM failed?
- **More is less; less is more.** The more products, markets and alliances, the less money a company makes; "Full-speed ahead in all directions" leads to oblivion. To succeed, narrow the focus to build a position. IBM used to stand for mainframe computers; "Today it stands for everything, which means it stands for nothing." Sears was big in hard goods, moved into soft goods and fashion, and even hired Cheryl Tiegs.
- **Strategy as a tent.** The conventional view says strategy is an all-encompassing vision, a tent big enough for every product today and tomorrow. IBM's tent covers the whole computer field and will be blown away as new companies and ideas invade a fast-growing market. GM's says if it runs on the road, or off it, we'll chase it. Be selective about where you pitch the tent.
- **Extension is the easy way out.** A new brand needs money and an idea: it should be first in a new category (law 1) or the alternative to the leader (law 9). Companies that wait for a market to develop find both positions taken and fall back on line extension. "The antidote for line extension is corporate courage, a commodity in short supply."

## 13. The Law of Sacrifice

"You have to give up something in order to get something."

The law of sacrifice is the opposite of the law of line extension. There are three things to sacrifice: **product line, target market, and constant change.**

### Product line

- "The full line is a luxury for a loser." To succeed, reduce the line. Emery Air Freight shipped anything (small, large, overnight, delayed); Federal Express concentrated on small packages overnight and grew far bigger. The sacrifice put *overnight* in the mind.
- **Then FedEx did what Emery did.** It bought Flying Tiger for $880 million and became a worldwide air cargo company without a worldwide position, losing $1.1 billion internationally in 21 months. It faced the classic dilemma (take a domestic name international or create a new worldwide name, and how to handle DHL, which got there first). Walking away from *overnight* was bad; failing to replace it with a new idea was worse. "Marketing is a game of mental warfare."
- **Eveready and Duracell.** The leader called each new technology by its own name: the Eveready heavy-duty battery, the Eveready alkaline battery. P.R. Mallory made alkaline batteries only, with a better name, Duracell, and the sacrifice put "long-lasting battery" in the mind ("lasts twice as long as Eveready"). Eveready renamed its alkaline the Energizer, too late; Duracell was already the leader.
- **The generalist is weak.** If diversification worked, the big generalists would be riding high; most are in trouble. Kraft has 9 percent of jellies and jams to Smucker's 35, and 18 percent of mayonnaise to Hellmann's 42. Kraft means everything; Smucker's has to be jam because that's all it makes. Kraft's one leading brand isn't called Kraft: Philadelphia cream cheese, 70 percent.
- **Department stores sell everything, a recipe for disaster:** Campeau, L.J. Hooker, Gimbels, Ames, Hills and Macy's all went bankrupt. Interstate Department Stores, bankrupt too, focused on the one product it made money on, toys, renamed itself Toys "R" Us, and now does 20 percent of U.S. retail toy business, earning $326 million on $5.5 billion. The formula, **a narrow focus with in-depth stock**, is copied by Staples and Blockbuster Video. Retail's big successes are specialists: The Limited (upscale clothing for working women), The Gap (casual clothing for the young at heart), Benetton (wool and cotton clothing for young swingers), Victoria's Secret, Foot Locker, Banana Republic. "We live in the age of the specialist."

### Target market

- In the late fifties Coke outsold Pepsi more than five to one. In the early sixties Pepsi sacrificed everything except the teenage market and exploited it by hiring its icons (Michael Jackson, Lionel Richie, Don Johnson). Within a generation it was only 10 percent behind in U.S. cola sales, and ahead in supermarkets. Then, by Advertising Age's account, it "has outgrown the Pepsi generation," pitched itself to the masses with "Gotta have it" and older stars, and its agency regretted having focused too much on youth instead of casting a wider net. There is an almost religious belief that the wider net catches more customers. Budweiser's plan has to cover everybody over 21.
- **Marlboro.** Old cigarette ads showed a man and a woman, to add women to a mostly male market. Philip Morris narrowed to men, then to a man's man, the cowboy, and Marlboro became the largest-selling cigarette in the world, and in the United States among both men and women.
- **"The target is not the market."** The apparent target of your marketing is not the same as the people who buy. Pepsi targeted teenagers and the 50-year-old who wants to think he's 29 drank it. Marlboro targets the cowboy and the market is everybody.

### Constant change

- Nothing says you must change your strategy at every budget review. Follow the market's twists and turns and you end up off the road. "The best way to maintain a consistent position is not to change it in the first place."
- **People Express** started as the no-frills airline flying to no-frills cities at no-frills prices; passengers didn't care where they went if it was cheap enough. After success it tried to be all things to all people: 747s, Chicago, Denver and Europe routes, Frontier Airlines, first-class sections. It lost altitude and escaped bankruptcy only by selling itself to Texas Air.
- **White Castle** has never changed its position. It looks as it did 60 years ago and sells the same "frozen sliders" at unbelievably low prices, and the average unit does more than $1 million a year, more than Burger King and not far behind McDonald's.
- "Good things come to those who sacrifice."
