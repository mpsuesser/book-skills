# The aesthetics of agency

This file covers the aesthetic qualities of acting, deciding and solving, as experienced by the player. Use it to name what makes a play experience beautiful or deliberately ugly, to explain why players seek difficulty, or to answer the claim that instrumental play cannot be aesthetic.

## Meaning vs. doing

Most defenses of games praise their *meaning*: what they represent, the stories they tell, what they comment on. Flanagan praises "serious games" as conceptual art. Brenda Romero's *Train* has players efficiently ship yellow pieces by rail until they realize they are carrying prisoners to concentration camps. Frasca and Bogost praise games as simulations and procedural rhetoric, and Tavinor praises them as interactive fiction. These are fine as *one* way games matter. But "we can find, in games, an aesthetic value unrelated to meaning. It is in the aesthetic qualities of doing—in the aesthetics of agency." The neglect comes from "anxiety about the value of goal-oriented play." Assimilating a new medium into an older one hides its potential: early art photographers blurred their pictures with soft focus to make them look like paintings.

Games are "the crystallization of practicality," following Dewey's view of the arts as crystallizations of ordinary experience. Aesthetic experiences of action occur in the wild: fixing an engine, finding the solution to a logic problem that "hits me like a bolt out of the blue," swerving perfectly around a car. But they are "far too rare." Games "refine those pleasures, concentrate them, and present us with novel aspects of them." Chess crystallizes the intellect, and a bouldering route setter arranges holds to induce one subtle, refined motion. These qualities are the player's, not only the spectator's. The philosophy of sport praises the grace of athletes from the stands. The call here is "not for an aesthetics of skilled performance, but for an aesthetics of agency": qualities that "arise in the act of analyzing, deciding, seeing, responding, and doing." Climbing's best moments are "movement *as the solution to a problem*," and dancing freely lacks this.

## Three harmonies

- **Harmony of solution**: the fit between the obstacle and its solution, such as an elegant chess move escaping a trap. It makes no reference to the actor, and spectators and players both see it.
- **Harmony of action**: "the fit between the obstacle and yourself as the originator of those solutions." Examples are timing a *Super Mario Brothers* jump just so, or sliding your hips just enough to balance on a tiny nubbin of rock. Players have "better and deeper access" to it. Its **social versions** include you and a teammate spotting the same bounce pass between two opponents, and coordinating the specialized roles (sniper, medic, tank) in *DoTA* or *Team Fortress 2*.
- **Harmony of capacity**: "harmony between one's *overall capacities* and the demands of the practical environment," a fit "between one's maximum skill level and the demands of the task. It is only available when you are pushed to your limit." *Super Hexagon* has two controls (rotate clockwise, rotate counterclockwise) and accelerates. You must split your brain between the near walls, the far walls and the maze's repeating patterns until "your abilities, worked at their maximum, just barely making it."

**Test for which harmony is present.** When warming up on easy climbs, Nguyen is at his most elegant (solution and action), well within his abilities. At his limit his movements are "clutching and desperate," uglier to an observer, but that is where the harmony of capacity is fullest. Spectators barely see this harmony: watch Michael Phelps break a record and then watch a routine workout, and without a timer you cannot tell which is which. It is especially hidden in mental games like Chess.

**Why seek difficulty.** Striving players "aren't doing it in order to have done something difficult; they are doing something difficult for the experience of harmony between their utmost capacities and the practical world." This differs from Juul's compensatory account (pleasure after the fact makes up for the risk) and from Kretchmar's dramatic arc (repeated failure makes the final victory dramatic). Neither explains the climber's **flash**: sending a hard problem on the first try, where there is no arc of failure. The harmony of capacity explains it.

**Why games deliver it.** The harmony of action turns up in daily life (packing a suitcase perfectly, placing an axe just so). The harmony of capacity is rare there, because the world's tasks misfit us. They are boring (folding laundry), difficult and unpleasant (proofreading for the seventh time), forced on us in mind-numbing volume (grading), or beyond us (curing cancer, the politics of climate change). In games the obstacles can be engineered to fit, by the designer, by finding the right game or opponent, or "even just fiddling with the difficulty level": "we design, fiddle, and pick until the struggle is tailored just right." This is the "existential balm": "problems can be right-sized for our capacities; our in-game selves can be right-sized for the problems."

## Negative aesthetics

Games, like tragedy and horror, use dissonance. Some are designed around deliberately awkward control:
- *QWOP*: Q and W drive the rag doll's thighs and O and P its calves. Running for over two seconds is a significant victory.
- *Octodad: Dadliest Catch*: each limb is mapped to a trigger or thumbstick. In co-op, each player controls separate limbs.
- *Minotaur in a China Shop*: a lumbering, inertia-heavy minotaur fetches orders and grows angrier as he breaks his own stock, "a vicious downward spiral of clumsiness and rage."

These are "the gaming equivalent of horror movies... eloquent and crystallized portraits of ineffectualness. They are horrors of practical incapacity." Their expressiveness (of "having one's heart's desires and one's actual abilities fail to fit") comes from engaging in the act, not from contemplating the design. The negative experience is "a constitutive part of what we aesthetically value," not merely a setup for later triumph: "you don't need to win at *Octodad: Dadliest Catch* to have a worthwhile experience."

## Can absorbed, instrumental play be aesthetic?

**The experiential requirement holds for games.** Aesthetic judgment demands first-hand experience. You judge Van Gogh's *Irises* by seeing it, not by expert testimony. You trust your mechanic about your engine, but you must play a game to judge it, and game reviews work "like movie reviews and fiction reviews, and not like *Consumer Reports* surveys of dishwasher reliability."

**Three stances.** Aesthetic experience of games can come from three stances:
- **Play stance**: directly involved in playing.
- **Spectator stance**: surveying a session without playing in it.
- **Design stance**: attending to the design itself and "how that design supports different instances of play." This is the stance from which Knizia's *Modern Art* is elegant. A handful of rules, under which an artist's resale value is set only by how often their work has been traded, produces a wild market of booms and busts that suits many different groups.

Only the play stance seems to conflict with disinterest.

**The disinterest worry** (Kantian: aesthetic experience must be disinterested). Mumford concludes that only the non-partisan "spectating purist" sees sporting beauty. The answer comes from layering. Zoomed in, play is interested. Zoomed out, the enduring agent plays for the experience. "Aesthetic striving play is *disinterested interestedness.* It is a disinterested attitude taken toward the interested states of an activity." Put another way, striving play is "*impractical practicality*." The same bracketing lets a partisan fan take up an interest in their team winning for the sake of the experience of partisanship.

**The attention worry** (Stolnitz: practical interest filters perception; you notice a kitchen knife's sharpness, not the ring it makes when tapped). Dor shows that experienced players filter out graphics and music, which is why Chess and *Starcraft* use simple, repetitive visuals. The answer again is layering. The inner layer attends narrowly, and the outer layer attends to the inner layer's activity in an unfiltered way. "The aesthetic experience is a reflective experience, had by the outer layer, which takes as its object the inner layer's practical activity... a contemplation of *what it is like* to engage in that narrowed, practical mindset." On a hard climb, Nguyen ignores the green of the rock and the smell of the granite but can savor "that lovely feeling of having the world disappear." The champion boulderer Shauna Coxsey, training for a World Cup, makes her second-order attention practical too: she notices what is useful for improving. Layers of motivation bring layers of attention.

**Aesthetic striving play** is play for the aesthetic quality of the struggle. **Aesthetic striving games** are games "designed primarily for the purpose of providing aesthetic experiences of practicality to their players." The two categories can come apart: a game built for achievement can be played aesthetically, and an achievement player can be led into aesthetic experience by a game's design. *Sign* is the paradigm case. Nguyen's house rule that nobody ever reveals their inner truth, so nobody learns whether they communicated, improves the game. That "would be absurd if we actually cared about winning in any enduring way."
