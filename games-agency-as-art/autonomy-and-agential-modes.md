# Games, autonomy and agential modes

This file holds the book's argument that structured games enrich autonomy rather than erode it. Use it when someone claims rules and goals make games subservient (Legos over Chess, sandbox over structure, free play over games), when the question is what games teach, or when play threatens to narrow a life.

## The suspicion and the reply

The suspicion: playing means adopting "an alien agency designed by another." Sicart says play is "free and appropriative," carnivalesque and disruptive. "Games are just a formal manifestation of play," and designers should create "a setting rather than a system, a stage rather than a world, a model rather than a puzzle." Social art shares the allergy: Kwon faults *Culture in Action* for "prescriptive and overdetermined situations." Behind both lies the **rules-free view of autonomy**: the fewer the rules, the greater the autonomy. It is a purely negative view.

Nguyen's thesis is "the restrictions and specifications of the agential medium offer a special path to enriching our long-term freedom and autonomy. The prescriptions of games are necessary for games to *communicate* modes of agency." "Playing games isn't an intrusion of autonomy from the outside, any more than reading is an intrusion of thoughts from the outside." "Free play won't help build that library." What builds it is the communicative practice of making and playing structured games. Free play stays essential to a human life. Structured games make their own distinct contribution.

Three preliminary points about restriction:
- **Self-binding expresses autonomy** (Elster). A smoker who wants to quit pays the nearby liquor store owner to refuse him cigarettes, so that his long-term will governs his recalcitrant short-term will.
- **Restrictions constitute options.** Add walls, a door and a roof to an empty field, and movement is restricted, but new and richer options appear: inside or outside, sheltered or exposed. "Making a basket" exists only inside basketball's rules. Refusing all rules shrinks the option space.
- The larger claim is that games shape the whole development of autonomy.

## Agential modes

An **agential mode** is "a focused way of being an agent. To enter an agential mode is to focus on a particular set of goals and on a particular set of abilities as the method for achieving those goals." A house can be made energy-efficient through carpentry skills or through the money to hire others, and made beautiful through a painter's sensitivities: three modes. Cognitively limited beings approach the world one mode at a time. One project needs a sequence of modes: in writing, research, creative, rigorous, communicative, then "nit-picky proofreading." Doing right often means switching modes, as when office hours with a distressed student call for moving from philosophy-teacher mode to a therapeutic one.

"Games are the formalization of agential modes. They are crystallized and framed agential modes." Seeing the shift sharply in games makes the softer shifting in the rest of life visible. Games "plunge the player into it, exposing the player to that form of agency from the inside," much as narratives do for emotions in Nussbaum's account.

## The library of agencies

Each game inscribes a mode. The book's own catalog:

| Game | Agential mode it inscribes |
|---|---|
| Chess | thinking ahead to possible opponent responses; careful calculation of precise countersequences |
| *Spyfall* | cooperation when unsure who to cooperate with; "a frame around the practice of bullshit and bullshit detection" |
| *Pandemic* | shared goal, different abilities; subsuming your specialty to a collective enterprise |
| *Monopoly* | entirely self-oriented, "narcissistically bent toward the destruction of others" |
| *Risk* | self-oriented but forming temporary alliances |
| *Imperial*, *1830* | manipulating others' actions by manipulating their incentives; how profit-driven corporations reason |
| *Space Quest* (point-and-click) | hunting for tiny missed details; repurposing objects to solve new problems |
| *Sign* | generating and stabilizing a language under extreme communicative limits |
| Rock climbing | anything in the world becomes a point to lever yourself around |
| Go | "Chess is like one fight; Go is like ten fights all going on at once"; weighing each fight against the whole and abandoning it for a higher-value move |

*Spyfall* in detail: seven players share a location card (say, the Opera House), and one is the spy. The team must signal that they know the location without tipping off the spy ("How are you enjoying this aria?" gives it away), while the spy bluffs and probes. Its narrowness would be obsessive in normal life, but "games permit us to become absorbed in a single facet of practical life." It is like Rothko's exploration of color, "but in agency rather than color." Once internalized, its mode can be worn at a university committee meeting to catch deception. Familiarity with destructive agencies, like *Monopoly*'s, helps a good-hearted agent understand bad actors.

## How modes support autonomy

- **Options view** (Sripada: freedom depends on the size of an agent's *constructed* option set). Knowing both an empathetic mode and a Machiavellian one gives internal degrees of freedom. Going to a funding meeting, you can plan to use a diplomatic, aggressive or bargaining mode.
- **Coherentist view** (autonomy is the genuine self's desires governing action). The young Nguyen wanted analytic philosophy but was weak-willed: his attention wandered and he hunted for no objections. Chess gave him a mode "extraordinarily interested in winning through careful calculation and predicting precise countersequences," which he could deploy in graduate seminars. "Taking on an agential mode can transform a merely instrumental interest into a direct interest, for a time." He washed out as a musician because he could not take a direct interest in drilling scales, and the best athletes and musicians love practice for its own sake. Schmidtz makes the same point: a being with only a bare interest in survival survives badly, so it is better to have "a rich hierarchy of related ends." Weakness of will is failing "to *temporarily inhabit an agential mode with an appropriate set of interests, attentions, and focuses*." "Agential modes are tools for exerting willpower by acquiring momentary focuses, which exclude whole ranges of reasons."
- **Responsiveness-to-reasons view.** The worry is that narrow modes make us miss reasons: Wolf requires responsiveness to a *broad* set of reasons, and an administrator who responds only to economic reasons is not autonomous. The reply is "Each frame digs narrowly, but deeply. So an appropriate succession of narrowed attentional frames can, in the long run, dig more." Finite beings must cycle through modes: "We cognitively limited beings need to become something like Swiss Army knives of agencies."

**What autonomy needs**: a broad *inventory* of modes, the *fluidity* to move between them, and the *accuracy* to pick the right one, which together make "appropriate inventory management." One goal can need several modes. Research takes a fussy, slow, delicate, ornery mode, much like Chess. Teaching takes a sympathetic, light-footed one, close to *Sign*. University politics takes a paranoid, conspiratorial, manipulative one, like *Spyfall*, *Imperial* or *1830*. The philosophy mode carried into a casual chat with friends makes you "something of a jerk." "Game playing is a way to practice agential fluidity."

Modes leak usefully into life. A good Go player stays emotionally detached from each fight and will abandon it for a higher-value move elsewhere, while a bad player is obsessed with a single fight. That detachment carries over into stepping back to weigh the comparative value of whatever you are currently pursuing.

The "life lessons" genre (basketball teaches teamwork, Chess teaches thinking ahead) sits one level below this claim. Each life lesson comes from an agential mode built from a game's rules and goals, and range across many games enhances self-determination. Games offer "shaped, precise, compressed, crystallized experiences of alternate agencies," quicker than a career or hobby, though likely less rich. The effect is gradual and comes over a lifetime of varied play, never from one game. "You can misuse games, just as you can misuse Jane Austen."

**The answer to Sicart.** The benefit comes specifically from structured games, not free play, though this is no case against free play. Structured games are "vessels for transmitting agential modes." They "harness the inventiveness of great numbers of people" to "codify, transmit, and store highly crystallized modes of agency." An individualist picture of autonomy is untenable: learning from others involves "temporarily submitting ourselves to them, in a controlled and consensual way," as in an art class.

## Fluidity through temporary rigidity

"Rigidity in the short term is sometimes crucial for flexibility in the long term," because new states of mind are delicate and hard to reach directly.
- **Meditation**: rigid exercises such as staring at candles and counting breaths. The Zen "Hello" exercise has monks shout a hearty greeting at anyone they meet. Nguyen adopted it for months in his department and on hikes, and it transformed his experience of the social world.
- **Climbing drill**: novices crank with their arms and never rotate their hips. Climbing many easy routes "without ever bending your elbows" forces them to discover hip movement until it becomes intuitive. "The temporary restrictions create short sessions of intense practical focus, which result in a long-term increase in your freedom of movement."
- **Yoga**: "Strictness is a technique for surmounting one's natural impulses and learned routines," a way to "break you out of the trap of your own nature."

"Left to our own devices, we are often creatures of habit." Too much negative freedom becomes "a repetition of one's own ruts and habits." Absolute refusal of anyone else's rules is "a symptom of profound distrust": sometimes we must "trust other people to mold a little bit of ourselves—for a little while." Games give easier entry to new modes than life does "precisely because they are thinner, narrower, and more precisely specified." In games, "one accepts direction from the outside about *the kind of agent to be*." **"Games are agency yoga."**

## When games corrode autonomy

"Game playing can be corrosive to autonomy when it reduces our exposure to a wide variety of agential modes." The extreme case is a single game with a non-exportable mode that crowds out jobs, social situations and other agencies. Addictive games offer "extremely simple agencies and repetitive exercises of these agencies." Schüll documents the slot-machine **ludic loop** (a continuous stream of gentle challenges and in-game rewards at the right pacing) and **"the machine zone,"** "in which awareness of the world, time, and the self falls away into nothing." These techniques now drive games like *Candy Crush*. Narrow exposure, modes that cripple when exported, and malicious or oppressive actors using games as they use literature are all real risks.
