# Games: Agency as Art (C. Thi Nguyen)

Nguyen's thesis: **games are the art of agency.** A game tells us to take up a particular goal, designates abilities for us to use, and packages them with obstacles crafted to fit. "Goals, ability, and environment: these are the means by which game designers practice their art." Players experience that art by flexing their own agency to fit. The skill applies across every kind of game the book treats (sports, board games, card games, party games, video games, tabletop and live-action role-playing games) and across the life outside games that the book compares them with: work, metrics, institutions, learning.

## The core system

These terms are the book's spine. Every branch below uses them.

**Suitsian game.** Bernard Suits's portable definition: "Playing a game is the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles." The full version:
- **Pre-lusory goal**: the state of affairs aimed at, described without reference to means (the ball goes through the hoop).
- **Constitutive rules**: rules that prohibit more efficient means in favor of less efficient ones (dribble; opponents may block).
- **Lusory goal**: achieving the pre-lusory goal within the permitted means ("making baskets"). The rules obstruct the pre-lusory goal but *constitute* the lusory goal.
- **Lusory attitude**: adopting the goal and rules "for the sake of the activity they make possible."

The contrast is **technical activity**: pursuing a valued end as efficiently as possible. The test is the **three climbers**. The Medical Climber, after a rare herb at the summit, would take a helicopter if one appeared, so they are not playing a game. The Professional Climber (after the record and its fame) and the Amateur Climber (after the joy of climbing) both play, because only climbing within the constraints gets them what they want. "Winning" here loosely means pursuing the lusory goal, since some games have only loss conditions (keeping a ping-pong volley going). When the bare word *game* appears here, it means a Suitsian game. Nguyen sets aside games without struggle, such as make-believe and narrative role-playing like *Fiasco*.

**Goal vs. purpose.** The *goal* is the target aimed at during the game (cross the line first, score points). The *purpose* is the reason for playing at all: fun, exercise, de-stressing, skill, beauty. Goal and purpose can be identical (the professional poker player), linked straightforwardly (winning for prize money), or **skew**. In Charades, the player who wins by making everyone miserable "may have succeeded in achieving the goals of the game, but I have failed entirely in my purpose."

**Achievement play vs. striving play.** *Achievement play* is playing for the sake of winning: goal and purpose are aligned. *Striving play* is pursuing the win for the sake of the struggle: goal and purpose are skew. Striving play is a **motivational inversion** of ordinary life: "we pursue the ends for the sake of the means." Its justification looks *backward*. A game goal is justified "by showing what kind of activity they will inspire," not by its own value or by what follows from it.

A second distinction cuts across the first: *intrinsic* vs. *extrinsic*, which concerns the **type** of value. Achievement vs. striving concerns its **location**, in the win or in the struggle.

| | Achievement (value in winning) | Striving (value in the struggle) |
|---|---|---|
| **Intrinsic** | winning for its own sake; purely competitive players | the value of the struggle itself |
| **Extrinsic** | what winning brings: money, glory | what the struggle brings: fitness, mental health, "brain training" |

The categories mix. A common error is to assume all striving play is intrinsic. Playing for fitness or skill is striving play, because "winning is entirely unnecessary."

**Disposable end**: "an end that one takes up voluntarily and that one can rid oneself of without doing significant damage to one's enduring value system or core practical identity." Every player treats the pre-lusory goal disposably: green tokens matter for two hours, then go into the Ziploc bag. For the striving player, *the interest in winning itself* is disposable. A disposable end is a genuine end, not a pretended one, because "they ground reasoning; they guide action; and for a period of time, they occupy the forefront of the agent's mental awareness."

**Submersion and layering.** To be absorbed, the striving player must make the disposable end "temporarily take the phenomenal position of being something like a final end." The player sets up an **inner agent**, which cares about winning, inside an **outer agent**, which holds the enduring purpose. "The outer agent sets up the inner agent... The inner agent tries to win. The outer agent has no reason to help the inner agent, and may manipulate their overall ability to win, in order to get the right degree of struggle." The outer layer justifies the inner one, but that justification is not active during play. It runs in the background and can **cancel** the game ("Wait. Are we really having any fun here?"). A player who keeps the purpose in view loses absorption. The ten-year-old who slipped his losing father extra *Monopoly* cash, so as to keep beating him forever, "is missing something crucial."

**The medium of agency.** The designer's artistic medium is "the goals, the rules, and the environment that these various parts animate into a system of constraints." The environment here is the **practical environment**: "the environment conceived of in its opposition to our goals and abilities." Designers create new actions (the *Portal* gun, "taking a piece") as well as restrictions. An obstacle is only an obstacle relative to a goal: "*a goal is partially constitutive of an obstacle as such*." Cookies obstruct a dieter but help someone at sumo camp, and a crevasse is one thing to a climber and another to a landscape painter. So setting the goal shapes the whole environment. "The rules of the game tell us to care about something and we start caring about it." Reiner Knizia calls the scoring system his central tool: "The scoring system creates the motivation." Because the designer controls "both world and agent," games can be "a way of *writing down* forms of agency." Painting records sights, music records sounds, stories record narratives, and games record agencies. Together they form "a vast library of agency."

**Value clarity: balm and threat.** In games, values are "singular and shared," and "each person is a simplified agent." That is "an existential balm" against the plurality of ordinary values, but it carries a danger: exporting "a false expectation: that values should be clear, well-delineated, and uniform." "Games threaten us with a fantasy of moral clarity." Game ends can be tailored "precisely because they are games." Gamifying life means tailoring enduring ends, and "we court disaster."

## Applying the lens

Run these steps on any case: a game, a player, a designed system, or a slice of life that feels game-like.

1. **Find the game structure.** Name the pre-lusory goal, the constitutive rules and the lusory goal. Apply the helicopter test: would the person take the efficient route if one appeared? If so, the activity is technical, not a game. Done when you can state what the obstacles are and what they make possible.
2. **Separate goal from purpose, and place the player in the four-way table.** Look for evidence of striving: holding back from skill gains that would end close matches, retiring a game once one player wins too easily, picking games for being "interesting" or "beautiful," preferring a fascinating loss to "a dull win." Done when you can say where the value sits for each player involved.
3. **Read the design as sculpted agency.** What goal does it tell players to care about? Which abilities does it grant or deny? What obstacles fit them? What agential mode does that inscribe? *Imperial* shows the stakes: it uses the same moves as *Risk*, but its goal is total cash, not victory in war, so players lose wars on purpose and stage wars between countries they both control. Done when you can say what the game is a *frame around*.
4. **Check the transition in and out.** Is submersion succeeding, and can the player dispose of the end afterward? Match the case to a failure mode below. Done when the failure, or its absence, is named.
5. **Follow the branch** to the reference file that the situation reaches, and use its tests.

## Failure modes in play

- **The diffident player** cannot bring themselves to care: "What's the point? It's just a game." They never submerge, so the game never grips them.
- **Stuck in the game agency** ("excessively competitive"): the player takes up the goal of winning and cannot put it down, so losses hurt terribly and wins bring great pride. A **sore loser** is "anybody who fails to dispose of their gaming ends when it would be appropriate to do so."
- **The transparently instrumental striver** keeps their purpose in view and so should rationally throw the game to prolong the struggle. They are left with "a perpetually anxious secondary consciousness, worrying not only about losing, but also about winning."
- **The achievement player in a striving context.** The dinner-party host who knows the party game, crushes the awkward guests, and leaves nobody having fun has "failed in my purpose, and the fact that I won the game should be something of an embarrassment."
- **Failing to cancel.** Submersion should be fragile. If the house catches fire or a player who has just been dumped starts crying, enduring values should break through. To keep playing single-mindedly "is to be something of an asshole."
- **Exporting the game's attitudes.** The all-consuming instrumental attitude and the expectation of value clarity can leak into life, which leads to *value capture* (see `value-capture.md`).
