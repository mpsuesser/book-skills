# Games as social transformation

This file holds the book's account of multiplayer games as designed social structures, and of how game design can turn competition into cooperation. Use it to design or analyze relations between players, to answer whether competition is zero-sum or wrong, to test magic-circle claims, or to compare games with social art.

## The medium of sociality

Designers create "specific relationships of interdependence, vulnerability, and antagonism" and so work in the **medium of sociality**. Games are "the original social art." A focus on single-player games misses the designed oppositions, alliances and dependencies.

- **Nested mediums.** In Werewolf (Mafia), a referee secretly taps the werewolves. The villagers outnumber them but are ignorant. By day everyone talks and votes to lynch someone. By night the werewolves murder. Werewolves misdirect and feign uncertainty. "Werewolf uses the medium of agency to work in the medium of sociality." The experience is one of social uncertainty, "a war between sowers of informational chaos and detectives."
- **Roles.** Team roles in *Team Fortress* and *Defense of the Ancients* (sniper, engineer turrets, tank, medic) push specific forms of cooperation.
- **Cooperative games**, such as *Pandemic*, *Lord of the Rings* and *Arkham Horror*. In *Hanabi*, 25 cards in five ranks and five colors must be built into rows from 1 to 5. Players hold their cards facing away, give one hint per turn, and deduce from what others *didn't* say. It draws on Karen Jones's distinction between **reliable** (like a clock) and **trustworthy** (actively taking into account that others rely on you, and *anticipating* their needs). *Hanabi* is "a frame put around our active trustworthiness," a "heightened, concentrated experience of epistemic dependency." *Space Alert* (a damaged space station, a simultaneous timed programming phase, screaming) crystallizes coordination in an emergency, while *Hanabi* gives unlimited time for memory and deduction. *Space Alert* is "very, very funny."
- **Loose vs. tight structure.** Some games leave cooperation unstructured (*Minecraft*, online RPGs). Others restrict it tightly, creating "an interlocking network of the players' abilities, needs, and blind spots."

## Competition transformed into cooperation

Nguyen and his spouse cook dinner together and then thwart each other at a board game: "locally speaking, we are competing, in a more global sense, we are actually cooperating." "For striving players, it's only the temporary in-game agencies that are competing. Their full agencies are cooperating in creating a struggle." So the zero-sum objection fails for striving players. Professionals in chess or boxing, and achievement players generally, may be thoroughly competitive and genuinely zero-sum.

Games are **morally transformative technologies**: they take a normally negatively valenced action, such as brutal competition, and turn it into something good.

**Why consent isn't enough.** Weimer's contract view treats opponents as "biological gym equipment" and is binary and unfinicky. Two counterexamples: humiliating cocky novices who consented, and proposing *Diplomacy* to a spouse who despises lying, knowing she won't back down. Both are wrong despite consent.

**The transformation requires "players *actually attaining the kind of activity they value*."** Check three conditions:
1. **Matching skill levels.**
2. **Psychological fit** between player and game. *Starcraft 2*'s speed is unpleasant for Nguyen, and his spouse despises lying games.
3. **Game design**. The transformation is "importantly *technological*." Dribbling around a guard, a chess fork and a *Team Fortress* grenade are transformed. An insult contest run until one player cries, or a whipping contest, is not.

Responsibility is *distributed*. Striving motivation makes the transformation possible, and design and player fit actualize it, so "game designers are thereby partially morally implicated."

Against Simon's view that competition is cooperation in developing excellences, which requires keeping that cooperation in mind: keeping it in mind is "training," not playing. Striving players instead "*psychically offload* our interest in cooperation to the game itself and to the gaming environment." They trust the externalities and can indulge their aggression, the "predatory delights of competition," morally. The responsibility moves to choosing the right game and context.

## The magic circle and the voluntarism critique

The **magic circle** comes from Huizinga. Salen and Zimmerman's *Rules of Play* gives it three features:
1. a bounded space formally separated from ordinary life;
2. precisely defined in space and time;
3. crossing it brings an alternate world with new rule authority and new meanings.

Radical impermeability has been criticized by Malaby (gambling, social status), T. L. Taylor (*World of Warcraft* forums) and Consalvo (gold farming). Zimmerman later repudiated that reading. Stenros (a negotiated social contract) and Waern (a frame of resignification) are both **voluntarist**: on their views, transformation happens by the players' mental act. Nguyen holds that the agency of transformation is distributed across players, game design and community structure: "it's not just the player, it's the game."

**The controlled-variable test.** A belligerent colleague joins pick-up basketball at lunch. The rules channel his aggression even though he intends to humiliate. His trash talk still wounds, because it can't be resignified by will, but his shot-blocking is transformed: "not an internal resignification, but an external transformation of action." Dodgeball, where bullies hurl balls and unpopular kids dread it, transforms nothing. This is a "natural version of the philosopher's controlled-variable thought experiment": the intentions stay the same and the design varies. Striving motivation is necessary but not sufficient, and design and community are crucial. "Multiplayer games are social technologies," and their social engineering "arises from regulated, structural aspects of game design."

## Designing a society out of agencies

*Root: A Game of Woodland Might and Right* (Cole Wehrle) descends from the COIN series (*Fire in the Lake*). It is radically asymmetric:
- **Marquise de Cats**: a fat-cat industrialist that builds infrastructure and troops, defends the status quo and scores steady points.
- **Eyrie**: brittle, rule-bound, warlike aristocrat birds. It is a programming game: the Eyrie must execute its whole plan every turn and cannot remove orders, and failure brings turmoil, lost points and a restart. It simulates regime instability.
- **Woodland Alliance**: underground critters who spread sympathy, stage mini-revolutions, and have warriors who melt into the woods.

The Cats and the Eyrie form an uneasy alliance against the Alliance while jockeying with each other, and the Alliance foments conflict. "They have created a society, using the building blocks of agencies." Playing Root from the status quo and then from the insurgency shows the same sociality from different agential angles.

## Games as social art

Social art includes Paul Ramirez Jonas's *Key to the City* and Claire Bishop's antagonistic social art, such as Tania Bruguera's *Tatlin's Whisper #5*, where mounted police herd the audience. Nguyen offers a mock gallery review of a relational artwork that creates intricate partnerships and silent signaling, then reveals: "What I've described is actually the game of contract bridge." Bourriaud wants art to be "learning to inhabit the world in a better way": "Art is the place that produces a specific sociability," building micro-utopias against cities that mechanize relations. Multiplayer games have done this for ages. Basketball loosely suggests a sociality (zone vs. man-to-man defense emerge), while *Hanabi* specifies one with tremendous precision.

A **social work** is a work in which social relationships are part of the medium. In social art the audience acts as themselves. Games force specific relationships through designated agencies, "not our selves at least, not our habitual and usual selves." That intrusiveness gives greater artistic control while leaving space for player agency. The values exported differ. Relational works export friendliness and dinner parties. Games export the "values of epistemic and practical communities": *Hanabi* carries over to the science lab and academic publishing.

The result is a **library of socialities**. Mill's "experiments in living" (communes) explore alternative ways of life, and games are their "quickest rough-sketch version." Interacting under a disposable end is a way of exploring social life under an alternative conception of the good, from the inside, as narratives let us explore emotions.
