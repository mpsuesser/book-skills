# Framed agency and agential distance

This file holds the book's account of games as works with prescriptions, and of how designers control an experience that the player generates. Use it for whether games are art, what counts as properly encountering or reviewing a game, how a design can predict experiences that pass through player freedom, and who deserves credit for an elegant play.

## The prescriptive frame

Games share something with traditional artworks: a **prescriptive frame**. "In order to experience the artwork, you have to follow certain prescriptions about how you will confront it." You view a painting from the front. To experience a game, you follow its rules and aim at its goals. Artworks and games both stand with "one foot in the material and one foot in a social practice." Skip the definition of art itself. Nguyen favors a cluster theory, under which games plausibly qualify because they are made for aesthetic experience, but the real question is value. The working category is the **work**: "any sort of intentionally authored, stable object, made for some specified form of appreciation and consumption" (newspapers and history books included).

- **Prescriptive ontology** (Davies): prescriptions help constitute the work. *Moby Dick*'s words read in random order are not *Moby Dick*. Prescriptions carry only contingent authority: *if* you want to read *Moby Dick*, read the words in order, just as waltz rules bind only if you want to waltz. "Both artworks and games are social practices with constitutive rules."
- **Frames say what to attend to and what to ignore** (Saito). Perfume is to be smelled, not tasted, so you don't pour it on a steak. A novel prescribes ignoring line breaks, while a poetry collection prescribes attending to them. Saito puts games (the baseball crowd) in frameless "everyday aesthetics." Nguyen holds that authored games, video and board games, are framed works.
- **Detect a frame by its failures.** It is a failure to review *Grand Theft Auto* by reading its code, to review a board game from its box art, or to play *Elder Scrolls: Oblivion* by using the character generator and then running in circles for twenty hours. "You can't review that game based just on how the box looks."
- **Against the charge of intentional fallacy.** Leino rejects the *ludic imperative* (play in the intended spirit and adopt the goals) as the intentional fallacy. The reply, following Irvin: the prescriptive ontology fixes what the work *is*, "what counts as a minimally adequate encounter," not its meaning or interpretation. The prescriptions are simple and public, carried by social practice (bookstore vs. museum glass) or by explicit statement.
- **Material basis vs. the work itself.** Speedrunning *Super Mario* (time instead of points, glitches allowed) is an alternate mode of encounter with the materials, "a different game—just like Chess variants." It is not bad, but don't review *Super Mario World* by speedrunning it. A forensic analyst examining the back of *Irises* meets the material, not the work, just as a sample of my blood cells is not meeting me.

## Communicative stability

Prescriptions give **communicative stability**: they guide many people's attention along similar lines. Bridge's restriction on speaking yields deduction and communication under adverse conditions. Poker's hidden cards make you deduce from behavior. Mario's rightward push yields precision platforming. Free climbing's rule against pulling on gear forces you to adapt to the rock. In a gym, routes are color-coded, so a novice who uses every hold is engaged in free play but "failing to experience the work," while the route setter can rely on the rules to sculpt "epiphanies of motion."

"Communication involves shared norms. The more I reject shared norms, the more freely I can play, but the less I can receive communication. And the more I wish to communicate, the more I must bind myself, for the moment, to a set of shared norms."

"Games are a technology of communication," "a language, of sorts, for communicating modes of agency." *Spyfall* frames bullshit creation and detection, *Sign* frames inventing a means of communication, and *Super Hexagon* frames "semidefocused reflex challenges." "Games are frames hung around aspects of the player's own activity. In short: games are framed agency."

## The General Prescription and the agential center of gravity

**General Prescription for Aesthetic Striving Games**: "The appreciator will play the game, following the indicated rules and aiming at the indicated goal, and then appreciate the resulting activity."

The prescription is loose but predictable. The goal conditions what the inner layer attends to: a climber attends to ripples and balance, a *Spyfall* player to verbal cues. Aesthetic attention is not prescribed but *called* by the activity, much as a novel's frame is brute and its content draws the eye. "The self-reflecting agent's gaze should naturally be drawn to the *agential center of gravity*," the place where agency is most active. The designer's toolset therefore runs: rules, goals and environment → the General Prescription → abilities and obstacles that concentrate the player on one part of agency (tactical reasoning, reflexes, social manipulation) → designed "densities of agency" that draw the aesthetic gaze. Designers relinquish complete control.

## Three kinds of game that add prescriptions

Some games add prescriptions about how many times to play and in what spirit. Diagnose which kind a game is before judging it.

- **Party games**: "long-term development of skill is unimportant or actively discouraged," and victory is arbitrary (the *Cards Against Humanity* judge). Studying Charades tips from forums would be absurd. In *B.U.T.T.O.N.* (Brutally Unfair Tactics Totally OK Now) players put their controllers down and take five paces back, and whoever's X button is pressed loses. Wilson on *WarioWare: Smooth Moves*: manic micro-games shift focus "towards the human beings performing and willfully making fools of themselves." The genre foregrounds "arbitrariness, skill-lessness, and intentional chaos."
  - **Party games vs. stupid games.** In party games, skill-less encounters are prescribed. In stupid games you must try to win, and the best part is failing. *Cards Against Humanity* is a party game but not a stupid game, because success (a funny joke) is cherished. Finger jousting is a stupid game but not a party game, because skill makes the failures more elaborate and funnier. (Nguyen finds *Cards Against Humanity* "truly abysmal" and prefers *Dixit*, *Why Did the Chicken...?* and *Funemployment*.)
- **Heavy strategy games**: "core features of the work *only become visible and coherent after repeat playings, and after the acquisition of significant skill*." *1830: Railways & Robber Barons* (Francis Tresham) combines stock manipulation with company management: you can loot a company and dump it on another player. Setting a *par value* in the first round cannot be done intelligently on a first play. *Dream Quest*, a rogue-like deck-builder, takes hundreds of plays before the early card synergies make sense. The prescription is that "*a player must play multiple times to adequately encounter the work*." The normative sting is that reviewers who play each game a handful of times "have never actually adequately encountered any of the games they review."
  - **Deep vs. minimally adequate encounter.** One reading of *Jane Eyre* suffices for a minimally adequate encounter. Chess, Go, Bridge, *1830* and *Dream Quest* need more. A Go teacher advises against reading texts on *influence* until the first ten levels. Dor's **strategic perception** (seeing a rook as lines of movement) is helped by consistent visual design.
  - **Infer prescriptions by best explanation** of the game's evident features, the way you tell a novel from a story collection on a shelf, not only from the context of display.
- **Community evolution games**: *Magic: The Gathering*, *Android: Netrunner*, *Hearthstone*. Deckbuilding is "rock-paper-scissors with a doctoral degree," and the **meta** is the "complex interplay between the game community and the game itself." Fantasy Flight released new cards monthly and rotated them out after two years, and its designers monitored the meta and added mechanics to restore deck diversity. The metagame is part of the work when design features are best explained by their effect on the community's strategic discourse. The prescription is *participation in the larger community of players*: two players at home have not fully encountered the work.

## Agential distance

Games are works, but unlike traditional art they open a gap between the stable artifact and the object of appreciation. **Agential distance** is the gap between the designer and the aesthetic effects. The designer shapes activity "through the active, and often creative, agency of the player." The special tool is control over the participating agent: "game designers use the medium of agency to overcome agential distance." The full slogan unpacked: "games are the art of agency because games use agential manipulation to create aesthetic experiences of agency across an agential distance."

**Object arts vs. process arts.** Traditional artworks are object-centered. Process arts are "made to call forth an activity in an audience, for the sake of the audience's appreciation of the aesthetic qualities of their own activity." Examples are social dances (group tango, square dancing), fondues and hot pots, cooking, and perhaps urban planning. Cookbook reviewers fixate on the dish rather than the process of browning onions before adding wine.

**Artist's work vs. attentive focus.** The *artist's work* is the stable artifact the artist creates. The *attentive focus* is the prescribed object of the audience's attention. In painting and dance they coincide. In aesthetic striving games they come apart: the artist's work is the rules, pieces, software and environment, and the attentive focus is each player's experience of their own activity. "An aesthetic striving game is successful, not when the game itself is aesthetically good, but when it inspires and shapes aesthetically valuable striving in the player." A dance audience looks outward at the same thing, while players each appreciate something different (my Mario run vs. yours). The design stance is secondary.

**Why Lopes's interactivity doesn't fit.** Lopes: "a work of art is interactive just in case it prescribes that the actions of its users help generate its display." On this view the work is a stable *algorithm*, and interaction is like walking around a statue. That fits a narrative game whose choices all lead to the same ending, explored and reflected on as a structure. It does not fit sports or *Team Fortress* (you just play), *Zen Bound* (the truest experience is the first play, and repeats are "only shadows"), or *Guitar Hero* and *Dance Dance Revolution* (there is no possibility space, so you evaluate skill). And some agential works aren't interactive at all, yet their attentive object varies anyway: logic puzzles, *Where's Waldo*, rock climbs, crosswords.

**The participatory art taxonomy.**
- **Participatory art**: the user is prescribed to substantially participate in order to experience the work. Examples are striving games, Rirkrit Tiravanija's gallery kitchen serving curries, Lopesian computer art, EDM concerts and tango *milongas*.
  - **Nongenerative**: the attentive focus exists prior to and independently of the participation (the Lopesian algorithm; a rock concert where you dance to appreciate the music).
  - **Generative**: the user generates the attentive focus.
    - **Manufacturing**: you create a distinct artifact to appreciate (assembling a smoked-salmon bagel platter).
    - **Process**: you appreciate your own activity. In *Jenga*, the point is not the tower but the desperate efforts. "The process arts involve essentially self-reflective modes of appreciation."
- **Agential art** (agential process generative participatory art) meets four conditions:
  1. the audience is prescribed to participate in order to appreciate;
  2. the participation generates the attentive object;
  3. the attentive object includes features of the audience's process of participation;
  4. the prescribed participation includes pursuing a goal. (Tiravanija's kitchen lacks 4.)

**Recalcitrance.** Wollheim: each medium has a characteristic **recalcitrance**, difficulties "that can be dealt with only in the actual working with them." Oils are slow, and watercolor is fluid. For Thomson-Jones, digital cinema's recalcitrance is an overload of possibility. The recalcitrance of the medium of agency is agential distance, the player's practical activity interposing between the artist's work and the attentive object. It is not merely player freedom: single-solution puzzles and exact-timing reflex games interpose skill rather than choice, oneself "as a reacting mechanism."

## How designers bridge the distance

One option is to surrender control, as social art and relational aesthetics do. Many designers instead exert control, making games reliably difficult, intense, calming, funny, gleeful, wild or chaotic. *B.U.T.T.O.N.* is manic hilarity, and *1830* is "thrilling, brutal, calculative intensity," in the 18xx community's words "a knife fight in a phone booth, but with spreadsheets."

- **The path of the puzzle**: a narrow range of solutions yields uniform experience. Climbs on tiny holds force slow, delicate, precise movement and a calm mental state. Older adventure games (*Zork*, *Space Quest*, *Myst*) used choke points. This works only in nonoppositional games. In oppositional games, obstacles are generated on the fly, and with simulated systems and AI enemies, outcomes emerge through feedback loops. Sid Meier: "players want to make interesting decisions with real consequences." Designers keep in mind the option space, the possible solutions, and how early solutions condition later ones.
- **Scale the distance to the genre.** *Super Hexagon*'s distance is narrow, because players use similar skill sets. *1830*'s is vast: track layout, the math on each stock, and manipulating other players' holdings to induce conflicts. *Magic* designs for three player profiles (Mark Rosewater): "Timmy" wants to experience something, a feeling; "Johnny" wants to express something about himself through originality in deck design; "Spike" wants to prove something through innovation or skill. Community evolution games have "nested layers of emergence": in-game choices, designed decks, the meta.
- **Reliable characters show the distance can be bridged**: *Half-Life* is pulse-pounding, *Civilization* absorbing and addictive, *WarioWare* hysterically funny, *Candy Crush* destroys the sense of time and self, *Super Mario Kart* is gleeful and slightly nasty, *Diplomacy* thrilling, gut-wrenching and alienating, and *Spyfall* amusingly labyrinthine. In *The King Is Dead*, players hold identical decks of eight action cards and act eight times with used cards face up. Reactions are uniform: "take turns sucker-punching"; Nguyen's spouse said "I almost puked. Twice" and then wanted to replay.
- **Sculpt the in-game agent.** The primary tool is "design of the in-game agencies themselves": create an option space and goals, and they condition the types of action. *Android: Netrunner* is asymmetric with "laser-like specificity." The corporation cannot initiate an attack, keeps its cards secret, wins if given time, and can kill the hacker. The hacker is the only initiator, plays its cards open, must attack, and cannot kill the corporation. So the corporation's agency is bluffing, deceit and misdirection, and the hacker's is managing resources to gather information, deducing and gambling. The medium both poses recalcitrance and offers tools, the way watercolor's fluidity does.
- **Comedies of agential fluidity.** *B.U.T.T.O.N.* and *WarioWare* use a "wrenched and chaotic agency": they snatch an agency up in an instant and throw it away. They are "comedies of agential fluidity" and comedies of medium self-awareness, like *Tristram Shandy*.

**Kin to architects and governments.** Others also negotiate this kind of distance: landscape design, urban planning, government, traffic flow, behavioral economics, UI design. Christopher Alexander places public spaces between private spaces and utility spaces to create unplanned encounters, adds a longer alternate path for privacy, and notes that loops circulate better than lines. "Games... are the artistic kin of governments, architecture, and urban design—at least as much as they are the kin of fiction, rhetoric, and conceptual art." The difference is that liberal governments must respect citizens' own ends and can only offer instrumental leverage on "sludgy" full agencies. "Designers of governments, urban environments, and roads largely work in the medium of *constraints for agency*, where game designers work powerfully and directly in the medium of *agency itself*." Illiberal governments that design citizens' agency lack games' "morally excusing factor of voluntariness and disposability."

## Aesthetic responsibility: who made the elegance?

There is no uniform answer. In an improv dance competition the dancers are responsible, and in a single-solution logic puzzle the creator is. In *Portal*, a player facing a trench may shoot one portal on the trench floor and one on the wall behind, fall and be flung out over it, an unexpected solution. Its elegance is elegance "*as a basketball pass*," a game action constituted partly by the rules (a pass is elegant against thickets of opponents). This is **split intentionality**: the player intentionally solves the puzzle but does not aim at elegance, which is a "byproduct of my absorption," while the designer created conditions that encourage elegance. Responsibility is "complexly distributed," often a collaboration in which the designer achieves effects "*through the agency of the player*." The artist's work is not co-created. The attentive object often is.
