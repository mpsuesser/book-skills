# Deep stories, narrative time and entropy

The depth behind deep stories and narrative rationality in `SKILL.md`. Read it when the situation is a transformative enactment: college, a marriage, a PhD, a product launch, a startup, a career change, a recovery, an artistic work, a campaign. Read it also when the question is how a life, or an organization's history, is unfolding.

"To enact a deep story you must remain aware of the tempo of different phases, manage the emotions they evoke, and time various shifts in optimal ways."

## Finding the deep story

Look for it three ways:
- between bouts of extended existential musing;
- around acts of significant creative destruction;
- easiest of all, by asking questions about motivation "until you get vacuous answers."

A deep story is the explanatory frame of last resort for intentional behavior. A student rushing out at 8:30 AM: "To make my 9:00 AM Mandarin class." "I like this one, I might major in linguistics." "I like to travel... especially to Asia." "You need a degree for the well-paid international careers, and the good opportunities are now in China." You have hit the boundary of the *College* story. Beyond it the answers turn banal and doctrinal ("money is important") and lose the telling specifics (*China, Mandarin*). This matches Toyota's "Five Whys". The boundary exists because the next expected passage (graduation) or unexpected one (paralysis after a car accident) can wipe the meaning-of-life slate clean. "Adopting intentions that apply to a future, transformed you, makes no more sense than micromanaging the future adult life of your child."

Significance depends on your role. To the priest who has conducted hundreds, a wedding is a routine enactment. To the bride or groom it is a deep story, bookended by first meeting and post-honeymoon reality shock, with its climax at the ceremony. A deep story builds a *sui generis* mental model for one significant new situation. You must "continuously improvise the background story while acting", which is learning in the most general sense. So situation awareness stays very poor for a long stretch, and that is the disorientation of early learning. Insignificant stories (a shopping trip) have the same rise and fall, muted, with a small peak at checkout and no liminal passages.

## The Double Freytag phases in depth

The simple Freytag triangle (Exposition, Rising Action, Climax, Falling Action, Resolution) is the rise-fall of fairy tales, from "Once upon a time…" through a climax to "…happily ever after", with something changed. Adding Campbell's monomyth gives two peaks with a valley between them: the **Double Freytag triangle**. The second peak (the separation event) stands higher than the first (the cheap trick), and the closing liminal passage ends above the level where the opening one began. Read its vertical axis as entropy, the disorder of the developing mental model. It has seven epochs and six transitions, two of them marked by special events (the cheap trick and the separation event).

**Liminal passages** (stillness). These are the bookends and the only necessary feature: a deep story must begin and end with one. Doctrine and self-archetype occupy center stage, and the only thing you can do is existential musing, pleasurable or painful, self-aware or self-indulgent. People avoid it by getting drunk, or get trapped in it. "Narcissism ... is falling in love with yourself at a particular liminal passage to such an extent that you are unwilling to submit yourself to further transformative experiences." Macbeth's "To-morrow, and to-morrow" soliloquy, spoken right after Lady Macbeth's death, is liminal musing.

**Exploration** (volatile, dissipative). "I'll know when I find it." Learning from scratch is "unsupervised, uncertain, unbounded, unstructured, and mostly unrewarding": no teachers, safety belts, syllabi, grades or prizes. Unknown-unknowns dominate, the "blooming buzzing confusion." The natural behaviors (play, hide and watch, poke with a stick) are random by necessity. To a child this is stimulating variety; to an impatient adult it looks like inefficient collection of irrelevant information. Exploration increases both the size and the entropy of the model.

**The cheap trick** (crescendo). The mind's demand for relief arrests the rise of dissonance and turns it around. You recognize an *exploitable pattern* in the raw material, which draws a **relevance boundary**: what conforms is included and the rest excluded, so the chaos "separates into exploitable meaning and ignorable noise." It is also called the Aha! moment and Clausewitz's *coup d'œil* ("strike of the eye"). It answers the deep story's driving question, "What's going on, and what should I do?" Every such insight is flawed because it excludes part of reality as noise, so it "merely buys you a certain amount of time." If the wrong answer is *elegant* it gives leverage. Reaping the rewards before unintended consequences arrive means you have "earned real rewards from fake truths, and fled with your ill-gotten gains before nature takes her revenge."
- **Timing.** Seek relief too early and the trick buys too little time and too little leverage; Knuth's "premature optimization is the root of all evil" names that failure. Wait too long and you meet "death by perfectionism." The moment is created by "the battle between your impatience and your ability to endure dissonance." Managing that battle influences the timing but cannot control it. "There is no known systematic method for triggering a cheap trick." Failing means falling victim to expediency or to perfectionism.
- **Steals and bargains.** "In narrative rationality, the cheap trick is a more fundamental mental event than goal setting." Where a calculative decision-maker weighs fair costs and values of goals, "the narrative-rational decision-maker looks for steals and bargains." Napoleon racked up apparently impossible victories by picking only battles where he could find a *coup d'œil*.
- **The first cheap trick** is the infant's boundary between "I" and outside, the seed of the self-archetype. It is extraordinarily hard to redraw later; doing so usually takes hallucinogens, intense stress, sensory deprivation or meditative rigor.

**Sense-making** (decrescendo with emotional relief). The cheap trick is an *organizing* insight, "a speck of dust around which a compact mental model can crystalize." Hunting jigsaw pieces, you sort them uselessly by green and blue until a piece with a fish's eye reveals that green is fish and blue is water. Look for "an organizing insight that dissolves the complexity." *Compelling* means comprehensive, organizing as much of what you know as possible. *Elegant* means the model is as compact as possible: local and unique to the situation, unlike general Jomini-style models (Porter's five forces) that "lack the leverage-providing core cheap trick." Entropy falls. "Elegant and compelling, however, do not add up to real": until the model meets new realities it "is indistinguishable from a delusion." ("All models are wrong, some models are useful.") A model is useful if it gets you through the valley.

**The valley** (steady, with slowing momentum and increasing depression). The cheap trick's organizing power yields diminishing returns, so the enactment takes more and more raw energy. You are sustained only by the belief that you are cheating nature on a grander scale. "The characteristic feature of the valley is decisive action without either reward or validation. Your enactment is charging ahead in the dark." Building the prototype time machine before you turn it on is a valley. So is the waiting period between adopting an intention and the first irreversible act, Brutus's "Between the acting of a dreadful thing / And the first motion, all the interim is / Like a phantasma, or a hideous dream." The valley is the longest and hardest phase and the least described; films skip it with a montage. Godin's Dip and Gartner's trough of disillusionment resemble it but plot different things. A deep story graphs entropy in a mental model over time.

**The heavy lift** (a high-effort, low-coherence increase in momentum). You exit the valley by "a massive effort of the will": the all-nighter, powering through doubt to launch.
- It is not just deadline pressure. "A heavy lift is *always* required in a deep story, whether or not there is a deadline. In fact, an arbitrary deadline of no real-world consequence is often imposed to force the heavy lift, and the *do or die* character of the effort is what makes it a *dead*line. In any consequential situation, the decision to start a heavy lift is deliberate, not forced."
- Why it comes: without reinforcement the new model starts to disintegrate and a steady tempo becomes unsustainable, so you spend "all remaining reserves of emotional resilience and energy in one final burst of fierce effort resulting in a forced encounter with reality."
- Timing follows temperament, and optimality depends on your estimate of the cheap trick's organizing capacity. Overestimate it and you stay in the valley too long (perfectionism). Emerge too early and you have not extracted enough value.
- Entropy rises during the lift, because exhaustion breeds expedient decisions: "books are never finished, they are merely abandoned." The result is doubt and anxiety in the last hours.

**The separation event** (crescendo). This is "the moment when a significant proportion of the newly created mental model, along with its momentum, is externalized into the environment, as your act of creative destruction": the moment of truth that validates or invalidates the leap of faith. It often differs from the calculative "objective". In a PhD the objective is the degree but the separation event is the defense. In a company the objective is repaying the investment but the separation event is the product launch. In *College* it is the first test of your new identity, such as sending a résumé and transcript to a recruiter. It is a birth, bringing relief and true sadness, since you can no longer protect what you made. Beyond it you can only take part in the construction of shared mental models, through an external dialectic.

**The retrospective** (decrescendo and a mix of joy and sorrow). You try to return to the starting state with as little subjective change as possible. Doctrine is revised to carry the story's morals, and the memory is cast into a final form that validates the revised doctrine: "the most valuable doctrine we can craft, not necessarily the most truthful one." Retrospectives can produce delusion as easily as wisdom; Aesop's fox remembered "the grapes are sour" so he could keep believing in his own cleverness.
- It is not a calculative debrief; it can last a lifetime, as it does for war veterans (Walt Kowalski in *Gran Torino*).
- On success (a passed defense, a great opening weekend) the cheap trick is validated and extended to the new realities it has weathered.
- On failure you face an existential crisis: search for new meaning in the ashes, deny and isolate, or stay in high entropy mourning. "We cannot easily love again what we have just attempted to destroy and recreate." A failed *College* sends graduates backpacking on another continent to find themselves.
- Even after success a full return is rare. The creator knows the compromises left by the exhaustion-timed heavy lift and "the tragedy of expedience inherent in the cheap trick."
- Whether the reconstructed memory is delusion or critical history "depends entirely on your capacity for honest introspection." Then the next liminal passage begins, and tempo slows to a near-standstill.

## Narrative time and the Freytag staircase

Clock time is oscillatory, and event time treats time as a container shaped by its contents. Narrative time drops the container and keeps the contents: time as *material change*, unidirectional with entropy (the thermodynamic arrow lines up with the psychological one), and bumpy. "Every sort of narrative time is a *specific* pattern of change, with a characteristic texture of periodicity and eventfulness." The clock you choose matters: the Nile flood, the moon, your breath, cesium atoms. "There are decades where nothing happens; and there are weeks where decades happen."

**Freytag staircase**: life as a string of deep stories, each liminal passage on average a little higher in entropy than the last; the rise across one deep story, from its opening to its closing liminal passage, is "The moral of the story." Its rise and fall is the **fundamental tempo**, "a narrative clock for an individual life": rhythmic, aperiodic and entropic. Picture riding it like a roller coaster with your eyes closed, where time is only how you feel. Personal doctrine is "a gradually growing set of irreducible axiomatic beliefs." Introspection compacts it like trash compaction, and the larger it grows the more rigid you become (*doctrinaire* means rigid). "Even the belief that one must be open-minded is doctrinal, and adopting the belief makes you less capable of living by it." Zen no-mind is unstable; doctrine regrows even in a monastery.

**Sketching rules**: "any unbroken graph drawn between a minimum-entropy floor (babyhood) and a maximum-entropy ceiling (death) is a valid Freytag staircase." It can be smooth or jagged, quick or slow, and can nearly kiss the ceiling and touch the floor (metaphoric death and resurrection). "Every peak is either the cheap trick or separation event of a deep story, or the climax of a simpler enactment. Every trough is either a liminal passage or valley within a deep story, or a context switch." Readings:
- a very smooth line is lifelong health, "the slowest possible rate of dying";
- many small waves is a high-tempo life, more active and less philosophical than a few big swings;
- a floor-to-ceiling rebirth between a jagged before and a smooth after is a mystic or prophet.

Represent a significant decision as a fork in the staircase. Both branches wind to the same entropic death, which offers "a sense of proportion and pensive comfort."

**Personal narrative time in practice.** Asked to collaborate when you can't commit, the calculative answer is "I am overbooked right now, but I'd like to talk next year, maybe in January." The narrative one is "I am in the valley of a deep story right now, how about I connect when I get to my next liminal passage?" Rao admits it sounds theatrical, but "it helps to *think* in terms of narrative time." Narrative times coordinate poorly across people ("when the cows come home" against "when the Nile floods"). Railroad time zones solved that problem at the cost of homogenizing local time.

Apply Double Freytag and the staircase "with taste and discretion." They are partial models of prototypical patterns that illuminate dissonance and entropy. As templates for storytelling they become formulaic, and literary **particularism** holds that every story must be understood on its own terms.

## Mortality, open worlds and entropy

Calculative rationality handles risk, learning and information with probabilities, discounting and automated learning, all resting on the fault line "the future is like the past." Such models amplify intuition, which is necessary but not sufficient. **Closed-world** models are those in which "predefined things exist, and only events that have been modeled can happen." The **open world** contains unknown unknowns and black swans, and it requires periods of high entropy while genuinely new information is taken in. Humans can compute with "clear as mud, but covers the ground" models. "In open worlds, understanding is a live, ongoing battle to lower the entropy of the information in active mental models."

Calculative decision-makers (perfectly logical, bounded-rational, predictably irrational) are immortal and ageless. Narrative-rational ones are mortal, with "a fixed capacity for absorbing open-world information and battling entropy, before they succumb": **entropic aging**. You can die accidentally (unknown-unknowns) or by accumulated entropy. **Thermodynamic theology** in folk form: 1. You cannot win. 2. You cannot break even. 3. You cannot quit the game. Zeroth law: *you must play the game* ("win-win or no deal" does not apply to life). Kauffman's fourth law: *the game keeps getting more complicated, and there are always more different ways to play.*

## Skill: Tetris thinking

Tetris is a closed world that models open-world risk management well: "dealing with increasing entropy and consciously choosing your path to death." Play it. Compact filled rows sink away, and poorly packed rows with holes persist. Over short periods the stack rises and falls, but over long ones it inevitably rises, leaving less room to maneuver. "If your current decision is poor, your next decision becomes harder, since bad decisions raise the stack height, leaving you with fewer options and less time to make the next decision." When the stack hits the ceiling you die, and its height over time traces a Freytag staircase. Use it to judge a choice by the stack it leaves behind: the options and time it preserves for the next decision.
