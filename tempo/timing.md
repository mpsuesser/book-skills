# Timing: tempo awareness, situation awareness, pace and interval logic

The depth behind the four timing behaviors in `SKILL.md`: worked examples, the thirteen interval relations, and the practice skills Rao gives for building a sense of timing. Timing skills "aren't directly teachable": like comic timing, it takes immersion, osmosis, apprenticeship and practice, and "your appetite for increasing risk will determine the extent to which you can rise above craft and get to art." What can be taught is the language and the exercises below.

## Tempo doodling (the foundation skill)

The skill is detecting changes of tempo in your environment and becoming sensitive to how they affect your own emotions and energy. Small cues count: the refrigerator compressor kicking in, a sitcom cutting to an excited commercial, restless shifting when the beverage cart comes out, a listless table perking up when someone walks in.

- **Doodle alphabet**: circles graded on two axes. Size grows as events get "Stronger": bigger is more energetic. Shading runs from solid black to empty as they get "More positive": empty circles are positive-emotion events, shaded circles negative ones. Closer together is faster. Doodle in cafés and meetings, one symbol per conversational turn. An irate customer at a service desk getting steadily angrier becomes a zigzag between two strands: one stays small and pale, the other grows larger and darker until its last circle is big and black ("Can you tell which dots represent the customer?"). If growing excitement needs symbols to overlap, let them. Then try something subtler, such as a boring conversation that starts politely and turns tired and slow. "Aim for art, not precision", and name the good ones ("Angry Dad yelling at his daughter").
- **Watch two things at once**: the external tempo, and the tempo inside your head as it responds.
- **Body as tempo barometer**: hold different levels of bodily tension, from complete relaxation to spikes of frozen alertness. Hold your reactions to tempo changes a little longer than they would normally last.
- **Recalibrate**: awareness calms you and mutes your reactions, and "An instrument that systematically under-reacts is no good." So deliberately overreact for ten minutes, jumpy and on edge, to feel it.
- **Consume art** made through more sensitive eyes, such as Walter Ruttmann's 1923 film *Berlin: The Symphony of a Great City*.

The scan-to-task tempo shift is the model case. A sleepy restaurant is swamped by a tour bus while the executive chef is out, and the staff panics. Ten minutes in, the chef returns and barks orders (water for pasta, you on vegetables, you to the store for potatoes). "You look around and rapidly assign every open resource you see to an open problem." The group's clock ticks faster, confusion becomes action, and that moment becomes the key beat of the story anyone tells later.

## Situation awareness: reading backgrounds

When you context-switch you notice the *what, when, who* and *where*, which loads the right mental model, but the main work is attuning to rhythms. The kitchen shows how rhythms nest by timescale:
- years: appliances come and go;
- months: rice, margarine and detergent run out;
- weeks: juice and bread;
- days: food rots, the trash fills up, the fridge is restocked;
- hours of cooking: a moving wave of clutter from cabinet to countertop to sink to dish rack;
- seconds: knives drumming, soups boiling to crescendo.

Moments of pure timing artistry sit inside this harmony, like the pancake flip: too hasty and it sticks, too late and it dries out or burns. You change on the same scales, as habits, recipes, diets and skills come and go. Read emotion and energy as well. Is the sink usually cluttered or empty? Does the trash go out on time? Is the room calm, distracted or depressed, alert or lethargic? You absorb all this subconsciously in a few seconds, and that is the context switch.

Workplace backgrounds work the same way:
- the drumbeat of quarterly reporting and annual planning;
- deep, slow rhythms beaten out by the upper hierarchy through large communication meetings that synchronize the organization;
- a frontline heartbeat as short as a day (Scrum's daily stand-up, sunrise and sunset meetings).

Probe the subtler rhythms too. Do senior managers process email on Sunday night? Who are the larks and the owls? Has mobile work-life blending created a fragmented arrhythmia? Free agents are attuned to a corner of the economy instead: city activity, mailing lists, release schedules, and the seasonal rhythms of trade shows, conferences and product launches. "Becoming a free agent means becoming attuned to the tempo of a particular corner of the economy." After a few weeks anywhere, you can answer all these questions accurately.

## Pace-setting worked examples

- **The kitchen jolt.** Coming home energized to a grimy kitchen with an overflowing sink, Rao nearly gave in to its buzz-killing tempo and ordered takeout. Instead he "attacked the sink with unnecessary ferocity" and finished in ten minutes. The minor lesson: the unpleasantness of a task misleads you into overestimating its size. The deeper one: the ferocity was the pace-setting. "I needed to get the kitchen clean *and* preserve my own buzz." His standing routine is to move clean dishes from dishwasher to cabinet and dirty ones from sink to dishwasher before cooking, a jolt of energy to the "kitchen cycle". Dishes in the cabinet are the swing at its highest point; dishes in the sink are a swing slowed to a stop.
- **Two email scripts with the same outcome.** In Script 1, A asks for a report at 10:15 Monday and B promises it "by COB" from a conference call. A reminds B Tuesday at 9:02, B says it slipped through the cracks, and the report arrives at 9:13: seven emails, two of them informative, almost 24 hours. In Script 2, A asks at 10:15 and B sends it at 10:19. Processing time was three minutes in Script 1 and four in Script 2, the extra minute being the context switch out of the meeting. Where Script 1 is the norm, B choosing Script 2 is a speed-up: B acts "locally as an accelerator" by resetting expectations, which may cascade. Where Script 2 is the norm (as in go-getter startups), choosing Script 1 is a conscious slow-down, passive-aggressive or calming.
- **Overclocking**: Charles Fine's *Clockspeed* gives each industry natural clocks (the product lifecycle among them). Making a system run faster than its design tempo is one kind of pace-setting.

## Disruption worked examples

- **Oatmeal.** Rao resisted his doctor's advice for months, out of routine inertia and because it looked like "brown sludge", until one morning he accepted his wife's offer "out of sheer boredom." He liked it, and the change rippled outward: lighter lunches, coffee deferred to mid-morning. That extended impact is why plausible "15-minutes-a-day panaceas" are so hard to adopt.
- **Clash of interruption norms.** In a citizen peace forum, Israelis interrupted cheerfully ("No, No…"), comfortable with high overt dissonance. Arabs held to *musayara* ("going together"), long, elaborate, uninterrupted speeches. Interrupted, an Arab speaker would fall silent in surprise, and the Israelis, expecting to be interrupted back, were confused in turn; the meeting was utterly disrupted. The healthy range of conversational tempos runs from leisurely turn-taking with active listening, through impatient sentence-finishing and violent agreement, to violent disagreement. A conversation with no dissonance interests a student of decision-making as much as a piece of music with no dissonance interests a composer.
- **Legitimate disruptors.** In traffic, legitimate disruption is reserved for those with the right talents, reasons and social roles: police cars, fire trucks, ambulances. Drivers read one another through archetypes (*jerk, roadhog, maniac, old lady, cop, teenager*) that predict behavior.

## Interval logic: the thirteen relations

Most decision models use **point logic**: a decision is a point, a fork in a temporal road, which confines reasoning to before/after frames. James Allen's **interval logic** (early 1980s) organizes time around intervals. Gantt charts are a crude early application. Two intervals of different lengths have thirteen qualitatively distinct relations: six symmetric pairs plus one special case.

| Relation | Shape (A *relation* B) | Kitchen | Workplace | Mnemonic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before / After | A ends, a gap, then B begins | Let dough rest before baking | Act after a seeded initiative has incubated | Sow and harvest |
| Meets / Met By | A ends exactly as B begins | Make the salad just before the guests arrive | Pep talk just before a major customer event | Billiard ball cannon shot |
| Overlaps / Overlapped By | A begins first and ends while B is under way | Buy milk before you run out | Air traffic control shift handoff | Passing the torch |
| Starts / Started By | A and B begin together; A ends first | Preheat the oven while you make batter | Initial oversight for a new employee | Booster rocket |
| During / Around | A begins after B and ends before it | Cut the cake after most guests arrive, before they leave | Product demo during the trade-show coffee break | Strike while the iron is hot |
| Ends / Ended By | A begins after B; both end together | Add salt and stir just before turning off the flame | "Bring in the CEO to close the sale" | Finish with a bang |
| Equals | A and B begin and end together | "It'll take two to carry that big cake" | Split a large meeting into break-out teams | "On three: lift" |

The second name in each pair swaps A and B (B *after* A, B *met by* A, and so on).

An **event** is the intersection of a (possibly virtual) venue, an interval of time (possibly spanning time zones), a subset of the world's social network, and an organizational context. Use the relations to lay out the thirteen ways a decision event can sit against another event, and to make informal phrasing precise ("bring that up towards the end of our next meeting"). The *fait accompli* is one such placement: an irreversible commitment made before you reach the default venue.

Worked questions:
- **Weekly meeting**: Monday, when situation awareness is low but energy high? Wednesday, when the opposite holds? Or Friday, when distraction rules but the momentum of Monday to Thursday is on your side?
- **Manager drop-ins**: once a month or once a quarter? As an exceptional review, or just before, during or after the weekly meeting? Each choice has distinct effects.
- **Industrial scheduling** uses six variables: release time (earliest start), actual start, actual end, processing time, due date, and delay (finish minus due date, which sets penalties). Four are points, one is a fundamental interval (processing) and one a derived interval (delay). Recast them as two intervals, the window of opportunity W and the processing time P, and find which of the thirteen relations are feasible.

## Calendar art

See the calendar grid "as a canvas for artistry. Your calendar is not an empty container. It is a landscape of invisible energy and emotion." A good breakfast and an appreciative email make an energized morning, so schedule the tough conversation there. Keep it simple: the point is developing an artistic sense of timing, not optimal calendar management. Use "small dabs of artistry":
- move a meeting here, try a *finished by* tactic there;
- if you are an owl, take a "lark day";
- if you schedule in one-hour blocks, try a week of four-hour blocks (Paul Graham's maker's schedule; managers use one-hour blocks).

Let the dabs that you enjoy grow into a personal calendar aesthetic. To see a calendar's narrative texture, color each event red or blue for emotional content and shade it lightly or darkly for momentum, then step back: it looks like a colorful river, with rapids and gentle stretches.
