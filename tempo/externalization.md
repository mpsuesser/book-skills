# Externalized mental models, fields and flows, organizations, time cultures

The depth behind externalized mental models in `SKILL.md`. Read it when the task involves an environment or a group: organizing a desk, kitchen or files; designing a venue, meeting room, process or signage; reading or reshaping an organization; collaborating; or understanding a culture's sense of time. Rao's aim here is "a sensibility, not ... an idea": the *clockless clock* sensibility. To avoid "the very high-modernist disease we are attempting to cure," stay with rich examples rather than codifying the ideas into a process.

## Codification, embedding, internalization

From the perspective of timing, mental models, narratives and universal tactics, "everything is a mental model." Artificial realities are *externalized* mental models. "We externalize mental models because they are burdensome." Dennett's sea squirt swims about until it finds a rock to cling to, then eats its now-unneeded brain. A desk encodes beliefs, desires, intentions, possible worlds and even deep stories. An austere Bauhaus High-Modernist desk expresses a doctrine of clinical efficiency; desk toys and cartoons express a relaxed one. In cognitive psychology this is *distributed cognition*.

Externalization happens in two steps:
- **Codification**: "expressing part of the meaning of a mental model in a form that can be manifested in material terms." Intentions get put into words for a to-do list; beliefs that calm is good and that plants relax you become a picture of the future desk.
- **Embedding**: "creating physical consequences that endure." The list lasts a week, the potted plant years. A spoken sentence embeds in a listener's memory, and that is why a boxer's punch sequences are procedural knowledge: the coach's knowledge externalized into the student. One architect's vision can be codified and embedded as engineering plans, as a speech, and as the finished city. Codification without embedding stays in the head as a possible world. In the weather analogy, codification is cloud formation and embedding is precipitation.

We read nature without teleology, but artificial realities in terms of agency and design. So we **internalize** the mental models of whoever externalized them. A to-do list reading *Call cat-sitter to confirm, Get oil change, Set Out-of-Office message, Check-in online, Pack, Remember to get gift for niece* tells you someone is taking a trip, and its one inconsistency (oil change *and* online check-in: driving or flying?) gives away still more. Internalization varies with the internalizer. Take a successor who inherits a desk and its potted plant:
- If introspective, he feels calmer and adopts both beliefs.
- If not, he becomes the sea squirt, "enjoying an advantage, but with no idea why".
- If his doctrine says "I need to be alert and have a sense of urgency", he accepts that plants calm but rejects that calm is good.
- A skilled designer or psychologist may internalize a richer model than the one externalized, inferring repressed childhood trauma.

"This process of mental models triggering related mental models in other minds is what we understand as communication in its most general sense." That is why anthropologists treat garbage and pottery shards as "texts." Speech is the hardest case, embedded in the most temporary medium there is, air. It works only through older, slower shared acts, like each of us once having apples pointed out to us.

## Seeing and creating: design and dance

How we create depends on how we see, and vice versa ("when you have a hammer in your hand, everything looks like a nail"). Creation is chemistry between perception and platonic categories (circles, lines, right angles). People who can only see platonic shapes, and savants with photographic realism, both struggle to function as autonomous adults. "Normal human thought [is] a balancing act between sensing realistically, and seeing through simplified platonic abstractions." The more you desire control and comprehension, the more your creations are simplified by your categories. The more comfortable you are with chaos, the more you act as a trusting agent of raw perception.

The two poles are the archetypes **design** and **dance**:
- **Design** is "the detached, objective mode of action" of Zeus and Indra: "causal forces that transform without being themselves transformed." The pyramids are its monument.
- **Dance** is "a participatory mode of action" of Dionysus and Shiva. "The dancers are themselves microcosms of the transformations they bring about."

## Legibility and meaning

"A piece of physical reality is legible if it is obviously the product of coherent human agency, a deliberate externalization of a mental model." Where human and natural order are hard to tease apart, legibility falls. Legibility so strongly suggests agency that it misleads (the "canals" on Mars). Illegibility comes from two sources:
- **Obfuscation**: camouflage, or the stick-figure code of Holmes's *Dancing Men*.
- **Complexity**: an old city is the collective externalization of millions of residents over centuries, so it is "*necessarily* illegible to a single urban planner," who reads what he cannot internalize as chaos.

James Scott's failure model, in Rao's terms, is "overweening impoverished design crushing an ongoing organic dance." Planners poorly internalize a rich emergent model and replace it with an impoverished platonic one: "design destroying dance." Adversarial OODA is the deliberate use of illegibility to cause failure, and rebellions start in the illegible parts of cities, the slums. Legibility runs along a spectrum: a chair (single-mind design) is highly legible, collective emergent orders are intermediate, and a *laissez-faire* economy is nearly as illegible as nature.

**Legible versus meaningful.** Take a mixed pile of papers from several projects. Filing them alphabetically in labeled binders, or stacking them in one unlabeled floor pile per project with each pile in its project's own order, externalizes and simplifies about equally. Binders are harder to search, by label and alphabet. Piles are faster but need a spatial map in your head ("that pile near the window has to do with Project A"). Both are legible, but the piles are *meaningful* only to you: "I am the local, you are the outsider." A Scott-style boss who imposes a company standard, a "Procrustean-bed outsider mental model", may simplify your workspace until you are unproductive. The decor policy might even take away your potted plant.

**Organization**: David Allen's definition is that being organized means "where something is, is related to what it means to you." Sherlock Holmes kept his tobacco in a Persian slipper. "Organization is about the meaning that matters to the person whose mental model is being externalized." People fail when they strive for legibility to an external eye by imposing received meanings:
- shelving a small personal library by the Dewey Decimal system instead of a useful logic like read/unread;
- arranging a kitchen and cooking the way grandma did, then failing to handle the microwave and frozen meals;
- getting organized so the boss will see it, which means focusing on the peripheral (filing, labeling, stacking things geometrically) at the expense of meaning, sometimes counterproductively.

"Apparent creative chaos is fine, so long as it is sufficiently legible and meaningful to *you*." It needs to be legible to others only as far as you must share meaning with them.

## The tempo of an externalization

Two artists' studios can be equally illegible, yet one feels vigorous and passionate and the other "deeply depressing and stalled." "Even when an externalized mental model is *not* particularly legible or meaningful to you as an outside observer, you can still detect the tempo that animates it." You can do this with handwriting in an unfamiliar script, or in a strange old city the day you land. "Tempo cannot be easily hidden, which is why disguising it is usually the hardest aspect of deception": Eichmann was caught in Argentina because an alert agent noticed his characteristically European saunter. The cues form a gestalt. One studio has in-progress paintings, still-wet palettes and framed work labeled for shipping. The other has dust and brushes crusted with dry paint, and "motivated, working artists are never sloppy about maintaining their equipment."

This lets you act before you understand. Tempo gives you situation awareness and momentum management "even in illegible and low-meaning situations": you can walk into someone else's depressing, cluttered kitchen and take control simply by starting to clear up. "We can begin to act decisively even before situations become legible and meaningful to us." Subjective and environmental tempo are practically the same in the human sphere. The four timing behaviors (merging, going with the flow, pace-setting, disruption) are the interaction of internal mental models with externalized ones, and "Mental models react with each other *even* when they are mutually illegible, because the tempos interact."

## Fields and flows

Externalized models lack fine-grained belief-desire-intention elements: a coffee cup maps to no single belief. Their coarser primitives fit literally spatial social realities best (buildings, cities, parties, armies); money and taxes need strong conceptual metaphors.
- **Fields** "are arrangements of the physical environment."
- **Flows** "are the behaviors that result when humans interact with a field. Flows are the observable parts of others' enactments."

A coffee mug externalizes beliefs about hot fluids: tall containers spill less, handles help, a fist-sized container is easy to hold, people drink 8 to 20 oz at a time, lips are easier to sip from. Its flow is *lift, tilt, sip, put down*. Fields vary in what they hold:
- a simple possible world (a gallery's arrangement sets a default viewing order);
- a universe of deep stories (a college campus);
- a lot of momentum (an assembly line), or very little (an empty room).

Fields can also be calculative-rational (a bureaucratic process) or narrative-rational, like Daniel Pink's "emotionally intelligent signage" that accommodates the emotions of an enactment. Flows range from orderly (a queue) to chaotic (a mob egged on by a demagogue). A field's latent momentum acts on a flow's momentum the way a magnet acts on a paper clip: a charging mob holds more than a quiet one, a mall more than a library. A field turns natural behaviors, like randomly oriented paper clips, into order, and so into effective procedural behavior.

An army on parade is an orderly, legible, meaningful flow, an "impoverished expression of its capabilities" meant to show power and signal control. On the battlefield it is illegible to an untrained eye and fully expressed. The environmental field plus the other people in it make a **social field**. "When there is little momentum or meaning in the primary environmental field, the dynamics of the social field start to dominate." Fields designed to catalyze social behavior contain **social objects** (Hugh MacLeod's term), and chairs and tables are the simplest:
- Semi-circular stadium seating is intimate and invites participation.
- Flat horizontal rows facing a raised stage create distance and dampen participation.
- Banquet rounds of six to eight decentralize the room and make it hard to command attention from a podium.
- An awards ceremony run as a banquet has a very different vibe from one run as a spectacle facing a podium.

## Orchestrating field-flow complexes

Systems and processes are the simplest field-flow complexes, "to social reality what calculative rationality is to narrative rationality." A system-process complex is usually "the codified embedding of the mental models of a few centralized decision-makers, working according to highly platonic logic, and driven by a great desire for legibility and meaning from a central, but external, point of view". Such complexes fail because "they prioritize legibility and order over effectiveness." Their aesthetic is Scott's "authoritarian high modernism", and its workplace form is Taylorism. Carefully applied, Taylorism is valuable (interval logic descends from its Gantt charts). It goes wrong outside its bounds, and its bigger problem is ineffectiveness rather than dehumanization. Test it by imagining time-and-motion studies applied to "how can I throw a great dance party?" Restless riders at a bank of elevators keep punching the call button. The Taylorist adds a floor display or a "please do not repeatedly push the button" sign. The artistic fix is a mirror opposite the doors, which turns impatient pacing into a moment of calm self-absorption.

"Successful field-flow complexes can neither be designed nor danced into existence. They must be *orchestrated.* A conductor or choreographer, rather than a pure designer or dancer, is necessary." The choreographer needs a sense of all the internal and embedded mental models in play and of their mutual legibility and meaningfulness. A wedding planner choosing centerpieces ahead of time puts design over dance. A hostess circulating to lift a party's energy puts dance over design. "The weakest interactions are at the level of tempo, while the strongest involve the construction of shared meaning." Getting two economists to waltz takes the right music, enough alcohol and a savvy host. Getting them to write an award-winning paper takes a shared language, complementary doctrines and strengths, and an academic community's fields and flows. Even then they need complementary enactment styles with the right mix of harmony and creative dissonance; different preferred talking speeds or a clash of interruption norms can sink a collaboration.

The environment is an active participant in any group situation. The designers of the chairs, the providers of coffee and cookies and the interior designer all take part *in absentia*. "A negotiation on a golf course is very different from one in a board room." "You can only understand a group with reference to the specific narrative within which it is situated." Rao's practice questions for any setting (a busy restaurant kitchen, a meeting, a courtroom, a wedding, an assembly line, a hostage negotiation, a busy store, Robinson Crusoe on his island):
- What are the relevant mental models in play?
- Are they internalized or externalized?
- What is the level of momentum in the situation?
- What are the levels of mutual legibility and shared meaning?
- Can you tease apart the pure tempo-level interactions from interactions where shared meaning is being constructed?
- Can you identify key fields and flows?

## Shared mental models and organizations

As scale grows, design gives way to emergent dynamics and decentralized orchestration by many. Dance overwhelms design, and at the scale of the global economy replaces it. At the largest scales deliberate design is effectively impossible: "the central reality which must be faced by all organizations." Large organizations are grown, not constructed, and make only partial sense even in hindsight; "Organizations that are grown are fundamentally less legible than those that are constructed." The seed models must be **shared mental models**, mutually legible and harmonized at the level of tempo, sustaining a shared situation awareness. They include organizational self-archetypes and doctrines, and archetypes of other organizations (a creative go-getter facing staid, slow incumbents). The orchestrator's role is limited to catalyzing the right shared models early, "planting the seed of an organizational self-archetype and doctrine that is appropriate to the *raison d'etre* of the organization". After that "the organization dances itself into existence and self-awareness." UI design's "paving the cowpaths" applies the same idea: let people walk the garden for months, then pave the worn paths.

Gareth Morgan's eight metaphors for organizations are machines, brains, organisms, cultures, political systems, psychic prisons, systems of change and flux, and systems of domination. All except the Taylorist machine naturally comprehend growth. Change in a machine is stepwise re-engineering; in the others it is generative growth, ontogeny and self-organization.

Organizations differ from individuals in two ways:
- **They need design-and-dance doctrines** for managing fields and flows in their domains, beyond momentum doctrines. The options include authoritarian high modernism, its antithesis orchestration ("*laissez-faire* low naturalism"), *wabi-sabi*, *feng-shui*, and behavioral economics' "libertarian paternalism".
- **They can overlap and subsume one another**, through true mergers and acquisitions, where individuals manage only a figurative mind meld. The organization-as-individual metaphor only goes so far.

To think about an organization of any scale, from married couple to civilization, reinterpret the individual ideas:
- How do flows stabilize into group procedural tactics, and how does ritualization create organizational inertia?
- Does ritualization make people stupider than their environments?
- Are missions and visions real thinking, or ritualized processes that fail to create shared mental models?
- Are self-archetypes the same as brand narratives?
- What are the organization's deep stories, and what are its cheap trick and its valley?
- How does its Grand Narrative relate to a Freytag staircase?
- "How can you tell whether a ritualized flow, based on externalized mental models of forgotten founders, is obsolete, or effective but illegible?"
- How do fields and flows decay, how do new ones take root, and must organizations die?

## Skill: reading time cultures

This is the abstract analog of tempo doodling, for any organization from a two-person marriage to human civilization, or a pride of lions:
1. Read, observe and participate in the target culture as much as possible; immerse yourself in it.
2. Note its important rhythms, basal energy levels and emotional temper.
3. Experience its core event stream in the language of temporal interval logic.
4. Experience its sense of historicity and entropy, its views on progress and decline as embodied in its codified rituals.
5. Learn how it frames risk, knowledge and the unknown.
6. Sketch the Freytag staircase for the culture as a whole, from its mythological beginnings to its prophesied end. You may find an eternal cyclic narrative instead of a staircase.

Geertz's *Deep Play: Notes on the Balinese Cockfight* is the model. Reading a time culture is "a distinctly political act, since you cannot hope to suspend your native narrative time entirely." Time binds more intimately than space: the world can share an instant as it cannot share a place, and "Rhythms in time bind us more closely than patterns in space." Atomic clocks are precise but not accurate for every purpose: you measure *when the cows come home* better by listening for a moo. Their impoverished, high-legibility design suits satellites and Internet traffic, but elsewhere it smooths local narrative time until it turns bland. Robert Levine's *A Geography of Time* is a *Lonely Planet* for narrative-time tourists. When you land in Hawaii, reset both your wristwatch and your clockless clock to *island time*: "The one will help you catch your flight back home, but the other will allow you to actually arrive at your destination in a narrative sense."
