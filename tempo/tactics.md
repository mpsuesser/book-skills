# Universal tactics, decision patterns, enactment styles, strategy

The depth behind universal tactics, decision patterns and the strategy/tactic definitions in `SKILL.md`. Read it when the task is choosing or naming a move, classifying how someone is deciding, analyzing a style (yours, an opponent's, an organization's), or clearing up a strategy-versus-tactics argument.

## Natural and artificial behaviors

In extremely new situations (infancy, or the exploration phase of a deep story) mental models are weak, and the only reliable core is the doctrine and self-archetype carried over from before. **Natural** behaviors are "rudimentary, hardwired" and evolved for unknown risks and open-ended learning; they bootstrap mental models. **Artificial** behaviors are learned and complex (driving involves around 1,500 subskills) and manage known risks within rich models.

You learn faster by doing than by watching, but doing carries more near-term risk. Environments are "relatively quiescent unless disturbed", so you have to provoke them: the child says "doggie" pointing at a cow and is corrected. When you know too little to act meaningfully, "You must act meaninglessly, or in other words, *randomly.*" Natural exploration is ranked by boldness:
- *hide and watch*: gather information passively while staying out of sight;
- *touch gingerly*;
- "dip a toe in the water";
- "poke with a stick";
- "stir the pot";
- "stir up a hornet's nest";
- *play*, possible only where safety is assured.

"The safer we feel, the more actively and aggressively we explore." Natural exploration is iterative (each action is designed to produce feedback) and anxious, because fight-or-flight is a likely follow-on. The **Yerkes-Dodson law** says learning needs the right arousal. Too little gives boredom and disengagement. Too much sends you into hide-and-watch or fight-or-flight where bolder exploration would have taught more. Natural behavior has an unstable tempo, poised "between the energetic bursting of fight-or-flight and the tense stillness of hide-and-watch." Hide-and-watch favors *complex but legible* environments, like lightly wooded land rather than dense forest or barren desert, where you can see without being seen.

Domain-specific tactical vocabularies are born, mature and die like languages (sword-fighting gave way to shooting). The universal vocabulary underneath persists, which is why chess served officers with cavalry and still serves officers with missiles.

## Conceptual metaphor and universal tactics

"A conceptual metaphor is a systematic structuring of meaning in one domain in terms of our understanding of another domain" (Lakoff and Johnson). "He gathered his thoughts" belongs to THOUGHTS ARE THINGS ("all over the place," "one thought stood out," "weigh the pros and cons"). Conceptual metaphors are "prelinguistic raw material" for meaning and behavior, unlike isolated figures of speech ("a lion on the battlefield"). PEN-AND-PAPER MATH IS MECHANICAL ASSEMBLY: you literally rewrite lines of symbols, but you think in terms of grouping, moving x to the left-hand side, plugging in, and cranking through. Our understanding comes from hands and eyes, and hands do much of the perceiving when visibility is poor (groping in the dark, clearing clutter, shuffling papers). A commander *separating* enemy forces and a mathematician *separating* an expression use the same divide-and-conquer tactic: see an imaginary line, sweep the objects away from it.

Develop an ear for metaphor in action-language:
- **Spatial tactics** (location, extent, orientation, distance, inside, outside, boundary, overlap): moving towards, moving away from, inclusion, exclusion, containment, surrounding, touching, connecting, disconnecting, overlapping. Examples: "His area of authority overlaps mine; we should define clearer boundaries." "Bridging the gap between theory and practice." "Outflank the enemy unit", which means defining a boundary, controlling flow along and outside it, and confining the adversary within it.
- **Material tactics** (mass, friction, compressibility, momentum, inertia, gravity, counting): resistance, yielding, dodging, dragging, attrition, rebounding, steering. Examples: "I dodged the question," "he wore down the witness," "lighten the burden of entitlements," "spread the risk," "bundling different asset classes." Hammer and anvil traps the enemy between a slow infantry anvil with lots of inertia and a fast cavalry hammer.
- **Patterning tactics** (ordering, arrangement; edge detection runs deep in vision). Organizations are rich in them: "next in line for promotion" (a line), "surrounds himself with yes-men" (a ring), the matrix model (an array), "very little room at the top" (a pyramid), rotations and connections (a circle and a web), "toxic silo" (an impervious cylinder), "vertically integrated" (columns against a horizontal market). Battlefield examples include the phalanx and, for texture, the Swiss pike square, a human porcupine against cavalry.
- **Other sources**: fluids ("watertight argument," "the surge"), gases ("expand on that?"), mechanical assembly ("construct a better argument"), and the classification tactics of overwhelming variety (a jigsaw puzzle, a foreign culture).

## The four basic decision patterns

Universal tactics combine into **decision patterns**, "little nuggets of narrative meaning," the sentences of behavior and "individual breaths in decision-making." Each has its own rise and fall and carries the enactment from one stable, meaningful state to another. "If a decision pattern is interrupted, you must either try to go back to the original state or improvise, since the intermediate states, like incomplete sentences, do not add meaning" (the sitcom character who steers a slip into another meaning or turns it into a sneeze). The four classes follow from where the driving information originates (your head or the environment) and whether you consciously recognize its influence.

**Reactive**: "a sequence of universal tactics that are consciously selected from among those suggested by the situation itself," devoted almost entirely to managing time, energy and momentum, and fast. Example: working a party to meet someone. You keep them in view, wait for a lull or a loosening of their group, or a transition (changing groups, going to the bar), then move: "Hello! I've been waiting to meet you." **The test**: an observer can describe it in one word, *following* (or *stalking*). Your behavior is described compactly by reference to your target's, and you inject energy but almost no new information. Reactive patterns rest on mimicry and mirror neurons. The common forms:
- *Selection*: "I am with you" answering "Are you with me or not?"
- *Reflection*: "No, YOU'RE stupid!" (catch, turn around, return)
- *Addition and removal*: "Yes, I agree, and to build on that…" or "I'd like to push back a little…"
- *Avoidance*: pretending not to hear; crossing the street
- *Negation*: "I'll go for anything except Chinese food"
- *Joining*: "me too"; voting (subordinating identity to a larger entity)
- *Scan-to-task*: placing unassigned resources into roles, as when picking teams for a pickup game

**Deliberative**: the sequence "arises from prediction, inference and *a priori* computation, rather than mimicry." It is not necessarily deterministic, since you can deliberately act randomly. You inject new information, so it needs preparation time, which is why thinking on your feet (real-time deliberation) impresses. You simulate a few possible worlds, and "whichever simulation gathers sufficient momentum to take control of your behavior first, generally wins." Each simulation pre-experiences its energy and emotion, so "we weigh options during the process of coming up with them." The logic follows the source metaphor: solids give the dynamics of solids, space gives time and movement, liquids give the laws of flow. Precise argument comes from mechanical assembly; De Bono contrasted *rock* logic with *water* logic. A deliberative pattern can end by handing off to a reactive one: prepare the elevator pitch, then *follow* the investor at the conference. The common forms:
- *If-then*: a candidate answers "What are your weaknesses?" with "I sometimes work too hard." Call out the cliché and you may learn about his honesty and humor (or he holds his position). Pointedly ignore it and change the subject, and he senses lost credibility and stays defensive.
- *Focusing*: "You've raised several points ... let me address them in order." A reactive move that simplifies the deliberation to come.
- *Deliberative dominance*: with a decisive agenda advantage (the interviewer's chair), predefine a landscape of ideas, where to go and what to avoid. Live, let that map dominate the situation "the way a set of dams, channels and valves controls the flow of water in a river valley", restricting the other person to predictable reactions while minimizing your own. Bill O'Reilly's "cut his mic" is the extreme.
- *Delayed reaction*: "wait till you see the whites of their eyes." The default is to push back when pushed. Adding a single deliberative element, a time delay computed off-stage to maximize effect, turns a reactive pattern into a deliberative one.
- *Recognition-primed deliberative action* (Gary Klein): when the theory of the situation matches a remembered one, from life or from a training exercise, possibly held only in muscle memory, steer by the old enactment. This is how narrative rationality grows out of calculative rationality. "An inefficient and time-consuming calculative-rational decision tree is reorganized as a more compact but cryptic narrative-rational one" as the novice folds each experience's cheap tricks into canned prescriptions. Novices go "by the book"; experts' tricks and hacks are "scribbled margin notes" improving the manual. Some get codified into a domain doctrine or design principles; others stay buried in muscle memory.

**Opportunistic**: "When an ongoing enactment is interrupted by a situation that is relevant to a different ongoing enactment, currently in suspension, you get opportunism." You pass a grocery store, remember you are out of coffee, and avoid both an extra trip and days without coffee. The keys are "*unconscious* recognition of a situation from one enactment while being absorbed in another, and the ability to artistically organize your enactments according to interval logic." It feels like a small "Aha!" because it shares the cheap trick's recognition of disproportionate reward. Trading enactments against each other is normally no-free-lunch, but "opportunistic tactical patterns violate no-free-lunch constraints by exploiting local conditions." It needs slack in the local interval logic: you would not stop for coffee while rushing to catch a plane. So "opportunism can often be catalyzed by introducing some sort of inefficiency into all your enactments. Temporal inefficiency (schedules with slack) is the simplest kind ... Highly optimized behaviors are also highly blinkered behaviors." It demands improvisation (in an unfamiliar store you wander the aisles randomly, which is exploratory, or zig-zag through all of them, which is reactive), and that makes it "the most demanding and creative kind." Gibson's Wintermute: "I improvise. It's my greatest talent. I prefer situations to plans."

**Procedural**: "when a decision-maker engages successfully in a complex and highly effective enactment without understanding the logic of his or her own behavior." Examples: using a formula you can't derive; a bureaucrat and a customer both complying with paperwork neither understands. It requires an environment holding mental models that others externalized through codification and embedding (*In* and *Out* trays, and the organizations they self-assemble into). The information has been "consciously engineered into the environment in a way that is only partly comprehended by the person being influenced." See `externalization.md`.

## Enactment styles and epochs

"The major claim in this book is that tempo can be used to modulate enactments that move along too quickly to manage one micro decision at a time." An **enactment style** combines a domain-specific aesthetic with a doctrine in a given situation. It is shaped by the doctrine but not defined by it. A tennis player with a strong backhand and a Ready-Fire-Aim doctrine plays aggressively off the backhand, may play differently in a particular match or when coaching, and adopts an unfamiliar, patient style where the domain rewards patience. An **epoch** is "a narrative-time period of indefinite length, characterized by a consistent enactment style." Narrative time only advances while an enactment progresses. To find your own epochs, review the last year of your calendar for the tempo changes that mark them.

"The *consistency* you exhibit in your variations reveals your enactment style. Style is always a cumulative phenomenon revealed through repeated reinforcement." A boxer reveals a style in seconds and a new CEO in quarters, yet both epochs may be the same narrative length: six punches, six meetings. In writing, one short sentence does little, but short sentences page after page set a rhythm. Consistent passive voice makes a text languid and active voice makes it energetic, and the mix modulates its energy. A whole passage without adjectives reads as Spartan. Choices like these "cumulatively affect rhythms, energy and emotions: they are about tempo." Each decision pattern is a pure style: bureaucratic style is near-exclusively procedural, improvisational style heavily opportunistic. Tactic-level flavors take adjectives from their source domains: *mechanical, fluid, heavy-handed*.

**Butterfly-Bee**: Muhammad Ali's "float like a butterfly, sting like a bee" mixes all four patterns:
- deliberative: the decision to keep moving fast;
- reactive-opportunistic: moving in response to the opponent, faster than him, and looking for chances to sting;
- procedural: by-the-book punch combinations inherited from training.

It works through pace-setting and pace-disruption by *acceleration*, and it is the simplest illustration of John Boyd's **OODA loop** (observe, orient, decide, act), a meta-style built on tempo, entropy and creative destruction. The central idea is to operate at a higher tempo than your opponent. "A higher tempo is *not* the same as higher speed ... think in terms of (narrative time) frequencies rather than speed." You "maneuver inside the decision cycle of your opponent, disrupting his or her enactment by introducing entropy into it faster than it can be removed." The opponent either stalls in the anxious exploration epoch or commits prematurely to the wrong cheap trick out of desperation. It works equally well in cooperation, a point many of Boyd's admirers miss: the upscale waiter who meets your needs before you recognize them is OODA, and so is exceptional customer service.

**Hierarchies.** In a well-aligned hierarchy, "Higher-level enactments in a hierarchy achieve control through modulation of faster lower-level enactments"; higher levels run at lower tempos. The usual reading, that higher levels have longer horizons, mangles this: "Horizons are actually determined by predictable epoch shifts (changes in tempo), rather than tempo itself." An authority that hides dissent behind a facade of consent must act through hierarchical models that presume consensus. That hands lower levels a channel for disruption, as in guerrilla war and intra-organizational rebellion: create "misleading patterns of information overload at higher levels," or force commitments with the *fait accompli* and other momentum tactics.

**Privileged information flows decide which style works.**
- **Bottom-up**: OODA styles win, as in combat, where "the fog of war blinds generals before it descends on privates."
- **Outward from a privileged, mid-tempo middle**: passive aggression works best. The Federal Reserve's rate tempo must be slower than the market it manages and faster than the legislature that oversees it.
- **Top-down**: autocratic regimes work best, as at Apple under Steve Jobs's design and market insight.
- *Yes Minister* and *Yes, Prime Minister* showcase middle-out and top-down styles.

## Skill: thriving in the strategy-tactics fog

The definitions: "*A strategy is a cheap trick.*" "*A tactic is a metaphoric mapping among primitive action concepts in two or more domains.*" They are deliberately non-prescriptive. Their value is like knowing that perpetual motion machines are impossible: they help you avoid certain patterns of failure, though "knowing how not to fail is not the same as knowing how to succeed." They remind you that novel perspectives and novel metaphor mappings are "locked in a yin-yang embrace that cannot be engineered away by any calculative-rational formula." "Decision-making is ultimately about constantly looking for the next cheap trick in ongoing enactments, scripted in constantly shifting tactical vocabularies. You will occasionally get a temporary and local advantage, but ultimately nature will defeat you." You can still consistently outmaneuver anyone who believes in universal formulas, as you can beat someone who always plays "rock."

Always playing rock means always reducing the fluid strategy-tactics distinction to one rigid, prescriptive distinction. Each such reduction is a **pattern of confusion** ("patterns of strategery"), recognizable by its characteristic language:
- *Levels and Horizons*: "Strategy is about the big picture/long-term; tactics are about the details/short-term."
- *What vs. How*: "Strategy is about *what* you want to do; tactics are about *how* you do it."
- *War vs. Battle*: "Strategy is about winning the war; tactics are about winning individual battles."
- *Why vs. What*: "Tactics is about *what* to do; strategy is about *why* you should do it."
- *Amateur vs. Professional*: "Amateurs worry about strategy and tactics while professionals worry about operations."
- *Process vs. Goal*: "That's a process question, we'll work that out later; let's establish our intent first."
- *Abstract vs. Specific*: "Let's consider this at a more abstract level" or "The devil is in the details."
- *Global vs. Local*: "We have to think globally, not locally" (the technically trained say "optimize").
- *Specialist vs. Generalist*: "Our problem is wooly headed generalists," or "our problem is narrow-minded specialists."
- *Conceptual vs. Technical*: "Forget those technical details; what's the *real* conceptual question here?"
- *Systems Thinking vs. Silo Thinking*: "We need more systems thinkers around here; everybody here is trapped in their own little silo."

Each distinction is useful in specific circumstances, which is what makes it tempting. **The diagnostic**: "the *same* distinction keeps popping up in *every* situation, with individuals consistently occupying the *same* position with respect to that distinction." It produces escalating "I am more big-picture/detail-oriented than thou" battles among those who hold the same position, and mutual contempt between opposite camps. The list is open-ended, since strategy gurus keep "selling perpetual creativity machines."

Two ways to exploit a pattern of confusion:
1. **Challenge it** with a situationally relevant counterexample that complicates the distinction or exaggerates it to absurdity, before it gathers momentum. For Levels and Horizons: "but we have a strategically critical presentation coming up tomorrow!" Open challenge generally leads to "tedious, thankless and ultimately doomed projects of language reform."
2. **Use it** (usually more productive): "reinforce individuals preferred distinctions and positions by appealing to their vanity, and using their immobility to make your own search for creative inspiration a little easier."

Definitions are unfalsifiable and are judged by the fertility of the questions they inspire. Rao's productive questions:
1. Are all tactics derived from universal tactics?
2. Is there a pure formula confined to one domain?
3. What distinguishes true cheap tricks from Aha! moments in simpler enactments?
4. Does a cheap trick's leverage diminish with reuse?
5. Does it diminish fast enough that there is no value in preparing to fight the last war?
6. Do forgotten historical cheap tricks regain their vigor?
7. Can your sense of history be systematically improved?
8. Is a cheap trick recognized by an adversary rendered useless?
9. "Is a strategy shared no longer a strategy?"
10. How does the efficient market hypothesis apply to the market of cheap tricks?
11. How quickly do cheap tricks diffuse within and across domains?
12. Can the time a cheap trick buys be computed *a priori*?
