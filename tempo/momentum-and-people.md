# Momentum in conversations, theories of situations, archetypes and doctrines

The depth behind momentum, theories of the situation, archetypes and doctrines in `SKILL.md`. Read it when the task turns on a live exchange, on modeling a specific person, or on a temperament (your own or someone else's).

## Momentum in conversation: the Wife Beater question

PROSECUTOR: "Have you stopped beating your wife?" DEFENDANT: "Yes,…no, … I mean … " PROSECUTOR: "Answer YES or NO!"

An effective response (one that strengthens the case for acquittal) has to:
1. resist the impulse to respond with fear or aggression;
2. foreground and invalidate the hidden assumption;
3. add any new assertions needed;
4. expand the space of acceptable answers;
5. choose one and respond.

All of it has to happen fast, because conversations accumulate meaning "even during interstitial silences ('pregnant pauses')."

The four candidate answers come from four mental models:
- Zen *"Mu"* (reject the premises) suits philosophical debate and is irrelevant in court.
- Logical "I don't beat my wife" lacks the emotional charge the accusation calls for and looks weak and defensive.
- Aggressive "What the hell kind of question is that?" brings in street-smart posturing and shows the jury a violent temperament.
- Situation-appropriate (assertively): "Are you trying to browbeat me?" It "refuses to acknowledge the sly framing, questions the motivations of the prosecutor, and reclaims momentum by answering the question with a question."

Archetypes set the conversational tempo defaults. A defendant who sees himself as *can-beat-the-system guy* facing a *Machiavelli* runs at *energy: high, emotion: assertive, rhythm: quick*.

## What a live mental model holds

A snapshot of a cook's mind mid-dinner shows how mixed a mental model is (Rao compares it to Borges' Chinese encyclopedia). It holds:
- concepts (*knife, apple*) and unique instances ("my big pan with the dent in it");
- people-concepts (self, guest, vegetarian);
- usually-true assertions ("knives cut apples") and vague beliefs ("he is a finicky eater");
- desires ("something crunchy tonight");
- intentions, which are commitments to *classes* of possible worlds ("I'll make lasagna");
- articles of faith ("healthy meals must include leafy greens");
- true right-now beliefs ("the big pan is in the dishwasher");
- premises for possible worlds ("what if one of my dinner guests is allergic to nuts?") and plug-and-play beliefs ("at least one vegetarian");
- awareness of dynamics ("the water is coming to a boil") and activated procedural fragments, including muscle memory;
- self-referential beliefs ("I feel like I am missing something"), numbers (six plates, eight guests), and beliefs about emotion and energy ("frustrated with this dough");
- inference snippets ("If I don't slow down, I'll ruin this pancake") and quick-reaction policies ("If pot boils over, turn off flame immediately");
- beliefs about yourself ("I always leave everything till the last minute");
- constantly mutating story-plans (chop the vegetables, do the dessert, step out for wine while it bakes).

A mental model relates to a possible world as a vocabulary relates to a story. The vocabulary restricts what can be said, and the story uses part of it, occasionally leaking out through neologisms. Intentions harden through lock-in tactics such as burning bridges and penalty clauses.

## Theories of situations in motion: *The Party*

At a large party your *Party* model is active from the moment you dress. In one corner a man performs animatedly for a woman, who encourages him with cool amusement, while a second, tense man stands mostly ignored. *Love triangle*: A and B are both attracted to C, and C is mildly attracted to A. Archetypes kick in (*Jock, Underdog, Ice Maiden*), and so does a doctrine ("support the underdog"). The background desire "Have fun" generates a foreground intention, and that intention creates an expectation.

You walk up thick-skinned. Underdog says he is an airline pilot, so you ask his view of a recent crash to make him look good. Three versions follow:
- **Validated.** Underdog holds forth and turns toward Ice Maiden; Jock clumsily tries to change the subject; Ice Maiden steers back with her aunt's crash story. "My work here is done," and your archetypes are reinforced. Failure (Underdog telling the story badly) would also have fit the theory ("well, I tried"). Either way the tempo doodle runs from straggly and uncertain to decisive and strident.
- **Invalidated.** Underdog says "Ah, that's been beaten to death" and has Jock carry on, cutting you out. You feel a stab, think "well, I read *that* wrong!", and retreat. Then you see what was bothering you: Underdog kept glancing at his watch and the door. Your uncertainty finds a focal point, with relief, and you form the theory *Late Date*: the two men are friends, Underdog dominant, waiting for his date. It is confirmed when she arrives, at the cost of a social bruise.
- **Model switched out.** The trio pull guns and rob the party. *Party* is replaced by *Hostage Drama*, Underdog's anxiety is reinterpreted, and the archetypes change (*Butch Cassidy, the Sundance Kid, Etta Place*).

"The one thing all enactments have in common is the rise and fall of dramatic tension, experienced as variations in tempo, created by the changing momentum."

The past and the future both act on present tempo. Debating whether to stop for coffee on the drive to work, the dread of a pending report drains momentum while anticipation of seeing the barista energizes. Both show up in how you ride the brakes and take turns. A neighbor's slightly cold "Hello" spawns two possible histories (bad mood, or upset with me), and both color the morning.

## Archetypes

"Your mental models of people are archetypes"; your model of yourself is a **self-archetype**, "the construct through which your sense of self inexorably gathers momentum through life." Archetypes are "essentially artistic rather than analytical constructs," with no fundamental taxonomy. Examples range widely:
- fox and hedgehog ("the fox knows many things, the hedgehog knows one big thing"; for Isaiah Berlin, hedgehogs seek a grand, unified worldview and foxes are happy with a fragmented one);
- Freudian ones (Berne's *Games People Play*) and Jungian ones (Myers-Briggs);
- pop culture (*Soccer Mom, Joe the Plumber*) and fiction (*Lady Macbeth, Sherlock Holmes*);
- animals (*The Wind in the Willows*, Pullman's daemons, familiars, Hindu *vahanas*).

The most interesting are very local: "why do you always have to …" is an informal archetype at work. Without archetypes, conversations would have silences of minutes rather than tenths of a second.

Two cautions from Rao: "archetypes are an artistic thinking tool, not a dictionary. If you don't feel the urge to mess with this list, you probably shouldn't use it at all." And every universe of archetypes leaves things out: avoid "locking yourself into limiting self-perceptions, or consigning others permanently to pigeonholes."

## Doctrines

"Archetypes give rise to doctrines. Doctrines are basic sets of beliefs and desires relevant to decision-making. They are particularly relevant to momentum management." "Your most stable beliefs, the ones that actually modulate your behavior, aren't about life purposes; they are about momentum management. You are more likely to switch religions than to switch from an impatient to a patient temperament." Kabir offers two opposed doctrines. One: "What you would tomorrow, do today, what you would today, do now. In a moment, the world may end, How will you finish then?" The other: "Slowly, slowly, oh mind, slowly the world evolves. The impatient gardener may flood his orchard, but the fruit will ripen only in season." Doctrines persist across centuries in nations too: Mahan found British, Dutch and French naval doctrines kept reflecting national personalities.

Doctrines evolve at liminal passages. An idealist living by "I can do anything I set my mind to" (MM) has the work destroyed by an earthquake, extracts the moral "outcomes are never certain," and matures into "I can take on anything, give it my all, and walk away content even if I fail. What doesn't kill me only makes me stronger" (RIF's belief).

## Skill: archetype impressions

The real skill is building **unique archetypes**, models of single individuals. Your self-archetype is a complex project you do alone, but your models of others can be simpler. You need data on characteristic patterns ("imprint of a pattern of human behavior"). We usually mimic others derisively, in fights, and pick behaviors that arise from weakness, which is counterproductive outside true zero-sum fights. Notice the positive patterns that arise from people's strengths instead. Among effective people this gives more accurate archetypes, because their strengths are more developed than their weaknesses.

1. Notice repeated phrases and assertions, like "Are we ready yet? Let's GO!"
2. Each time you notice one, reinforce the observation by scribbling a note or repeating it silently.
3. Occasionally, in jest or in situations where your subject might use the phrase, mimic-repeat it. Saying it at the exact instant they do is timing artistry, a "finish each other's sentences" mind-meld.
4. Move on to exhortation: use their pet phrase when they themselves hesitate ("What do you say, Let's GO! Right?").
5. Once really comfortable with the pattern, use the "hang by own petard" tactic: persuade people with their own favorite assertions. At best this means you "tactfully help others be consistent and lock-in their positive behaviors firmly."

Mimic in front of the person to validate the pattern. A real positive pattern will be appreciated (or will give them self-awareness), and a mistaken one will be challenged. Once you know enough patterns, match the person to a broad archetype: perhaps your boss is roughly a fox or a hedgehog. Keith Johnstone's *Impro* is the practice manual.
