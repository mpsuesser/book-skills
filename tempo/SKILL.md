# Tempo

Venkatesh Rao's lens on decision-making. Decisions come too fast to manage one at a time: at 30 miles per hour a driver takes in about 1,320 "items of information" per minute. Tempo varies slowly enough that you can keep it in awareness and "use it to modulate the blurry torrent of your life." Managing tempo also protects the few big decisions you do pluck out for conscious processing: "The more you consciously manage tempo, the more creative and realistic your options during the big decisions."

The lens raises *when* (with *where* and *who*: timing, framing, background, context) above *what* (options), *why* (causation, motivation, reward) and *how* (means-ends planning). "*when* today stands supreme as the fundamental one." Restrict yourself to the first three and you live with the Red Queen, running faster to stay in place. For micro-decisions (answer this email now or later? special meeting or the routine weekly one?) the lens is timing. For big ones it is synthesis, design, metaphor and storytelling, not selection among options: college is "the creative process of continuously retelling and enacting the most compelling *College* story you can." The larger the scale, the further the six *W* questions recede.

Think in Rao's terms as defined below, across every domain the book draws on: kitchen, workplace, personal life, traffic, war, sports, music, comedy.

## Core vocabulary

**Tempo**: "the set of characteristic rhythms of decision-making in the subjective life of an individual or organization, colored by associated patterns of emotion and energy." It has three elements: **rhythm, emotion and energy** (as *adagio, allegro, presto* convey mood and energy as well as pace). Rhythm is many frequencies combined, with a texture or *timbre*. You can change a tempo with four levers:
- **frequency**: faster or slower;
- **phase**: switching from lark to owl, or "Early to bed, early to rise";
- **energy**: the same rhythm, or a slower one, with more energy in it gets more done;
- **duty cycle**: the share of up-time to downtime, slow-and-steady versus spiky.

**Emotion and timing.** "Emotion results when you force yourself, or some part of the environment, to operate at a faster or slower tempo than it likes. To change a tempo you must add or remove energy by applying a force." Picture a child on a tire swing: when and in which direction you push makes it go higher, dampens it, or changes its direction or phase, and where you hold the rope changes its frequency. Drive tempo up too far and calm turns to excitement, then anxiety, then panic (this is how FUD and "shock and awe" work). Damp it too much and calm turns to impatience, frustration, anger and finally depressed acceptance; the Kubler-Ross stages are the extreme case, a living tempo brought to a dead stop. With no change at all, "Stay too long at one tempo, and boredom will set in."

**Sense of timing**: the behaviors involved in managing tempo. "The most basic decision-making skill is adapting to the tempo of your environment, and setting your own pace within it. It is significantly harder to become a pace-setter or pace-disruptor." Highway driving shows all four behaviors:
- **Merging**: context-switching into a situation's flow. You judge its speed and spacing and slide into a gap. This means developing situation awareness (below).
- **Going with the flow**: the path of least resistance. Automate what you can, because "Decision-making is hard." It is smart in a stable, rewarding niche where *what* arrives self-defined, *why* is well motivated, and *how* is practiced, so that when/where/who shrink to "*here, now* and *in relation to the cars around me*." The failure mode is going **ritualized** when assumptions are never re-examined: "*in a groove* and *in a rut*" show how efficiency and the potential for irrationality go together. Choosing the venue is itself a timing decision. Whether you share critical news at the next routine meeting or through an urgent cascade of emails that triggers an exceptional meeting "will actually drive the urgency of your organization's reaction to the news more than the content of the news itself." Routine handling of critical news breeds dangerous complacency ("did you not recognize the importance of this?"). Exception handling for trivia breeds overreaction and "cry-wolf patterns of career self-sabotage."
- **Pace-setting**: "the art of harmoniously driving the natural tempo of your environment away from its current state and towards your preferred state – slower or faster – in non-disruptive ways." The default go-with-the-flow option is usually unconscious, so the change-the-pace option has to be adopted deliberately. The **operating point** is the current tempo. The **operating envelope** is the range of safe paces, or gaits (saunter, walk, jog, run, sprint), each with its own rate of energy use. The **comfort zone**, at the heart of the envelope, can be traversed "without significant changes in the emotional quality of the tempo". Beyond it lies the pace-setting zone of charged emotional responses (running is exciting; walking very slowly is frustrating unless you are a monk). Push the boundary of the envelope itself and you get dissonance. Energy reserves set how long you can hold a pace: walk for hours, sprint for ten seconds.
- **Disruption (dissonance)**: the most complex skill. It needs a musician's aesthetic and involves "real creative-destruction" with the potential for irreversible structural change. "Boredom is your ally": "the most sublime moments of artistry are often achieved when you judge the onset of boredom just right, and insert a smart disruption of tempo that takes advantage of the latent energy of boredom to create momentum." Used judiciously, interrupting and talking over others is how you "arrest the momentum of developing group-think and assumed consensus." Develop the thick skin to interrupt and to be interrupted, or you will help enable pathological decision-making cultures. Too thick a skin enables abrasiveness and numbs emotion, which you need for situation awareness.

The four behaviors combine in a comedian's improvised gag: high situation awareness of the venue, momentum kept up with punch lines as "push the swing" moments, the tempo of the evening dominated through pace-setting, then a new line slipped in that disrupts the canned logic. They come together "within a single short window of opportunity; and risk, luck and boldness conspire to create art from craft." Each artistic leap of faith increases your boldness, and boldness improves your eye for the next opening.

**Situation awareness** (Mica Endsley's military definition): "The perception of environmental elements within a volume of time and space, the comprehension of their meaning, and the projection of their status in the near future." For information work, read *space* as *virtual social environment*. Most micro-decisions go simply to maintaining it: *truth maintenance*, the largely subconscious work of deciding which beliefs to add, update or discard. Developing it is "primarily the process of getting attuned to the dynamics of the background." You separate change into a **background** of predictable rhythms and a **foreground** of unusual rhythms and non-rhythmic elements; "Anything left over and unexplained is the foreground: raw material for active engagement." In many domains you can only develop it by acting: a new cricket batsman develops it ("getting your eye in") by cautiously playing the first few balls, then signals dominance with one confident, decisive shot. Context switches are asymmetric. "Preparing your mind for the *to* domain is uphill work. Letting go of the *from* domain is downhill work." That is why capturing notes after a meeting comes easily, preparing before it comes hard, and start-up rituals (agenda review, approval of minutes) are more common than exit rituals.

**Mental model**: "a dynamic, unstable and partially coherent set of beliefs, desires and intentions held together by narratives that weave through the current realities, possible histories and possible futures of a situation." Its primitives come from Bratman's BDI framework: "beliefs create or constrain possibilities, desires lead to preferences among them, and intentions represent commitments to specific courses of action." The narratives are **possible worlds**. Intentions are "informal, dynamic working focal points," not formal goals. Situation awareness is our sense of the current quality and relevance of the active mental model.

**Momentum**: "The sheer quantity of information in a mental model provides the inertial mass that carries the momentum. Momentum comprises inertia and movement." Beliefs refresh and lock in futures. A belief that the big pan is in the dishwasher is 15 hours old until you see it there, and deciding to use it for dinner extends a possible world five hours ahead. Model movement is "a staccato process comprising dozens of changes every minute ... This is the process that we experience as decision-making." Momentum is coupled to emotion.

**Enactment**: the part of the active mental model becoming real as time passes. You understand it through a **history** (a possible world you claim might be true) and an **expectation** (a possible world that might *become* a history, partly through your actions). Delusions are false history/expectation pairs, the equivalent of flawed science. "Planning" and "execution" are only "symbolic markers at points where significant and irreversible changes in tempo occur." Switching from Plan A to Plan B shifts momentum between bundles of possible worlds: "The clean fork-in-the-road shift is the exception; the dissipation of momentum associated with one course of action, and the cohering of another, is the rule." At low momentum (pen and paper, first look), past and future act on you through conscious thought. At high momentum they act directly, through policies and predispositions, so "pay more attention to the emotional quality of the pasts and futures you are considering."

**Theory of the situation**: "an informal organizing pattern (either recognized from past experience, or improvised) that accelerates the development of situation awareness by providing a strong filter." Input that refines, confirms or invalidates it bubbles up to the foreground, and irrelevant input is banished to the background. "Elements of unclear relevance remain in limbo as a vague sense of uncertainty, creating drag, which we experience as doubt." When outcomes confirm the theory, the enactment gathers momentum and you act with growing decisiveness. Unexpected consequences or growing uncertainty drain momentum "until we switch to a better theory." Conversations are "the *e. coli* of momentum-driven decision-making": you need only get each response roughly right and correct course, because effects "are cumulative rather than isolated." Keith Johnstone's rule for improvisation (all you need to know is "what status to play") points to the book's claim that "only a few variables need to be managed to control a stream of decision-making": archetype assessments and tempo-control settings such as *energy: high, emotion: assertive, rhythm: quick*. For worked examples (the Wife Beater question, the three versions of *The Party*), archetypes and the ten doctrines, read [`momentum-and-people.md`](momentum-and-people.md).

**Decision dynamics**: the overall process, which Rao likens to "an entire planetary climate system in your head." Three forces change a model's momentum: internal stresses, other mental models, and interaction with reality-data. Expect meteorology, not billiard balls; pendulum-level theories such as game theory, used as generic theories, give "a false sense of clarity and security." The named phenomena are:
1. ***Fait accompli***: "an irreversible commitment to a course of enactment before a stakeholder has a chance to contribute." The other side's alternate possible worlds "die a sudden death, taking your psychological investment – the momentum – with them." It is a pace-setting tactic: commit before you reach the default venue.
2. ***Brinkmanship***: "the calibrated letting go of control of a consequential decision." Decisive movement (troops to the border) gives the other side's worst-case model momentum and surfaces new theories ("rogue field commander with a hair-trigger temper").
3. ***Procrastination***: delaying a context switch "by adding more momentum to our current mental model, thereby making it harder to displace." It usually takes the form of **displacement**, such as cleaning the apartment instead of facing the dissertation. "Within limits, this is often a *rational* reaction": the displacement can serve as a warm-up. Diving in unwarmed can start a vicious cycle of increasing aversion.
4. ***Second-guessing***: "works by forcing someone to reverse acts of destruction". The delegate has already paid to kill possible worlds and must resurrect them. "Next time, you won't put so much momentum in to begin with."
5. ***Passive aggression***: "works by fragmenting and dissipating momentum." It kills an anticipated positive future (*Sale!*) and forces you to spin up several possible worlds about the aggressor's unclear intentions (the URGENT!!! email seen at 5:03 PM: "Oh, I didn't see the email till this morning").
6. ***Time-outs***: "Waiting drains emotions from situations and associated mental models." It is unreliable, because waiting can produce a "getting angrier and angrier" effect.
7. ***Defaulting***: occurs in systems that combine defaults with preprogrammed commitment patterns. "If you don't keep up and over-ride where necessary, you will default to outcomes." Boiled frogs are the extreme case, and it matters most with automated processes.
8. ***Building mindshare***: collective momentum-building, especially for changing system defaults, through meetings, presentations with examples, what-if stories, prototypes and demos. "You do not need to drive overtly towards a decision."

Folk idioms carry the same dynamics: *strike while the iron is hot*, *that ship has sailed*, *in the fullness of time*, and the Hindi *eet ka jawab pathar se dena* ("to reply to a brick with a rock": answering provocation with escalation).

**Calculative vs narrative rationality.** **Calculative rationality** is Jomini's process approach, carried on in business by Michael Porter: perceive, generate options, calculate, act. It assumes an appropriate mental model rather than building one. "This simple view is not wrong, it is just limited to simple situations that fit one or more of your existing mental models very well." In complex situations planning is "merely a training exercise to sample the space of possible worlds" ("plans are nothing, planning is everything"). **Narrative rationality**, whose roots run back to Clausewitz, starts from "all our choices are among life stories that end with our individual deaths." It is "*the ability to think, make decisions, and act in ways that make sense with respect to the most compelling and elegant story that you can improvise about a developing enactment.*" It lets you "structure your behaviors meaningfully even when feedback is impoverished, delayed and ambiguous." Rationality is situated and relative: there are "many useful, local and temporary rationalities," and "there is no such thing as non-narrative thought." The defense against the narrative fallacy is "ironic skepticism and systematic doubt" added to your narrative imagination.

**Deep story**: an enactment between two **liminal passages** (interludes of existential musing between the waning of one important life story and the waxing of another). It is "an episode of creative destruction that is significant enough to transform you." Liminal passages chop life into deep stories and create **the fundamental tempo** of life. The canonical shape is the **Double Freytag triangle**, read as entropy (disorder of the developing mental model) over time, and each phase has a characteristic tempo:

| Phase | Characteristic tempo | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Liminal passage | stillness | Doctrine and self-archetype take center stage; existential musing |
| Exploration | volatile, dissipative | Random, unsupervised learning; entropy rises ("I'll know when I find it") |
| **Cheap trick** | crescendo | An exploitable pattern is spotted; a relevance boundary splits meaning from noise |
| Sense-making | decrescendo with emotional relief | The model crystallizes around the trick; entropy falls |
| Valley | steady, with slowing momentum and increasing depression | "Decisive action without either reward or validation" |
| Heavy lift | a high-effort, low-coherence increase in momentum | A deliberate final burst that forces an encounter with reality |
| Separation event | crescendo | The model is externalized; the moment of truth |
| Retrospective | decrescendo and a mix of joy and sorrow | Doctrine revised; the memory fixed in its final form |

The **cheap trick** is to a deep story what a theory of the situation is to an ordinary enactment. Its name comes from Mencken: "for every complex question, there is an answer that is simple, elegant and wrong." It "merely buys you a certain amount of time," and if its leverage earns rewards within that time you have "cheated nature." Its timing is "the battle between your impatience and your ability to endure dissonance": too early is expediency (premature optimization), too late is "death by perfectionism." For the full phase guide, discovering deep stories, the Freytag staircase, narrative time and Tetris thinking, read [`deep-stories.md`](deep-stories.md).

**Universal tactics, decision patterns, strategy.** Universal tactics are "primitive concepts of action, such as *push* and *pull,*" drawn by conceptual metaphor from universally experienced domains (space, matter, pattern). They combine into four basic **decision patterns**: reactive, deliberative, opportunistic and procedural. A consistent way of modulating enactments by tempo is an **enactment style**, a domain aesthetic combined with a doctrine, "the primary signature of an enactment style is its characteristic tempo." The book's definitions: "*A strategy is a cheap trick.*" "*A tactic is a metaphoric mapping among primitive action concepts in two or more domains.*" For the tactic inventories, the pattern tests, OODA and Butterfly-Bee, hierarchies, and the patterns of confusion around strategy and tactics, read [`tactics.md`](tactics.md).

**Externalized mental models**: the material things we create are "parts of reality arranged to conform to the structure of an internal mental model." We externalize by **codification** and **embedding**, and others **internalize** what we externalized. Social reality is read in **fields** (arrangements of the environment) and **flows** (behaviors that result when humans interact with a field). Its tempo can be read even where its meaning is illegible: "Tempo cannot be easily hidden." For groups, organizations, workspaces, venues and cultures, read [`externalization.md`](externalization.md).

**Clockless clock**: the pinnacle of artistry, where tempo and momentum are so well managed that the one fork-in-the-road moment hardly matters: "The narrative can end gracefully whatever" the other party chooses. All the concepts are scaffolding for this state. It is a sensibility to develop rather than an idea to apply. Beneath it runs the book's overarching tension between action and contemplation: "the unexamined life is not worth living. Equally ... the unlived life is not worth examining." The aim is a mode of life that "doubts the possibility of meaning sufficiently to choose action, and believes in the possibility sufficiently to be tempted into reflection," turning the tension into creative-destructive energy.

## Applying the lens

Work through these readings of the user's situation in order, naming each finding in Rao's terms. You are done when you can state the move, its timing and its venue, and say which tempo, momentum and story reading justifies it.

1. **Read the tempo.** Name the background rhythms (daily heartbeat, weekly meeting, quarterly drumbeat, release schedule, seasonal cycle) and what stands out in the foreground. Rate rhythm, emotion and energy. Place the setting "on a spectrum between complacency and panic", and ask whether email threads straggle into lethargic non-response or cascade with chain-reaction urgency. Note boredom building, and whose tempo is being forced faster or slower than it likes.
2. **Read the momentum.** Name the active mental models and the theory of the situation each party holds. Find what is gathering momentum and what is dissipating, and where doubt (unexplained elements) is creating drag. Name any decision-dynamics phenomenon at work, done by or to the user.
3. **Read the narrative time.** Decide whether this is an ordinary enactment or a deep story, and for whom (to the priest a wedding is routine; to the couple it is a deep story). For a deep story, name the phase and its characteristic tempo, and what the phase calls for next.
4. **Choose the timing behavior.** Decide between merging, going with the flow, pace-setting and disrupting, and pick the lever: frequency, phase, energy or duty cycle. Then place the act against the surrounding events with the thirteen interval relations, and pick the venue (routine or exceptional) knowing the venue sets the urgency. The relations, calendar art and worked timing examples are in [`timing.md`](timing.md).
5. **Act roughly right, then correct course.** Effects are cumulative, and you will often get situation awareness only by acting. Treat any organizing insight as a cheap trick that buys time.
