# Headlines

Read this when writing or judging a headline, subject line, subhead, tagline, slogan or first line.

A headline "is actually the most important sentence because if you write a bad one, nobody will care enough to keep reading. ... If you write a bad headline, *you fail.*" A flat headline is "white paper on a white desk." Schwartz's rule covers every sentence, headlines first: "No sentence can be effective if it contains facts alone. It must also contain emotion, image, logic, and promise."

## Make it compelling: four core ways

1. **Emotional: make it *dramatic*.** "They Laughed When I Sat Down At the Piano—But When I Started to Play!" (John Caples), one of the most successful headlines of the 20th century. Dramatizing the claim, or its result, is storytelling: the prospect sees a clear narrative in as few words as possible, and if she can relate to it, you have her attention.
2. **Vivid: make it *appeal to the senses*.** "Tastes Like You Just Picked It!" (United Fruit Company). Sensitizing the claim, so the prospect feels, smells, touches, sees or hears it, transports her to a moment. Here it's a hungry one.
3. **Logical: make it *a question*.** "Has This Man Really Discovered the Secret of Inevitable Wealth?" (Gary Bencivenga). To make someone think, ask a question. A good question changes perspective, and "A change in perspective is worth 80 IQ points" (Alan Kay).
4. **Hopeful: make it *inspirational*.** "To The Man Who Is Afraid To Let His Dreams Come True" (Rolls Royce). It ran during the Great Depression and sold more cars than any Rolls Royce ad before it. An inspirational headline challenges the prospect's limiting beliefs and helps her with the perennial struggle between doing what she wants and doing what's expected of her. Where appropriate, write a headline that bolsters hope.

Four more ways:

- *Metaphorical*: "Banishes Acne!" (Pears Soap).
- *Inherently valuable*: "What Everybody Ought to Know About the Stock Market" (Merrill Lynch).
- *Addressing the prospect directly*: "To People Who Want to Write But Can't Get Started" (Newspaper Institute of America).
- *Curious*: see impossible headlines below.

## Self-interest or news

Caples, after thousands of headlines: "The best headlines appeal to people's self-interest, or give news." Promise something *helpful* or something *new*.

- **Self-interest words**: sale, save, easy, quick, results, proven, how to, guarantee, last chance.
- **News words**: new, now, at last, it's here, discover, breaking, just arrived, introducing, announcing.

## Big promises

A headline needn't be outrageous ("How The Broke, Homeless, 28-Year-Old Became a Millionaire in 6 Months"), but it has to "make or imply a fairly big promise" (Bob Bly). Bly's challenger to a planner company's control is the model. The control read:

> How many times have you told yourself: "Next week I'm going to get organized..."

Bly's version beat it by 50 percent:

> Inside: Now you can get at least one more hour of productive time each day than Day-Timer or other planning systems can give you *guaranteed*.

Three things did the work: the promise itself, "inside" (instant gratification) and "guaranteed" (de-risks the claim). Aim for "a clear, specific, big promise."

## Specific numbers

Milt Pierce tested *28*, *17* and *45 Ways* before *32 Ways to Save Time & Money from the Pages of Good Housekeeping* won and ran unbeaten for almost 20 years. A number is "an implied promise that you'll be getting certain, specific information." Make it specific: *177 Ways*, not *200 Ways*. A round number invites "Did you pad it?" "Generalities roll off the human understanding like water off a duck's back" (Claude Hopkins). "Specifics sell, generalities do not" (Denny Hatch).

## Conditions

Grounding a claim makes it believable. In 1974 Dan Rosenthal's "2 reasons why the price of silver **may** rise steeply" outperformed Bencivenga's bolder "**will**" version "by maybe 200 percent": one word, a 200 percent lift. Conditions apply to bullets and body claims too. See [persuasion.md](persuasion.md).

## Impossible headlines

Curiosity is "one of the strongest human incentives" (Hopkins). "An impossible headline deliberately makes a preposterous claim or promises a baffling outcome. It opens a loop so strange and implausible, so curious, The Reader feels she must keep reading to close it." Hamilton watches, 1941: "We waited 21 years for a train…*and missed it!*" Proud parents missed their son's college graduation because Dad's watch lied. The headline presents a completely typical situation in an impossible way.

How: "write the story *first*, then reverse-engineer the headline to bring it full circle."

## Incomplete headlines

Joe Sugarman wrote headlines so short they felt incomplete: "Hungarian Conspiracy," "Mail Order Mansion," "Gold Space Chains," "Magic Baloney," "Pet Plane," "HOT!" An ultra-short headline creates momentum, moving The Reader into the next line almost automatically. "The sole purpose of the first sentence in an ad is to get you to read the second sentence. Nothing more, nothing less." His "Vision Breakthrough" opened the ad that sold 20 million BluBlocker sunglasses. Its subhead was "When I put on the pair of glasses what I saw I could not believe. Nor will you."

## Taglines and slogans

- **Metaphors** reduce a complex concept to a single, vivid image: "King of Beers," "The Heartbeat of America," "Red Bull gives you wiiings." Plug the product into a template and finesse it: "It's a ___." / "It's ___ meets ___." / "It's a ___ for your ___."
- **Brand = Adjective.** The best brands stand for one thing. Volvo = Safe. See [campaigns-and-positioning.md](campaigns-and-positioning.md).

## Small promises

A colon is a small promise of more information: an answer, a reason, or something else that closes the loop opened *before* the colon. Use it to propel The Reader through the copy.
