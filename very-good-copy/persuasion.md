# Proof, credibility and the psychology of response

Read this when copy makes a claim that must be believed, needs proof, testimonials, guarantees or urgency, or leans on a persuasion trigger.

"Human nature is perpetual. In most respects, it is the same today as in the time of Caesar. So, the principles of psychology are fixed and enduring. You will never need to unlearn what you learn about them" (Claude Hopkins). People everywhere respond to curiosity, social proof, high and low prices, scarcity and urgency, certain colors, guarantees and other **persuasion triggers** "in basically the same way."

## Proof

If the prospect doesn't *believe* your claim, the promise your ad makes, "you're dead in the water." "Proof is the rarest component to manufacture" (Joe Sugarman). Each form of proof has a direction to push in:

- **Testimonials prove.** The more, the better. Two reviews are good; two hundred are impossible to ignore. The prospect thinks *It worked for them; it'll work for me.*
- **Guarantees prove.** The more courageous, the better. A "30-day return policy" is good; a "lifetime guarantee" communicates the utmost confidence. The prospect thinks *I've nothing to lose.*
- **Demonstrations prove.** The more dramatic, the better. Cleaning a greasy plate with dish soap is good; using that soap to clean an oil-spill duckling is remarkable. The prospect thinks *Seeing is believing.*
- **Video testimonials**, especially from notable and trusted people, "are among the most potent sources of credibility and validation, mainly because they're so damn hard to procure." In a reality-TV experiment called *The Push*, fake celebrity video endorsements were what convinced the subject that a made-up charity was real. Use them whenever possible and appropriate.

## Heuristics, LISH and the testimonial wall

People decide two ways. **Systematic processing** is the hard way: "critical thinking," deliberately analyzing whether information is valid and reliable, which tires us out. **Heuristic processing** is the easy way: deciding "through cues and shortcuts." Brains developed heuristics to avoid thinking, because thinking deeply is taxing.

The **Length-Implies-Strength Heuristic (LISH)** is the assumption that more words equal more substance, so *longer* copy seems more impressive and believable. A 200-page book seems more credible than a 60-page one on the same subject. A **testimonial wall** (hundreds of positive reviews in one place, as on Amazon) triggers it. Nobody reads every review; they get the message of a positive consensus, that all these customers can't be wrong. Shleyner put a wall of hundreds of reviews on his homepage and subscriptions "shot up." Recommend one when a page has the reviews to fill it.

## Conditions

"When you make a claim, put a 'condition' on it." A condition makes clear the claim isn't a 100 percent certainty. It grounds the claim and makes it more feasible, and "belief garners trust, and trust compels action." Conditions take the edge off a promise that sounds too good to be true, "snoozing the prospect's '*Yeah, sure…*' alarm" (Bencivenga). Ben Settle's bullets show how:

- "*almost* neurologically impossible for people to delete your launch and affiliate emails"
- "*Believe it or not*, this is one of the fastest, easiest, most ethical ways"
- "it *usually* only takes a few minutes"
- "*Do it correctly*, and it can create an overwhelming need"

The silver headline that said "may" beat the one that said "will" by about 200 percent.

## Truth over hype

**Exclamation marks** are, "very often, less 'truth' and more contrived enthusiasm, *hype*," and The Reader sniffs it out and resents the manipulation. Ben Settle's fastest cure for hype: "get rid of all the exclamation marks or use them really sparingly... especially if what you're saying is *true*." "Truth is the ultimate exclamation mark." Put something truly valuable in front of the ideal prospect, and "Just saying it—with a period—makes it exciting." The same goes for volume. When a director asked Robert De Niro for "more energetic," De Niro answered "*that's* energetic... I'm not selling cars, okay?" The energy is in the message. "If your message is inherently relevant and interesting to the audience, you don't need to shout it."

## Urgency, tension and risk

If you give people time to decide, they'll usually take it. In direct response you want The Reader to feel tension and pressure, because those feelings compel action. Three levers:

1. **Dimensionalize the savings**: "Buy 2, get 1 free." "Get 3 for the price of 2." "Save $40…it's like getting another bottle FREE!"
2. **Create urgency**:
   - Timing deadlines: "Save 50%, offer ends at midnight."
   - Trial deadlines: "Start for just $1, today only."
   - Scarcity deadlines: "Only 30 units left."
3. **Remove the risk of ordering**: "Money-back guarantee." "*Double*-your-money-back guarantee." "Order now, pay later."

**Shock copy** creates urgency for a reader who resists acting (see [storytelling.md](storytelling.md)).

## Other triggers

- **Pain over pleasure**: "We jog to pleasure, but we sprint from pain." Bob Stone's Gem gives the consequences of inaction their own step.
- **The norm of reciprocity**: it is human to want to return a gesture, and "we tend to give back far more than we receive," especially after a no-strings-attached gift. "This is how free samples turn into sales." Be generous, in life and in marketing.
- **Harmonizing**: questions that tee up affirmative answers create positive inertia, and "Agreement begets agreement." At the door: "Do you read magazines?" "Got one in the house?" "Bet it cost about six bucks, right?" Then the offer: "So you get about nine issues *for free*."
- **Habituation**: "We acclimate to anything we consistently experience." Hopkins: guarantees became "so commonly sold that guarantees have ceased to be impressive." When every bicycle maker offered a money-back guarantee, the Mead Cycle Company's "30 Days Free Trial" won. It wasn't economically better; it was novel, and people noticed it. When a device is standard in the category, change its form.
- **Mere exposure**: repetition breeds familiarity, and people prefer familiar things.
- **Simplicity**: "Simple sells. And very simple sells very well." "What is simple is more convincing than what is complex." Keep the offer simple too, "because a complex decision is often a delayed decision" (David Deutsch).
