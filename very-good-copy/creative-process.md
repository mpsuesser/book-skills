# The creative process

Read this when stuck, blocked, out of ideas or on a deadline, or when deciding how to generate ideas, draft, rest and stop.

## How ideas happen

- **Creativity is connection.** "You can't take nothing and make anything. You're not God" (Schwartz). Creativity is "putting old things together in new ways," a two-step process:
  1. Get knowledge and experience.
  2. Make unexpected connections.

  Seek out places, people and perspectives new to *you*, then ask: "How can I *connect* this place or person or point of view to my product in a compelling way?"
- **Originality is an illusion.** "Everything is a remix." The **Cult of Originality** wants you to think new things are "new" only if they're "unrecognizable, void of influence, pure." Instead: "A new thing is born when you connect two or more old things in a surprising, seamless way." Justice Holmes: "To take someone else's idea and claim it for your own is stealing. But to take ideas from two or more sources and *combine* them, that's creativity." **Something familiar made new** means taking an old story, concept or product and presenting it "in a new place, time, or circumstance."
- **Highly creative people** have *more diverse experiences* and *think more deeply* about them. Leonardo's genius was "his *insatiable curiosity*": his study of optics informed the Mona Lisa's smile. Live "bouncing between interests... constantly observing, constantly connecting."
- **Big ideas are usually simple ideas** (Ogilvy). Roger Sterling in *Mad Men*: "I'll tell you what brilliance in advertising is—99 cents. Somebody thought of that."
- **An idea is not an ad.** A creative director might ask copywriters for 20 or 30 ideas. "Recognizing good ideas and turning them into viable ads—is the *true* creative work." Don Draper took Peggy's Glo-Coat **kernel** (a kid locked in while Mom waits for the floor to dry) and gave it "form, narrative, and humanity." AI can generate more kernels faster, but "an idea is just a *kernel*, just this hard, tasteless, inedible thing. It needs heat... It needs a warm body."

## Gathering material

- **The copywriter's lifestyle.** "The preparation for becoming a great copywriter is a *lifestyle*... a hunger for knowledge, a curiosity and desire to participate in life that is broad-based and passionate" (Sugarman). John Carlton: "Don't just walk the dog. Stop and talk to the other dog owner." Go to bars and drink 7-Up, bowling alleys, stores. "Put yourself out of your comfort zone every day, half the day."
- **Free ideas.** Ask strangers random open-ended questions. "They love talking about themselves. And the more I ask, the more they tell." Raymond Carver listened with sympathetic "*He said what?*" noises and asked "How did that make you feel?" Then "he'd be absolutely ruthless about using anything he heard."
- **The second swipe file.** A swipe file is a collection of ads. Eric Betuel keeps a second one in his head, made of people-watching. The daughter is *tired*, the granddaughter *impatient*, the old woman *grateful*. Add to it daily, a scene here, a face there. It is storytelling practice and empathy practice, because "people-watching makes us forget ourselves."
- **The Well** is a folder of hundreds of documents, each holding an idea, a kernel, the beginnings of a piece. "Become excellent at recording your ideas as they come to you": pause the movie, wake up at night, derail the conversation. The Well is *writing insurance*. When you have nothing, it gets you started fast, "And starting is the hardest part."
- **Pick topics by feeling.** Note whatever helps or inspires, let it sit, and if it still moves you when you come back, write: "If I'm moved, I'm writing." Approach the work "like a curious idiot rather than a know-it-all genius" (Mike Monteiro). You don't need to be an expert; write as you learn.
- **Have something to say.** "There are plenty of pretty voices with nothing to say." Style comes with time. When slogging, ask: "Do I have something to say?"

## Generate many, then cut

"Over 95 percent of anyone's ideas are garbage" (Scott Dikkers, *The Onion*). Onion writers each bring 15 to 20 ideas to a meeting, and roughly one in twenty becomes an article. "The fewer ideas you have, the more weight each idea holds in your mind." A writer with one idea links it to their personality and takes critique as a personal slight. **Don't write with your ego.** Start with 20 or more. Three to five gives no slack, and you'll be forced to settle. Pare down. "Eventually, you'll start cutting ideas you actually like. This is the mark of true progress." "Kill your darlings" (Stephen King). "Copywriting is a numbers game. The more bad ideas you have, the more good ones you'll get."

## Incubation

- **Incubation** is "our brain's ability to subconsciously process information and make connections," and its products are "lightbulb moments." Draper: "just think about it deeply. Then forget it. And an idea will jump up in your face." There are unlimited ways to forget it: walk, cook, vacuum, garden, call your mom, look at the clouds, socialize. Or alternate several projects, switching every couple of hours, so each nourishes the others.
- **Not doing your job.** "Letting our creatives be unproductive…until they are" (Draper). "You're often most creative when you're the least productive" (Austin Kleon). Part of a creative's job is not doing the job.
- **Use your unconscious.** "Most great ideas come from the unconscious." Ogilvy never wrote an ad in the office. He wrote at home, at night, after dinner, or while shaving.
- **Let your brain wander.** Leave gray time, nothing time: a walk or a park bench with "Nothing in my ears, no music, no podcast."
- **Embrace accidents.** Seth Rogen's company Houseplant got its oversized matchbooks from a printing error: "we should embrace that."

## Starting and drafting

- **Inspiration is for amateurs** (Chuck Close). "Inspiration is a luxury, not a reliable strategy." Use Schwartz's timer:
  1. Set it for 33 minutes and 33 seconds.
  2. Sit, and don't get up until the alarm goes off. If words come, write. If not, stay put, study the brief and wait.
  3. If the alarm rings on a blank page, walk away for a few minutes and let the brain incubate.
  4. Try again.
- **Writer's block.** "Just start writing. Write anything. Write badly. *Write*." "Action precedes motivation."
- **Write to get ideas, not to express them** (Kevin Kelly). "I don't actually know what I think *until* I try to write it." Ideation and writing are symbiotic.
- **Restrict yourself.** "You can't escape a borderless place." A king lost in a desert dies "scared and free," while the one in the labyrinth gets out. "Limit your time, format, word count, and audience." "The scariest thing in the world (to a copywriter) is a world of possibilities."
- **Write decisively.** Parkinson's law: "Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." Give yourself half the usual time on a countdown timer, treated "like a bomb set to explode at 00:00." You will finish, at least a first draft, because the time crunch forces *simplicity*. You won't complicate, procrastinate or second-guess.
- **Write faster through focus.** *Attention residue* means lingering thoughts from the last activity, and they can take half an hour to clear. **Deep work** (Cal Newport) is a mind free of it. "When you're writing, *write*": close your tabs, close your door, mute your phone and put it out of sight.
- **Write slow on first drafts.** Create bottlenecks between thought and page: "Write longhand. Write with one finger. Write with physical restrictions." Obama writes longhand because word processors give a first draft "too much polish, and more opportunity to make half-baked ideas tidy enough to appear passable."
- **Push, then pull.** "Writing is *pushing*. Editing is *pulling*. You can't do both at once." Be "a much more ruthless editor and a much more careless artist" (Christoph Niemann). Rules:
  1. Don't *stop writing* until it's done.
  2. Don't *start editing* until it's done.
- **When to stop writing.** Aaron Sorkin writes once he has "a beat on the scene," roughly as fast as he can type. If lines come "like honey dripping from a jar," he stops: "You don't know what you're doing yet." More research makes it easier. If connections won't come, you may not be ready. Step away, reread the brief, ask questions and think.
- **Remove needless friction.** Know your *ideal room* (Toni Morrison). Clear the desk the night before, *mis en place*, so the morning starts frictionless. Or stop mid-sentence, as Hemingway did, so you can start immediately the next day. Ryan Holiday: a person who puts up with needless friction "will eventually be worn down."
- **Deadlines.** "If the campaign is on time and doesn't work, we'll get fired. But if it's late and it works, all good." "Take your time. Get it right."

## Creative rest

- **Take creative direction.** "When you're stuck, stop *making* creative decisions. Instead, start *taking* creative direction." Build a model or a LEGO set, or paint by numbers. Trey Parker builds LEGO because "you just sit there and do exactly what *something else* tells you to do." Constant decision-making is the occupational hazard. Following instructions is "a rejuvenating form of creative rest."
- **Mindless, refreshing work.** Keep a folder of simple, compelling images to recreate. "I'm not leading. I'm following." Quality isn't the point; the break from deciding is.
- **The analog-to-digital loop** (Kleon). Keep two desks: analog for craft with the hands, digital for screen work. "When you start to lose steam, head back to your analog station and play." It works against blocks, lethargy, imposter syndrome and boredom. "Rest begets productivity."
- **Time-based work** (Kleon), which Robert Boice calls *radical incrementalism*. Work against a sensible timer, ten minutes to a few hours, and always stop when it ends, even mid-flow. Have no word count: "I just have hours... whatever gets done gets done." Racing to the finish line daily is "impatience masquerading as zeal" and is how creatives burn out. "Stopping helps strengthen the muscle of patience" (Burkeman).
- **Disconnect.** "Creativity is about connection... but it is also about *disconnection*... You must play a little hide-and-seek in order to produce something worth being found" (Kleon).
- **Acquire perspective.** Joyce Carol Oates puts each first draft in a drawer, sometimes for a year or more: "we must always doubt our first burst of excitement and, indeed, anything that comes easily" (Ryan Holiday). Rereading feels different later because "*I've* changed."
- **Slow looking.** "Time illuminates the details. Details give way to connections." Jennifer Roberts has students look at one artwork for three hours. "Allow yourself to decelerate to the speed your work demands."
