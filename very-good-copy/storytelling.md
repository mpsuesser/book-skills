# Storytelling, images and emotion

Read this when copy uses a story, scene, anecdote, vignette or dialogue, or when it needs images and emotion: "stories are the currency of marketing."

## Zoom in on the moment

Making The Reader feel something is central to good copywriting. Be sentimental, "sensitive to life's nuances," and recognize "traces of emotion—of pain and pleasure, gain and loss—in the mundane moments, the ordinary moments, the moments people take for granted." Zoom in close enough and those moments become stories everyone can feel. "Moments are relatable, powerful."

**Inner drama.** Fred Rogers told the U.S. Senate he dealt with "the *inner drama* of childhood": getting a haircut, feelings about brothers and sisters, the anger that arises in simple family situations. That is "much more dramatic than showing something of gunfire." Copywriters express the emotions of something "equally typical and profound." They do it calmly, not loudly, and "in a genuine way, a realistic way," not sensationally, zooming in on "moments that feel big to the person we're writing for."

**Primal stakes hijack attention.** Blake Snyder's *Save The Cat!*: "Give him stakes, real stakes, primal stakes... husbands and wives, fathers and daughters, mothers and sons... You say 'father' and I see *my* father." Show The Reader a spouse, a child, a father or a mother, and she sees *her* people, her life. Now she's listening.

**Sonder** (John Koenig) is "the sudden realization that you're not alone, that we're all the same." Every extra in the background lives "a life as vivid and complex as your own," and those strangers are the people copy speaks to. You don't know them personally, "but your work is about connecting with them individually, personally." To induce sonder before writing, browse street photography (Vivian Maier, Garry Winogrand, Robert Doisneau) as "an emotional warmup."

## Show, don't tell

David Abbott's Chivas Regal Father's Day ad is the model: a list of "Because..." lines ending "Because it's Father's Day. / Because if you don't deserve Chivas Regal, who does?" It is "a masterclass in 'show, don't tell' writing, short on details, which leaves holes for The Reader to fill in with their own memories." Abbott: "I really did have a red Rudge bicycle... I know others who say that it *vividly echoes* their own experience." The *vivid echo* is what makes it great: people see themselves in the writer's world.

| Told | Shown |
|---|---|
| "Because you were silly and fun." | "Because you used to dance in the kitchen with a tea-towel round your waist." |
| "Because you were a great provider." | "Because of all the nights you sat working at your desk while I lay sleeping in my bed." |
| "Because you're proud of me." | "Because I know there's a faded newspaper clipping in your wallet about my scholarship." |
| "Because you're affectionate." | "Because you still hug me when we meet." |
| "Because you're a great husband." | "Because you still buy my mother flowers." |

The result is a **"mental movie,"** which affects The Reader as a good movie would. To convert a told line, ask what the person *did* that proves the trait, and write that act.

**Think of a context.** In *Saving Private Ryan*, Miller explains how to see home: "you don't just think about their faces. You think about something *specific*, something you've done together." For Miller that's "my hammock in the backyard... my wife pruning the rose bushes in a pair of my old work gloves." Good writing creates images, and "Images move people."

## Write in images

"Our brains actually think in images, not words" (Schwartz, *The Brilliance Breakthrough*). It is easier to *decode* a picture into words than to *encode* words into a picture, so images make a message clearer and more memorable. A dentist says "little bits of food are like termites for your teeth." He could have said that lodged food "can cause impaction, which, over time, can lead to tooth decay." The second version is accurate but abstract: "The audience must not work to understand."

**Strong words vs. weak words.** Strong words "put a clear, specific image in The Reader's mind, forcing her to visualize something pleasant or painful, evoking an emotion that affects her thoughts, mood, and... her actions." Weak words are abstract. TRASH and GARBAGE are abstract. LANDFILL shows where the Coke can ends up, and that image changes where you throw it.

**Contrast.** Contrasting things (words, concepts, colors, images) "create drama, tension, energy." *Breaking Bad*'s "Fly" opens on an ultra-close shot of a fly cleaning itself (horrid) over a distant lullaby (soothing): "One plus one equals three." Pair opposites so each highlights the other: long + short, clean + dirty, dark + light, big + small, new + old.

## Realism: the immersion technique

Realism doesn't let The Reader inside a character's mind. Only *facts*, *actions* and *dialogue* are allowed. In reality we can't know what someone is thinking; we judge by what they say and do. Shleyner's blood-draw story "Dry" shows each element:

- Facts: "My mouth is dry." "My palms are still wet, still cold."
- Actions: "I sit beside the water cooler, looking at it, staring at it." "I pump my hand once, twice, three times, four times, five—"
- Dialogue: "Make a fist for me." "Ow."

Realism conveys much with little. It is "working class" writing: efficient, deliberate, no excess, "almost clinical." The lack of detail leaves gaps that The Reader fills automatically from her own life, producing the mental movie.

The rules of realism:

1. Never let The Reader inside the mind of a character. Show only facts, actions and dialogue.
2. Don't give too much information. Over-describing the setting or characters fills in the gaps for The Reader, which is counterproductive.
3. Use the present tense. It isn't always necessary, but it makes events unfold in front of The Reader, live.

With these rules you can "transport The Reader anywhere, into any situation or circumstance, good or bad, painful or pleasant."

**Minimalism.** "Trust your reader... Don't try to describe things. Give a hint, and they will fulfill this hint with their own imagination" (Paulo Coelho). Minimalists favor brevity, avoid adverbs and use cursory descriptions, which gives The Reader an "active role." Hemingway's *Hills Like White Elephants* gives only the *elements* (the woman, the man, the girl, two glasses of beer, two felt pads, white, brown, dry): "He does the *minimum*—and The Reader happily does the rest." Withholding details respects "The Reader's innate ability to fill in the blanks."

**Shock copy** "illustrates the consequences of a problem in a severe way." Shleyner rebuilt a prostate-health hook: at a wedding, the father of the bride's tan pants turn dark, and it's urine. Shock copy jolts The Reader, dominates her attention and creates urgency for your solution. It is best when The Reader is particularly resistant to acting, for example about scheduling an annual prostate exam.

**Visceral copy.** "A 'visceral' reaction is a deep feeling. You don't think, you just emote. It's impossible to ignore." For example: "My bone, white and smooth, takes my breath away."

## Structure

- **Great stories start with conflict.** "'The cat sat on a mat' is not a story. 'The cat sat on *the dog's* mat' is a story" (John le Carré).
- **Conflict and change** (Snyder). Mark each scene with Conflict (><) and Change (+/-). Conflict means Man vs. Man, Man vs. Nature or Man vs. Society, and "only one conflict per scene... Every scene. Every time." Change means the emotional tone shifts drastically, from plus to minus or minus to plus. Each scene is a "mini-movie" with a beginning, middle and end, and "ads can be mini-movies, too."
- **In Medias Res**, "in the middle of things," drops the audience into a problem, a climactic moment, a fight or a revelation involving the main character, "without preamble." It creates tension and drama, and it makes the audience curious. The sooner you do it, the likelier the hook (*The Iliad*, Omaha Beach in *Saving Private Ryan*). For a sales email:
  1. Open with "a clear, concise, vivid depiction of your prospect's biggest problem, something deeply painful."
  2. Promise to make it go away with a credible, accessible solution.
  3. Withhold some information. Don't paint a complete picture.
  4. Ask the prospect to "learn more."
- **The Bourdain Structure**: See stuff › Talk about how it made you feel › Be honest. It is the boiled-down alternative to the Hero's Journey (Departure › Initiation › Return), the 3-Act Structure (Setup › Confrontation › Resolution) and the 5-Act Structure (Exposition › Rising Action › Climax › Falling Action › Dénouement).
- **Open-endedness.** Bourdain on a rough cut that spoon-fed meaning: "They don't need the truth pounded home with meaningless platitudes or bland, generic sum-ups. They'll get it." Embrace ambiguity: "Open-endedness is where all the answers are."
- **The zero-ending.** Raymond Carver ends at the moment a character faces a realization, "on the precipice of change." *Cathedral* closes on "It's really something." The Reader "doesn't merely witness the character's epiphany—she undergoes it," and she carries it afterward.

## Dialogue

"Dialogue is how we get to know people... just the way they speak and what they do and do not say" (Patrick DeWitt). It tells much in few words. George Lois's Coldene cough-syrup ad was a black page with two lines: "John, is that Billy coughing?" "Get up and give him some Coldene." It tells a whole story in twelve words.

Good dialogue sounds like how people talk, so read it aloud. Keep **dialogue tags** invisible: "The dialogue itself should tell the whole story." Two faults make tags too loud:

- *Said-bookisms*: exclaimed, lamented, pronounced.
- *Adverbial tags*: said quietly, said hesitantly, said excitedly.

Use tags only when needed, and then "only use two verbs: *said* or *asked*. No adverbs." Shleyner learned this from Hemingway. Use fewer tags when few people are in the scene. Quotation marks are optional for a writer who knows what she's doing: Sally Rooney, Joyce, Beckett and Doctorow have done without them.

## Connecting in letters and emails

Gary Halbert's two moves for quick intimacy:

1. **Date the copy** like a journal entry, leading with the date and time. It "bonds the writer and The Reader closer together" and gives "the quality of immediacy."
2. **Describe the setting**: "describe where you are and what you are doing as you are writing the letter."

## Poetic devices

A 115-word letter raised $40,000 to replace a church organ, after hundreds of phone calls had raised $50,000 toward an $80,000 goal. It worked because it went to a warm, highly receptive list, it was technically sound, clear, concise and easy to read, and it was nostalgic and poetic. It read like a sermon:

> Nothing gives like a church organ.
> It gives joy at weddings; strength at funerals; family greetings at baptisms.
> It gives wings to worship; power to praise; humility to Thanksgiving.
> It gives rest to the weary; welcome to strangers; binding ties to friends.

- **Nostalgia** ties the product to The Reader's own memories (weddings, funerals, baptisms) and so "dimensionalized its true value... because he made it about The Reader."
- **Anaphora** repeats an opening phrase ("It gives...").
- **The rule of three**: "triplets are easier to understand and remember." Write three bullets, three benefits or three steps, and avoid twos or fours where you can. "Three is the magic number."
- **Repetition** breeds familiarity, and the *mere exposure effect* says people prefer familiar things. An NFL app spot repeated "Free phone football" a dozen times in 30 seconds.
