# Practice and career

Read this when learning or teaching the craft, assigning exercises, coaching a copywriter through doubt, pitching or selling work, choosing projects, or sustaining a career.

## How to learn the craft

Shleyner taught himself by obsessive study. He read, watched, and transcribed and analyzed sales letters and print ads by the masters. Then, for each insight, he challenged himself to explain it in writing: "if I could explain a concept or principle simply and efficiently, I was ready to use it." That is the method behind the book's micro-lessons. "There are no 'big secrets' here either." Everything you need is available in abundance. What's scarce is "the individual drive and determination." In the words of Jiro's son: "It really comes down to making an effort and repeating the same thing every day."

## Exercises

- **Copyworking**, a term coined by Gary Halbert, means transcribing another writer's work by hand. It "helps you internalize the writer's syntax and diction, her punctuation and cadence, her voice." Transcribe proven headlines, subheads and entire sales letters (Shleyner's editor assigned Bencivenga, Halbert and John Caples) by hand, never typed: "If you type it, you'll go too fast." A page a day. It will feel like a waste of time and you'll want to stop; be gritty. Then walk away and rest. The editor's verdict that prompted it: "You write like a fire hose. You need to be a nail gun."
- **Synthesize long into short.** Rewrite a Wikipedia paragraph to a third of its length without sacrificing meaning (~150 → ~50 words). Wikipedia is already concise ("Articles should use only necessary words"), so cutting it demands "hypersensitivity to syntax." Shleyner cut HubSpot's 175-word history to 51 words. Do it once a day, five days a week for a month, and your writing will drastically improve. Do it for a year and it will surpass that of many professional copywriters.
- **Write for one person you love.** Replace morning free-writing with fifteen minutes of writing to someone specific. Shleyner writes emails to his baby son. "Write to please just one person" (Kurt Vonnegut). Copywriters say: "Never write for anyone; always write for *someone*." Writing for someone becomes automatic.
- **Vignettes.** "A vignette is a short passage that captures a moment, a scene." Writing them teaches "how to zoom in on a moment, how to tighten that moment or expand it," and serves the modern reader's demand: "Quick, make me *feel* something." On quantity: the students graded on how many vignettes they submitted "always... produce the finest quality work, too." Shleyner wrote fifty in a semester.
- **Write the picture.** Take any photograph and "write the picture": give it context, animate the subjects, show what they're doing and saying before and after. Use figurative and sensory language, and make us feel its tone and mood.
- **Write the film.** "Novelize" a scene by converting it into writing, transferring imagery and emotion from screen to paper. It is a warmup, and it helps with writer's block.
- **Fill in the blanks.** Invent narratives for anonymous photographs, as in Jason Lazarus's *Too Hard to Keep*, to exercise the imagination and learn to withhold detail.
- **Learn the voice of copy.** Schwartz assigned "all the junk magazines": read *The National Enquirer* "every single word," and see $100 million movies two or three times, "Because *there* is your audience! *There* are your people! *There* are your headlines!" Note the clear, simple language, small words and terse sentences: "*This* is the language of the people. *This* is the voice of copy."
- **Read authors who write like copywriters**: Kurt Vonnegut, Charles Bukowski, Ernest Hemingway, Joyce Carol Oates, Sandra Cisneros, Sally Rooney. They write clear, concise, simple sentences. Developing copywriters should set aside Joyce, Burroughs and Faulkner, whose cryptic styles rub off. Joyce wanted The Reader to *earn* clarity, and "Reading copy must never feel like work." Make reading a reflex: "Carry a book with you at all times" (Ryan Holiday).
- **Copy your heroes.** The Beatles started as a cover band, and Hunter Thompson transcribed classic novels. "Producing derivative work will help you find your own style." Emulate, then gradually tweak toward your own tastes until the work is your own.
- **Daily writing.** "If you write each day, you're a writer. If you write *ads* each day, you're a copywriter." "Just don't lose the muscle." Writing is a skill like an instrument or a sport: be hard on yourself, stay unsatisfied, practice daily. "Improvement begins with I" (Arnold Glasgow).

## Style

- **Minimalist or maximalist.** Salman Rushdie: figure out which you are. Shleyner's practical version: "A maximalist writer edits words *in*." "A minimalist writer edits words *out*." Carver knew a story was finished "when I find myself putting back punctuation marks I'd previously deleted." Pick one. Embracing minimalism (terse narratives, compact sentences, simple language) made Shleyner faster and his voice more distinct. "The only right answer is which is right for you."
- **Style arrives late.** "Most writers find their style eventually and accidentally, not immediately and purposefully." Gilbert Gottfried went on stage "a few billion times" before waking up to find "I seem to be like *that* now." "Style will only get you so far," so have something to say.
- **The process really is whatever works.** Mason Currey's *Daily Rituals* profiles 161 creatives and shows no common pattern. Doing it long enough is how you find *your* process.
- **Trust the feeling.** A creative director's "It makes me vibrate" means trust. Anne Lamott: trust your imagination on a first draft. "Don't look at your feet to see if you are doing it right. Just dance."
- **Write toward risk.** Lamott: "Risk placing real emotion at the center of your work. Write straight into the emotional center of things. Write toward vulnerability. Risk being unliked. Tell the truth as you understand it." Shleyner writes better when he's emotional, and "The Reader... can tell."

## Mindset

- **Creative loneliness** is "the feeling of dread or insecurity leading up to a breakthrough." Trey Parker feels it, and it is normal.
- **Imposter syndrome and self-doubt** hit even Jerry Seinfeld and a successful screenwriter. "A degree of self-doubt is baked into creative work": anticipate the angst, embrace it and convert it into good energy. Always sit back down and try writing again.
- **The odds of creative success are low.** *SNL* writers prepare 40 to 50 sketches a week; about eight make the show, and a quarter of those land. Sugarman: "My failures far outnumbered my successes," and they gave him "a very costly education." "Creative success is a numbers game. Don't quit."
- **Perfection is an illusion.** "It's not about perfection, it's about *resonating* with people" (Katy Perry to a singer who stumbled).
- **Craft gets harder.** Going from zero to 80 percent is easier than 81 to 90, and 91 to 95 is harder still. "96 to 99 percent could be a lifelong pursuit."
- **Patience.** "Your craft will find you."
- **Joy in the process.** "If you do it for a result in the future, you're not doing it" (Alan Watts). If learning the craft makes you happy, "just keep dancing."
- **Compete with the immortals.** Ogilvy: "Be more ambitious... try to hit the ball out of the park every time. Don't bunt."
- **Healthy competition.** "Good competitiveness is predicated on *your success*. Bad competitiveness is predicated on *your opponent's failure*." Share what you know. Ubuntu: "I am because we are." "A solitary human is an oxymoron. A solitary copywriter is, too." "You will get all you want in life if you help enough other people get what *they* want" (Zig Ziglar). Generosity "is the beginning of everything."
- **Prop people up** before they're great, "before they have anything except potential," and especially after failure. "The right words, at the right time, can change someone's trajectory."

## Working with clients

- **Selling work.** "Even a brilliant idea won't sell itself" (George Lois). "When you finish an ad, it's not done. It's not done until the client buys in." Every creative person is a salesperson. Walk the client through the reasoning in named principles. Shleyner removed every exclamation mark from a client's copy, then answered "have you heard of Bill Bernbach?"
- **Passion vs. arrogance.** Stand by your work, but be respectful. "Advertising is a people business." Be nice, considerate and empathetic.
- **Copywriter's law**: "Any idea that can be picked will be picked." Present only options you'd be glad to see chosen.
- **The idea comes first.** Since Bill Bernbach's 1959 creative teams, which paired copywriter and art director in one room and produced VW's *Think Small*, the best ideas come from writers and designers who "think, work, and create *together*" from the start.
- **Fix the game.** Bryan Buckley directed 72 Super Bowl commercials by choosing "the best projects... that have a shot to win the game" and passing on the stinkers. In the beginning you'll take what comes. Once you have a selection, *be selective*: "Choose projects with a clear path to success."
- **Time, money, and reputation.** "Any time I'm telling myself, '*But I'm making so much money*,' that's a warning sign that I'm doing the wrong thing. Money can always be regenerated. Time and reputation cannot" (B.J. Novak).
- **Be consistent and generous in public.** After a rejection, Shleyner committed to a weekly newsletter, "consistently creating, sharing, and being helpful." Recognition, audience and money will come. In the meantime, "be observant, make connections, and write consistently."
- **Balance.** Brian Dyson's five balls are Work, Family, Health, Friends and Spirit. Work is rubber and bounces back. The others are glass: "irrevocably scuffed, marked, nicked, damaged, even shattered." Creative work "can engulf you, if you let it," so guard the glass balls.
