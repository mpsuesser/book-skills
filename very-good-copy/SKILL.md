# Very Good Copy

Eddie Shleyner's book is 207 micro-lessons in two halves: thinking like a copywriter (mindset: creativity, research, ideation, influence, success and failure) and writing like one (execution). The execution half has one aim: write "clearly, concisely, vividly, and with emotion, the cornerstone of persuasion."

The book calls the person the copy is for **The Reader**, capitalized, and so does this skill.

## The copywriter's stance

Every judgment in the book comes from these principles. Use their names when you explain a choice.

- **Clean windows.** Eugene Schwartz: "the words in advertising are like the windows in a store. You must be able to look right through them and see the product. If you see the window, it's dirty." When someone admires the words themselves, "you're seeing the words; you're not seeing *through* the words." Conversion copy calls attention to the prospect and her problems, or to the product, "the pleasure it produces or the pain it prevents," never to itself. Be clear, not clever, especially in direct-response copy.
- **Ads vs. art.** "Great art starts with *the maker*. Great ads start with *the market*." Art is self-indulgent: you can't make art *with* someone else in mind. Copywriting is the opposite: you can't make an ad *without* someone else in mind. Copywriters are hired to solve business problems. As Don Draper puts it in *Mad Men*: "You're not an artist, Peggy. You solve problems."
- **Sailing.** "Copywriters don't create demand. We channel *existing* demand." Ramming a product down a market's throat because you think it's great never works. Get to know the market's needs, wants, dreams and desires, then write ads that speak to them. "A gifted product is mightier than a gifted pen" (Gary Bencivenga). If the market doesn't want the product, even the best copywriter can't help.
- **Copyresearching.** "'Copywriting' is a misnomer. 'Copy*researching*' is more accurate." "The power of the ad is always in the product itself. It is *not* in the copywriter. The copywriter simply finds it and expresses it" (Schwartz). Sloppy copywriters focus on writing; careful copywriters prioritize research. Put a careless great copywriter against a careful "copy cub," and two times out of three the careful one wins.
- **Connection is the true work.** A better word for creativity is "connectivity": "trying to connect two separate ideas that logically would not go together up until that moment." "Copywriters are not paid to write." The job, above all else, "is to think, to connect things."
- **Moments.** "Copywriting is all about moments, these small slices of life." To put a huge concept across efficiently, *zoom in* on one specific moment tied to it. Shleyner explains love with the moment he rolls onto his wife's side of the bed and hugs her pillow, which smells of her shampoo. "Zoom in on the moment, and it will speak for itself." Great copy "boils down to *illustration*: how well can you *show* the prospect a moment in time?" Generate a feeling, then connect that feeling to the product.
- **Sell a feeling.** "In the factory, we make cosmetics. In the store, we sell hope" (Charles Revson). The $53 at Home Depot bought the moment of his wife smiling at him through the window, not the flowers and dirt. Revson made *people*, not products, the focal point of his ads. Advertising "should almost always work to sell a feeling."
- **Truth.** "The most powerful element in advertising is *truth*" (Bill Bernbach). "A great ad is a mirror you put in front of your audience" (Jeff Goodby). "got milk?" shows a craving that happens countless times a day, and it ran for almost 20 years. The boy who turns into a bodybuilder after a sip of milk shows something that never happens. To make The Reader feel something, write what she perceives as absolutely accurate. Ads make promises, and promises bring people hope: "Don't mess with a person's hope."
- **The most personal is the most creative.** Copy resonates when it comes from a *singular* point of view: "the details that are unique to you but understood by all." "Use your life to animate your copy. If something moves you, it will touch someone else, too" (David Abbott). AI can source ideas, organize them and "establish the dots," but it can't connect them or be personal. Shleyner shows this with an 88-word AI description of meeting a firstborn child: "a truly magical experience," "a wave of love and warmth," "an unforgettable moment that I will cherish forever." Generic sentiment like that is what to replace. So when you write for a user, draw out their real moments, details and customer stories, and build on those.

## Applying the book to a copy job

Run the steps in order. Each one ends on its completion criterion.

1. **Diagnose before writing.**
   - *Is the creative the problem?* Under **the 40/40/20 rule**, a direct-response effort's success depends 40% on the list, 40% on the offer and 20% on the creative. When response is poor, check the list and the offer before blaming the copy: "nine out of ten times, it had nothing to do with the creative." For the full breakdown, read [campaigns-and-positioning.md](campaigns-and-positioning.md).
   - *Which type of ad?* **Direct-response** ("conversion") ads compel *immediate* action, for example with urgency and scarcity. **Creative** ("general") ads are memorable: they live in the subconscious until a buying opportunity brings back the emotion. All advertising is made to compel. The difference is *when* the action takes place.
   - *Where are the prospects?* Schwartz: you can't write an ad until you've identified the market's **Level of Awareness**, meaning what prospects already know about the problem the product solves:
     1. *Unaware*: they don't realize they have a problem.
     2. *Problem Aware*: they know they have a problem but don't fully understand it.
     3. *Solution Aware*: they understand the problem and know of similar solutions, but have never heard of this product.
     4. *Product Aware*: they know the product but haven't bought. They're still researching and weighing options.
     5. *Most Aware*: they are almost customers.

     "Advertising must meet people where they are. It must tell them something *relevant*."

   Done when you can name the market segment, the offer, the ad type and the awareness level. Ask the user for whatever is missing.

2. **Research like a detective.**
   - Know the product as well as its maker, or better. Schwartz read each book he advertised cover to cover, highlighted every *claim* (promise) and built the letter from the most valuable ones. When a doctor-author struck the word "eggs" from a subhead as false, Schwartz quoted the line from the doctor's own book that supported it: "I want to know the book as well as or *even better* than the author!" Ogilvy: "The more you know about the product, the more likely you are to sell it. Study the precedents."
   - Do **sales detective work** (John Carlton). "You're not gonna find your story in the company line." The brochure won't have it either. The hook is hiding with the secretaries, the salespeople and "the guy who invented the product." Carlton found the hook for a golf-swing video by asking its inventor where the swing came from: a golfer who drove the ball on one leg. Ask questions, then listen.
   - Listen for the market's own words. Schwartz listened to a client for hours: "About 70 percent of the copy was the client's own words." "And the headline was a direct quote!" In his words: "You don't need to have great ideas if you can *hear* great ideas."
   - Ask Kim Krause Schwalm's research questions. What are the market's biggest pain points, frustrations and hopes? What alternatives have they seen? What hasn't worked for them? Why will *this* work now? Why should they believe that?
   - Study a **person, not a persona**. "A persona is a Frankenstein" (Sean D'Souza). One real prospect's hopes, needs and desires are the most honest and accurate source.

   Done when you hold the product's claims, the market's pains and hopes in its own words, and at least one hook. A hook is the curious, specific thing that creates *interest*, the I in AIDA (Attention › Interest › Desire › Action).

3. **Find the angle.**
   - **Dimensionalize** each benefit: find "the benefit *of a benefit*, again and again." Ask *"So what?"* until you reach the **core benefit**, "the thing your prospect truly desires, consciously or otherwise." Example: an extra-large Escher print (feature: 65" x 76"). It becomes the room's focal point, so it commands guests' attention, so it becomes a conversation piece. So you speak about it as an authority, so people are impressed by your knowledge and sophistication. A flat benefit about interior design has become a core benefit about the prospect's ego.
   - The bottom of the benefit rabbit hole is **the Life-Force 8**, the eight desires that Drew Eric Whitman says humans are "biologically programmed" with:
     1. Survival, enjoyment of life, life extension.
     2. Enjoyment of food and beverages.
     3. Freedom from fear, pain, and danger.
     4. Physical companionship.
     5. Comfortable living conditions.
     6. To be superior, winning, keeping up with the Joneses.
     7. Care and protection of loved ones.
     8. Social approval.

     "When in doubt, align your benefits with the desires we're all born with."
   - **Pain vs. pleasure**: given a choice between avoiding pain and gaining pleasure, people almost always choose the former. "We jog to pleasure, but we sprint from pain."
   - **Say it first.** "When you say something first, you own it." A preemptive claim about an industry standard (for example "Every Bottle Sterilized" for Schlitz) ties the standard to *your* brand, especially for a commodity.
   - **Reflect a mood** instead of creating one. Phyllis Robinson didn't invent the "Me Generation" for Clairol's *It Lets Me Be Me*: "It was already there—and I just recognized it." Sometimes putting what's already in the zeitgeist into words is all a copywriter needs to do.
   - Run **the cross-out test**:
     1. Write your copy.
     2. Cross out any references to your company and its product.
     3. Write in the name of your competitor and their product.

     "If the copy still fits, you have a problem—and some editing to do." Find points the competition cannot say about their products.

   Done when the angle reaches a Life-Force 8 desire and passes the cross-out test.

4. **Choose the structure and length.**
   - **Problem-Agitation-Solution (PAS)** works for short and long copy alike. Expand or contract it to fit the space.
     1. Introduce a **problem**: a question or statement that identifies The Reader's issue.
     2. **Agitate** it: tell a relevant anecdote, a story The Reader recognizes, that shows the consequences.
     3. Offer a **solution**: give The Reader an out, a clear and preferably easy way to avoid the issue.
   - **Bob Stone's Gem** is the seven-step outline behind a Kiplinger mailing that went unbeaten for more than 35 years. Use it for letters, emails, articles, pitch decks, anything meant to persuade.
     1. **Benefit**: begin with your strongest benefit, usually in the headline.
     2. **Expansion**: expand on it, for example by *future-pacing*: show its impact on The Reader's life tomorrow, next week, a year from now ("will" triggers this).
     3. **Positive**: explain exactly what the prospect will get and why to act now, and de-risk the decision with a guarantee.
     4. **Proof**: prove the value with past experience, using facts, statistics and testimonials.
     5. **Negative**: explain the consequences of inaction.
     6. **Summary**: sum up the benefits.
     7. **Action**: ask for action, saying clearly and specifically what to do, and remind her she has nothing to lose.
   - Open a story or sales email **In Medias Res**, in the middle of the prospect's biggest problem. Plot each scene for conflict and change. See [storytelling.md](storytelling.md).
   - **Harmonize**: "Agreement begets agreement." Questions that tee up yeses create positive inertia. Schwartz: "If your first thought holds him, he will read the second..." Every line has one job, to get the next line read.
   - **Length**: "If your copy is fascinating, the length is irrelevant." Long copy sells more than short copy (Ogilvy) because it is *informative* (it answers questions and assuages concerns) and *transportive*: "Good long copy lets us fantasize." A prospect interested in the product "will happily read thousands of words" about its impact on her life. "Our attention spans aren't necessarily getting shorter." Short copy is enough when you're selling something she already wants, especially a commodity: "Sometimes the best copy to sell a horse is 'HORSE FOR SALE'" (Jay Abraham). No copy is best when the art tells the story: "Copywriting is more about *ideas* than it is about writing."

   Done when you have an outline whose every section serves the angle.

5. **Write the draft.** Write now, carelessly. Edit later, ruthlessly. Don't stop writing until the draft is done, and don't start editing until then. For the headline, read [headlines.md](headlines.md). For scenes, images, dialogue, emotion and story, read [storytelling.md](storytelling.md). For claims, proof, guarantees, credibility and the psychology of response, read [persuasion.md](persuasion.md). Done when a complete first draft exists.

6. **Edit.** Read [editing.md](editing.md) and run its passes on every draft before you deliver it. Done when every sentence has passed the three questions of "short, but thorough" and the line checks.

7. **Test.** Every ad is "an *informed* guess," and direct marketing "is very counterintuitive" (Joe Sugarman). "Failing often and testing big differences shows you are trying hard enough" (Schwartz). Propose a **big split** first: two unique *angles*, such as pain vs. pleasure. Once it finds a winner, refine with **small splits** that test one *variable*, such as the headline. The winner becomes the **control**, and the conversion copywriter's one job is to *beat the control*. Before launch, gather mass critique with the **CRIT system**. See [campaigns-and-positioning.md](campaigns-and-positioning.md). Done when you have delivered the copy with at least one big-split alternative angle and the reasoning behind each, named in the book's terms.
