# Editing

Run these passes on every draft before delivering it. "Fetishize brevity… …even when you have space."

## Short, but thorough

Short writing usually isn't thorough, and thorough writing usually isn't short. Shleyner's process marries the two:

1. Commit to a final word count, say 318 words.
2. Brain-dump a 4X first draft (1,272 words).
3. Cut the first draft in half (636 words).
4. Cut the second draft in half (318 words).

Take it a sentence at a time and ask three questions:

- **"Does this edit express the same idea, but faster?"** If you can say the same thing in fewer words, you've made the writing better. Nietzsche's ambition: "to say in ten sentences what others say in a whole book."
- **"Is this word absolutely necessary?"** Every syllable must make your point, create imagery or conjure emotion. "Anything else is fat. Trim it as though you're being paid a day's wage per word." "Writing is 1 percent inspiration and 99 percent elimination" (Louise Brooks). Be draconian; your severity will be rewarded.
- **"Do I *really* like this?"** You are the work's first reader. Trust yourself when something sounds strange or off. "If it doesn't move *you*, chances are it won't move someone else. You must affect yourself first." "No tears in the writer, no tears in the reader. No surprise in the writer, no surprise in the reader" (Robert Frost).

For practice, rewrite a Wikipedia paragraph to a third of its length without losing meaning (see [practice-and-career.md](practice-and-career.md)).

## Line checks

- **Clean windows.** Find anything that makes The Reader see the words instead of the product, and cut it.
- **Simple words.** "A big word will never impress The Reader as much as a *big idea*, clearly expressed. Good copy, first and foremost, is understood." Reading copy must feel easy, even effortless: "If you confuse them, you'll lose them" (Donald Miller). "Simplifying your language doesn't simplify your message": accessible writing can still be profound. Paul Graham: "The less energy they expend on your prose, the more they'll have left for your ideas." "Kill all gobbledygook and flab."
- **Strong verbs instead of adverbs.** "He carefully held the baby" → "He *cradled* the baby." "She quickly ran" → "She *sprinted*." "They cared for each other dearly" → "They *loved* each other."
- **Well-placed adjectives.** "Adjectives are not a writing sin. Only *misplaced* adjectives are a sin." Zinsser: most adjectives are unnecessary because "the concept is already in the noun." But "a well-placed adjective speaks volumes." Justin Schmidt rates a paper wasp's sting "Burning, throbbing, and *lonely*." The unexpected "lonely" makes the sting understood emotionally. Keep an adjective that adds what the noun can't.
- **No redundancies.** "Concise writing doesn't repeat itself." "She nodded her head in agreement" → "She nodded."
- **Active voice.** "The car was driven by Jim" → "Jim drove the car." Passive voice is grammatical but wordier, harder to read and awkward. Active voice is concise, clear and confident.
- **Relaxed tone.** "Write the way you talk, *naturally*" (Ogilvy). Use active voice, contractions ("you will" → "you'll"), abbreviations ("Television" → "TV") and colloquialisms ("going to" → "gonna"). Relaxed writing makes reading easier.
- **Idioms are allowed.** Gary Halbert, one of the most *original* direct-response minds, used *unoriginal* idioms constantly: "easy as pie," "the real McCoy," "open-and-shut case," "blood is thicker than water." They make copy conversational, clear and comprehensible. "We are not in the business of being original. We are in the business of reusing things that work" (Bob Bly).
- **Periods, not exclamation marks.** Remove them, or use them very sparingly (see [persuasion.md](persuasion.md)).
- **Specifics over generalities.** Use exact numbers, concrete images and strong words (see [headlines.md](headlines.md) and [storytelling.md](storytelling.md)).
- **Punctuation by feeling.** Place commas where you want The Reader to pause: "Copywriting isn't perfect grammar... It's a *feeling*."

## Layout

- **Empty space.** Double-tap "enter" every sentence or two or three. White space creates emphasis the way negative space does in photography and design. It makes copy look less intimidating and more readable. "It invites The Reader in."
- **Drop-caps.** Ogilvy: "When you start your body copy with a drop-cap, you increase readership thirteen percent."
- **Image captions** are the second-most-read copy after headlines, so an uncaptioned image wastes valuable real estate. On Shleyner's site, more people clicked the small gray text in image captions than the big green button at the end of each piece. Put conversion copy in captions: a good offer, easy to act on.
- **Design serves reading.** "If the copywriter's job is to connect with The Reader, then the designer's work is all about getting the copy read." It is not about "a cool look or an award-winning design."

## Distance and stopping

- **Distance.** "Never send a letter or a memo on the day you write it. Read it aloud the next morning, and then edit it" (Ogilvy). The words stay the same overnight; your perspective changes.
- **You're too close.** After many rounds of reading and editing, words can suddenly sound off. That usually doesn't mean the copy was bad all along. "The sentences have carved a path through your brain... You are close, but The Reader isn't." Get distance or another reader before you rewrite.
- **Rewrite.** "The only kind of writing is rewriting" (Hemingway). *Write It Again!* "Your first draft will almost always be your worst draft."
- **When to stop editing.** "A poem is never finished, it is only abandoned" (Paul Valéry). Endless tinkering goes "down the staircase of perfection" until you lose interest. Jerry Jenkins: make sure "you're not simply making it different, you're making it *better*. Once you've hit that point, you stop editing and move on."
