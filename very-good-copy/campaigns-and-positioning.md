# Campaigns, testing and positioning

Read this when planning or diagnosing a campaign, promotion or offer; choosing a list or audience; setting price or incentives; designing tests; gathering critique; or positioning a product or brand.

## The 40/40/20 Rule of Direct Marketing

Some people think the creative makes or breaks a campaign. It doesn't. "Over a century of conventional wisdom" holds that a direct-response effort's success depends:

- **40%** on the **LIST**,
- **40%** on the **OFFER**,
- **20%** on the **CREATIVE**.

With an excellent list and offer but abysmal creative, you still have roughly an 80 percent chance of success. With an excellent list and creative but an abysmal offer, roughly 60 percent. Use the rule to *project* a promotion's success beforehand, or to *evaluate* it after the fact. Score each element and fix the weakest 40 first.

### List (40%)

The list is the people who see your message. It should be a *segment* of the market, not the whole market. A strong list is made of people who:

1. have bought products similar to what you're selling, and
2. have bought them via similar ways of buying.

Take a prostate supplement sold through Facebook Ads. The market is all men over 50. The list is men over 50 who have bought supplements *in the past*, *via Facebook*. A strong list has a *proven desire*. A weak list is "blindly compiled based on surface-level characteristics, like the age, sex, and location of the prospects." Kim Krause Schwalm: "You risk so much less of your marketing budget when you're spending it on people who've already bought your product or who have bought similar products."

### Offer (40%)

The offer combines *desire*, *price* and *incentives*. Ask one question of each:

- **Desire**: "is this product even something people want?" Schwalm wrote more than 20 successful promotions for one supplement client. The one flop was an untested "Eastern" formula with an angle that was maybe "old news." It wasn't the copy, which her mentor revised without saving it, and it wasn't the list, which was known buyers. "Sometimes, no matter how good the copy is, no matter how good the list is—it's a product people just don't want."
- **Price**: "is this product priced appropriately and competitively for what it is?" This is positioning. In direct marketing a product is usually either the *economical* option or the *premium* option. A premium price can only be commanded through differentiation: "some kind of unique ingredient or delivery system or proof—an endorsement, perhaps." An economical product typically has to compete on price with incentives.
- **Incentives**: "is this ad giving people a reason to buy now?" Incentives are imperative for any direct-response promotion. Use the three levers in [persuasion.md](persuasion.md): dimensionalize the savings, create urgency, remove the risk of ordering.

### Creative (20%)

The creative is *copy*, *design* and *format*.

- **Copy** is the most important of the three, and "so much of that is the research you put into it." The research questions are in SKILL.md step 2.
- **Design**: copywriter and designer should work together from the start. "Direct marketing is, first and foremost, about getting the copy read. It's the only chance you have at getting the prospect to respond."
- **Format** is how copy and design are arranged and presented. In direct mail, for example, a "magalog" (a 16-page, 8½" x 11" ad) can become a "bookalog" (a 32-page, 5½" x 8¼" ad). Test formats for response and for cost: printing and postage.

"Advertising is rarely one-size-fits-all," which is why testing early and often matters.

## Testing

- **Every ad is a guess**, but an *informed* one, backed by research, best practices and the principles of persuasion and psychology. "Making these informed guesses is half of marketing. The other half is testing them, measuring the outcome, and adjusting." The angle you're sure of may flop, and the one you doubt may pull best.
- **Split tests** pit one version against another and record the response rate. The winner stays on as the **control**, and the sitting control is split against each new version, sometimes for years.
  - A **small split** tests one *variable*, such as the headline.
  - A **big split** pits two unique *angles*, such as pain vs. pleasure, positioning the product or offer "in a patently different way."

  Most breakthroughs come from big splits. Start with a big split, a "big difference" as Schwartz calls it. When you find a winner, refine it with small splits.
- **Fail often.** Sanrio's creative department invented over four hundred characters before Hello Kitty. "Quality almost always comes from quantity," so expect most ideas to dead-end. "Creative success is a numbers game." "Marketing isn't medicine. It's not life or death. It's okay to get it wrong."
- **Study the long-running controls.** Some ads persevere for decades, like Bob Stone's Kiplinger letter, unbeaten for 35+ years, and *32 Ways* for Good Housekeeping, unbeaten for almost 20. "These are the ads you want to read, study, transcribe, and emulate."
- **Patience.** *The National Enquirer* took years of tinkering with content, layout and distribution, through trial and error, testing and measuring, to become the most-read newspaper in America.

### The CRIT system (Gary Bencivenga)

CRIT is short for *critique*, not an acronym. It is the feedback method behind Bencivenga's agency guarantee: "guarantees to beat your best ad by at least 10 percent, or you pay us nothing." It is slow and painful ("odious," "sadistic," "devastating," "weird," "cumbersome"), but the result is a campaign a client "could run for years."

1. Distribute the copy to everyone who will read it. In Bencivenga's agency that meant the receptionist, the account executives, the art director and anyone else. Today it can mean social media, or a testing platform for a targeted audience.
2. Ask them to "rip the copy apart" and "point out holes in the argument." They should flag grammar and spelling, psychological follies, readability, word choice, rough transitions, anything strange. Encourage terse, biting notes: "I don't believe this claim!" "Get to the point…" "I'm confused." "So boring…" "But why?"
3. Collect *volume*: dozens, even hundreds of notes. "Volume, ultimately, is the secret," because volume shows overlap, patterns and themes.
4. Act on consensus, not on individual opinions. One of 12 people confused by the headline is an outlier. Eleven of 12 is a consensus, and it tells you what to rewrite.

## Positioning and branding

- **Brand positioning boils down to differentiation.** One way to differentiate is to align with a unique set of *values*. AND1 aligned with streetball's attitude and creativity against Nike's elitism and rigidity. That forced people to *choose*, pro or street, and they did.
- **Brand = Adjective.** "A brand is the most valuable piece of real estate in the world. It's a corner of someone's mind" (John Hegarty). The antidote to clutter is "draconian simplicity": strip the value proposition "down to the bone and then again to the marrow" (Luke Sullivan, *Hey Whipple, Squeeze This*). Jeeps are tough, Porsches are fast, Volvos are safe. Ask: what's your brand's adjective?
- **Preemptive claims.** "When you say something first, you own it." Claude Hopkins wrote "Every Bottle Sterilized" and "Bottled Purity" for Schlitz. Every brewer sterilized and filtered, but Schlitz said it first. That created a positive association and sowed uncertainty about the competition. In *Mad Men*, Don Draper's "New York, Brought To You By Bethlehem Steel" works the same way for a commodity with no functional advantage. The idea is "so basic, you feel like you already know it. You just haven't thought of it lately." When a client says a process is "an industry standard," ask them to walk through it step by step, then write it: "you'll be the one saying it *first*."
- **Stand out from the category's convention.** When 1960s beauty brands all went full color, Estée Lauder ran sepia. Competitors called it "ugly" advertising, and it pulled about 30 percent more sales. "The ugly thing in a world of beauty stands out" (Schwartz).
- **Reframe the category.** Red Bull's Thai original, Krating Daeng, was a *tool*, about effect and not taste, and European focus groups called the drink "Vile!" Dietrich Mateschitz changed the name, the packaging (a short, thin can that stood out on the shelf), the tagline ("Red Bull gives you wiiings" made a promise) and the price (more than double the cost of soda, positioning it as an expensive, functional alternative). He kept only the logo, and sponsored The Dolomitenmann, "the world's toughest team race." The message: "Red Bull isn't just a drink, it's a *tool*."
- **Follow the market.** Biggie had every show's audience taped to see which songs got the strongest reaction: "our shit was always *the crowd*." Marketers work this way too.
- **Swipe files date.** "Successful campaigns target a specific audience with a specific message during a specific moment in time." When the market moves, the message must move too. A swipe file is "a repository of *once-well-timed* approaches and angles," for learning and inspiration, "not a copy catalog to casually pluck from." "There are no silver bullets in copywriting."
