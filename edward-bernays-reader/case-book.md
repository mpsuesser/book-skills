# Case book

Bernays's campaigns, grouped by the technique each one demonstrates. Each case gives the problem, the move and the result, with the piece and year that tell it. Model a new campaign on the closest precedent: take its move and refit it with your public's leaders, symbols and channels.

## Created circumstance: juxtapose authorities at one time and one place

- **NAACP convention, Atlanta, 1920** (Manipulating Public Opinion, 1928). The goal was to change national attitudes on lynching and Jim Crow.
  1. Dramatize the year's campaign in an annual convention, which centers attention "at one time and at one place" on the ideas and the men behind them.
  2. Hold it in the South, because "a point of view on a southern question, emanating from a southern center, would have a greater force of authority", especially when it runs against the traditional Southern view.
  3. Surround the conference with "people who were stereotypes for ideas that carried weight". Bernays solicited views by telegram and letter from national religious, political, social and educational leaders, and above all from Southern ones: a group of Atlanta ministers already on record for interracial amity.
  4. Put on a program with Black and white Southerners on one platform saying the same thing. Add dramatic touches, such as a descendant of a Massachusetts Abolitionist agreeing with a Southern Baptist preacher.
  5. Extend the effect by mailing reports and documents to selected groups.

  The result: Southern editors came to see the question as "not an emotional one, but a discussable one."
- **Millinery against the felt cloche** (1928). Bernays classified the hats into six groups and tabulated the four classes that set women's hat habits: the society leader ("the fountainhead of style"), the style expert, the artist and the beautiful women who wear the hats. The task was "to bring into juxtaposition all of these groups ... at one time and at one place, before an audience of those most concerned, the buyers of hats." An artists' committee chose six beautiful girls to wear six hats in six tableaux at a fashion fete. A committee of distinguished women backed it out of interest in an American industry, and fashion editors backed it for its style value. The story went out through news services, store advertising, broadsides and rotogravure. "In ten days the industry was humming." One maker had sold no large-trimmed hats before the show and sold thousands after it.
- **The panatrope** (1928). Bernays had to launch an electrical phonograph to a public "much more interested in football scores and Lindbergh." He formed a sponsoring committee: patrons of music (Mrs. Vincent Astor, Otto Kahn), so that music lovers would accept it; scientists, so that it would read as scientifically correct; and a Metropolitan Opera tenor for "the stereotype of the Metropolitan Opera House". He gathered them at one dramatic event in a symbolic place, Aeolian Hall, and the instrument was accepted at once.
- **Light's Golden Jubilee, October 21, 1929** (Emergence of the Public Relations Counsel, 1971). For General Electric and Westinghouse, Bernays made the fiftieth anniversary of Edison's lamp a worldwide celebration of what electric light did for society. It culminated at Henry Ford's Edison Institute with President Hoover and Madame Curie present. The Post Office issued a light-bulb stamp on its own initiative because the Jubilee's visibility was so high. A critic called it "one of the most lavish pieces of propaganda ever engineered in this country during peace time". *The New Yorker* named Bernays a "specialist in making news events." The sponsorship was never hidden.

## Attack at the source: work through the channels that already carry authority

- **Velvet** (1928, also told in The New Propaganda). Analysis showed a velvet fashion could not be started in America, because fashion came from Paris and silk from Lyons. "The attack had to be made at the source." The aim was "to substitute purpose for chance." The manufacturers openly backed a velvet fashion service. A Parisian agent induced the couturiers (Lanvin, Worth, Agnes, Patou) to use velvet and arranged for countesses and duchesses to wear it. American buyers and editors met velvet in the ateliers, "the actual (though created) circumstance", and reported it. One department store advertised velvet on the couturiers' authority and quoted their cables, and hundreds of stores echoed it. "Fickle fashion had veered to velvet."
- **American silk at the Louvre** (1928; told in 1971 as the Luxembourg Museum and Cheney Brothers). American women would not credit American silks until Paris approved them. Exhibiting them in the Louvre, "because that stands for the idea of accredited beauty in the American mind", with the American ambassador opening the show, settled it: "It must be good, therefore!" The index of success was that American cities "vied with each other for the honor of exhibiting what the Louvre had shown." The campaign launched "art in industry."
- **Men's fashions** (1971). "The principle of group leadership dominates men's fashions, as it does women's." Bernays coordinated new lines with style leaders in London and on far-western campuses to cut launch risk.

## Overt act against a rumor or a typecast image

- **Coolidge "weaned on a pickle"** (1928, 1971). To humanize a frigid image, Bernays brought "the most human groups" into juxtaposition with the president: Al Jolson, the Dolly Sisters and other actors at a White House breakfast. "The country felt that a man in the White House who could laugh with Al Jolson and the Dolly sisters was not frigid and unsympathetic."
- **Beech-Nut** (1971). The counsel ranged from top-level policy to "counteracting false rumors."

## Making a cause of the project

- ***Damaged Goods*, 1913** (1923, 1971). A play about sex education was at risk from the vice crusader Anthony Comstock. Bernays lifted the part of opinion that believed in education and truth away from the part that condemned all mention of sex. Educational groups and their leaders sponsored the play through the Sociological Fund of the *Medical Review of Reviews*, whose members paid four dollars to attend a performance. It "made it strike sex o'clock in America."
- **Hairnets** (1971). Short hair was killing hairnet sales. Bernays promoted hairnets as a safety measure for women working at machines, and several states made them compulsory under certain working conditions. "The segmental approach was developed by stressing the sanitary aspect of hairnets for cooks and waitresses."
- **Soap sculpture** (1928, 1971). Bernays tapped "the desire for beauty of a strong minority" and made soap a sculpture medium for children and a school aid. An annual contest was held in a leading art gallery. It "helped develop a generation that enjoyed cleanliness."
- **Dixie cups** (1971). The campaign killed the communal drinking cup, "a constructive example of the coincidence of public and private interest."

## Change the surroundings, not the product

- **Green for Lucky Strike** (1971). Research found women shunned the green pack because it clashed with their costumes. The client refused to change the pack ("Change the color of the package," Bernays had suggested), so the campaign made green fashion's color instead, for $25,000. A charity Green Ball was held for the New York Infirmary for Women, with tableaux of socialites in green. Glove, stocking and shoe makers were lined up to match, and *Harper's Bazaar* and *Vogue* ran green covers on the date. "Green became fashion's color."
- **Bookshelves** (1971). Bernays urged contractors and architects to build bookshelves into houses and apartments: "Empty bookshelves induced book purchases."
- **Greeting cards and sporting goods** (1971). Social scientists defined the function of greeting cards: they gratify the need for gregariousness and boost morale. The cards became more socially acceptable once the makers used more sophisticated art and sentiment. Spalding was advised to stress that sport strengthens character and physical fitness.
- **Bacon** (1971). A survey of physicians urging heavier breakfasts for health came, for many, to mean bacon and eggs.

## Change the policy first, then tell the public

- **Vegetable shortening** (1928). Research found that Orthodox Jews would not buy the product. The maker altered it to meet the dietary laws, then had religious leaders, dietary officials and hospitals known for strict observance approve it.
- **Brewers after Repeal** (1971). Bernays drafted a public-policy platform: the brewers would support the authorities in preventing the return of the saloon.
- **Procter and Gamble** (1971). Studies of community folkways preceded new plants. The campaign also covered guaranteed employment, company participation in community life, the hiring of Black workers and a department of public relations inside the company.
- **General Motors** (1971). Bernays helped get Henry Weaver's consumer-preference studies before Alfred Sloan, which started GM's consumer research, a pattern for industry. He also placed a paragraph on public relations in the 1932 annual report.

## Changing a cliche or a self-image

- **"Evacuation post"** (1928). Renaming the "evacuation hospital" reset expectations to emergency treatment only.
- **Art in industry, 1925** (1928). Bernays was associate commissioner for a commission Secretary Hoover sent to the Paris exposition, with about 150 delegates from different industries. "Industry itself became conscious of the new cliche of themselves."

## Articulating a latent idea, removing a prejudice

- **Ripley and corporate reports** (1928). Ripley "articulated an idea that was latent in the minds of a large majority". The aroused public then forced the New York Stock Exchange to act.
- **Margarine** (1928). The prejudice "remained long after its cause had been altered". The campaign worked through correspondence with leaders in medicine, hygiene and dietetics and published their findings.
- **Chilean nitrates** (1971). Bernays fought the superstition among Southern farmers that only bird-made nitrates from Chile worked, to win acceptance for American-made nitrates.
- **Branch banking** (1971). Bank of America's program for public acceptance of an unpopular practice was still in use four decades later and became a model for other banks.

## Humor, novelty and drama for attention

- **Salad dressing** (1928, 1971). An art-gallery show paired "palette oils and palate oils": salads dedicated to famous artists, displayed under still lifes of salads. "An idea may strike the fancy and arrest the attention of hundreds of thousands of people."
- **Dodge Victory Six** (1971). Bernays staged the first nationwide broadcast for a model launch, with Jolson and Will Rogers. Showrooms bulged the next morning.
- **Oriental pearls** (1971). Against cultured pearls, a sheik from Bahrein toured "to dramatize and validate our cause."
- **Hampshire House** (1971). The cornerstone inscription stressed "yesterday's charm and tomorrow's convenience."

## Nations and causes

- **Lithuania** (1928, 1923, 1971). Bernays made 110,000,000 Americans see "a small country on the Baltic" as more than a spot on the map. It was presented through its drama, music, literature, habits, economics and agriculture, with pamphlets, parades, pictures and films, and with "events created to symbolize facts and ideas". The result was economic aid and political recognition.
- **Veterans' reemployment, 1919** (1971). The campaign was run for the War Department, at a time when no G.I. Bill existed.
- **The arms traffic** (1971). Bernays sent proofs of a *Fortune* article on arms makers to Senator Borah, who opened an investigation on the strength of it: "the power of the printed word."
- **1939 World's Fair** (1971). Its theme was "a living symbol of democracy"; Doris Fleischman coined "Democracity".
- **Credit expansion** (1971). In the depression Bernays worked with economists to win support for credit expansion, citing Carl Snyder's finding that prosperity held when credit grew 4 per cent a year.

## Institutions that "sold" themselves (Crystallizing Public Opinion, 1923)

- **New York Central.** Its public relations department studied the public to find where service could be mended and wrong impressions corrected. It also built cooperative goodwill through help for travelers, conventions and children's camps, which had a conspicuous dramatic effect.
- **Subways.** A monopoly still has to sell itself. The subways worked to create "a feeling of submissiveness toward inconveniences which are more or less unavoidable" and to move traffic onto less used lines.
- **AT&T in wartime.** Service fell below standard, and efforts "analogous to a gracious personal apology" got the public to accept irksome conditions.
- **Jewelers.** Their message moved from the gift value of jewelry to answering the charge that the trade was useless: table silver is essential, and without watches business "would be a sad chaos."
- **Coal.** Hoover prescribed accurate publicity, including the ratio of production to capacity, to steady demand, let consumers judge prices, check overexpansion and shield the majority of operators from criticism earned by a minority.
- **Health departments.** Where disease comes from ignorance or neglect, publicity becomes a main function.
- **States and cities** compete for conventions and visitors. Vermont published a magazine on its resources and scenery.
- **Public figures at the head.** Industries such as motion pictures and organized baseball appointed men prominent in public life (such as Will Hays and Landis) to direct them, to assure the public of their members' honest and social-minded conduct.
- **Paul Revere.** Three riders carried the warning, but only Revere is remembered: "Did Revere make history or did Longfellow?"

## How the profession grew (1971)

- Bernays gave the first course in public relations, at New York University in 1923. By 1971 at least eighty-nine colleges gave concentrated work in it and more than 300 had at least one course. Bernays and Doris Fleischman coined "counsel on public relations."
- He set fees on a par with attorneys, *quantum meruit* ("what the matter is worth"), under letters of agreement. A normal year's fee was $12,000 to $15,000, paid in advance. The firm sought no clients directly. Its leaflet *Contact*, defining the field, went to group leaders and opinion molders.
- A 1950 study found how large firms conceived public relations: 35.3 per cent as a two-way activity including policy formulation, 29.4 per cent as good will, 10.6 per cent as part of selling, 10.6 per cent as publicity alone, and 8.2 per cent as interpreting business and public to each other. Listed counselors in New York rose from ten in 1935 to 394 in 1950. Spending on public relations reached an estimated $2,000,000,000 a year.
- "Business will change or change will be forced on it." The corporate counsel helps management set priorities, plan action programs, and devise internal and external communications. The counsel of the future is both generalist and specialist, and should be validated and licensed by the state. His ethical obligations rest on the public interest. Using social-science research, he projects future trends and interprets the public to the client "to enable him to anticipate the future in his attitudes and actions."
