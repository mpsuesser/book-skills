# Research and polls

Step 2 of the engineering of consent, and Bernays's rules for attitude polls. "Analysis of the problem and its causes is the first step toward shaping the public mind on any subject" (Manipulating Public Opinion, 1928).

## What research must yield

Research "should aim to establish a common denominator between the researcher and the public" (The Engineering of Consent, 1947). When it is finished it is a blueprint: "who does what, where, when, and why." It must indicate:
- the overall strategy, the themes, the organization, the use of media and the day-to-day tactics;
- how long it will take to win the public, and the short- and long-term trends of public thinking;
- "subconscious and conscious motivations in public thought, and the actions, words, and pictures that effect these motivations";
- public awareness, meaning "the low or high visibility of ideas in the public mind" (1947).

Research may force you to modify the objectives, enlarge or contract the goal, or change methods. It is "the mariner's chart, the architect's blueprint, the traveler's road map" (1947). It isolates areas of interest and support and the motives and attitudes of each public, and it "indicates accurately potentials of success". It tells you which favorable attitudes to intensify, which on-the-fence attitudes to convert and how to eliminate negative ones (1979). It also "defines areas of ignorance, apathy and knowledge" (1971). It applies to both sides of the problem: the public, and "the idea or product he seeks to propagandize" (1928).

## Questions to answer about each public (1947)

- What do they know?
- What are their present attitudes toward the situation, and what impulses govern those attitudes?
- What ideas are they ready to absorb? What are they ready to do, given an effective stimulant?
- Where do they get their ideas: "from bartenders, letter carriers, waitresses, Little Orphan Annie, or the editorial page of the *New York Times*?"
- Which group leaders or opinion molders effectively influence which followers? What is "the flow of ideas—from whom to whom?"
- How far do authority, factual evidence, precision, reason, tradition and emotion figure in their acceptance of ideas?

The public's attitudes, assumptions, ideas or prejudices "result from definite influences," and you must find what those influences are. Add the 1942 questions as well: "What are the limits of tolerance of the public? What symbols—words, pictures and actions—are effective in changing public attitudes and actions? What are the channels of communication through which to reach the public with these symbols? What is the group formation of the public we are reaching?" (The Marketing of National Policies, 1942).

For an industry or institution, ask (Molding Public Opinion, 1935):
- What is the general attitude of the public towards the products and service provided?
- How do specific actions and broad policies of the industry affect the public's attitude?
- "In what ways does the industry fail to measure up to what the public expects of it?"

Watch for fundamental changes under way. Then either change to meet the new demand, or "educate the public to its own point of view wherever public demands seem impossible of fulfillment." A survey surfaces facts that the salesman-customer relationship never brings out, and it shows everything competing with what the client sells (1935). Do social-science studies "of folkways and mores of communities" before acting in them. Procter and Gamble had these studies made before locating new plants (1971).

Measurement tells you "how far private interest conforms to public desires": "A baker now only bakes the bread he knows his public will buy." But "organized persuasion often changes attitudes of the public and leads it to new actions", so research sets the starting point and does not cap the goal (1942).

## Classifying the public (1935)

The political tactician's rough split is useful but rarely clean: those for him (no need to propagandize), those against, and those who may be swayed. Classify further by any line that predicts response. Possible lines are geography, age (dictators used youth movements to sway the more temperate adults), sex, finances, occupation, economic or political belief, social group, reading habits, intellectual capacity, leader or follower, employer or employed, religion, race and national origin, and special interests (sports, philanthropies, hobbies, clubs).

## Sources and methods

- **Polls, questionnaires, personal interviews** (1947). Professionals such as the Gallups, Harrises and Ropers are best. A layman without funds can plan and run his own poll from the books on polling: "Any poll intelligently planned and carried out, as a basis for procedure, is sounder than proceeding without a poll of any kind" (1979). "Any research of public opinion, no matter how minor, helps" (1971).
- **Querying leaders.** Query business leaders, trade-association heads, union officials, educators, and the heads of the medical association, architects and engineers. Query social-service executives, women's-club officials and religious leaders, as well as editors, publishers and radio and film people. Go down to the local unions of barbers, railwaymen, clothing workers and taxicab drivers: "Grass-roots leaders are important" (1947).
- **The double-barreled effect.** The leader survey teaches you what the leaders know and do not know, how far they will cooperate, which media reach them, which appeals are valid, and "the prejudices, the legends, or the facts by which they live". In the same stroke it shows whether they will run informational campaigns of their own and so supplement yours (1947).
- **The reference library.** Keep the standard directories of newspapers and periodicals, the editors' and broadcasters' yearbooks, the congressional directory, the almanacs (which list the country's thousands of associations, "a cross section of the country") and the telephone book (1947).
- **The mirrors of the public mind.** Studying the press, films and lecture platform shows their standards and those of the groups they reach. Add group and individual psychology to judge how readily each group will accept a change of view (Crystallizing Public Opinion, 1923).
- **Depth interviews.** They are not polls. They "try to discover the motives of people", find "what attitudes are permanent, what words, pictures, actions fix them, which attitudes can be changed, and how", and can indicate future trends (1945).
- **Trial balloons.** At the Washington and Hague conferences, diplomats tested projects through public statements and kept clipping bureaus up to date each morning on opinion at home. That is why world opinion was ready to receive the finished work (1923).

## Attitude polls: servants or masters? (1945)

**What a poll is.** "An attitude poll, in itself, conveys no message. Figures of a poll are the raw material." A poll is "a flash of light that reveals only a split second attitude", and it is an index to the future only if the interpreter "knows a good deal more than the figures of the poll show in themselves." Scientific polls cross-section the public before questioning (quota sampling, area sampling, panel polls), and some are repeated at intervals. "To interpret a poll from figures alone is like diagnosing a patient by only reading the thermometer." Stable readings over time can mislead too: the public may be apathetic or ignorant, and new facts tomorrow may change it.

**Where polls forecast well.** Five polls forecast the last presidential race within less than 2 per cent. Voting runs on traditional lines: the election "is usually decided long before the campaigning starts", and only a small share of the vote is flexible. Attitudes toward billboard advertising, radio commercials or child labor are another matter. Read those figures against "a broader analysis of public trends, counter drives and significant events."

**How fast attitudes move.** "Attitudes are changed very quickly by planned action, by unplanned events, even though developmental change is usually slow." The sinking of the *Panay* sent American hostility to Japan up overnight. Publicity about a single botulinus death from an olive turned attitudes from favorable to unfavorable. When Sonja Henie wore white skating shoes in a film, thousands of girls dropped the traditional black ones. "Authority or factual evidence dramatically presented may modify attitudes. So may effective reasoning or persuasion appealing to tradition or emotion." Words, pictures and actions can push polled attitudes up or down, or hold them by intensifying them.

**Why answers mislead.** A respondent may give:
- what he wants to tell, or what he thinks the inquirer wants to hear;
- the generally accepted view, out of conformity;
- an answer trimmed by unconscious censorship ("A man who says on Monday he isn't prejudiced against Negroes, may join a lynching party on Tuesday");
- a bandwagon answer;
- an answer that builds his ego or displays his status;
- an answer shaped by the conditions of the moment.

Question wording, the pollster's technique, bias and personality, and the "psychosomatic condition of the inquirer as well as the respondent" all shift answers. So does a good breakfast against a night out, a trip to the doctor, or the day after the fall of Paris. "Such widespread moods don't cancel one another out, so that the law of averages does not always apply." Answers are often rationalizations hiding reasons the respondent is ashamed of, and neurotic answers reflect inner struggle rather than real views. Yes-or-no polls miss **intensity**, and "the intensity with which an attitude is held indicates the potential of change."

**Dangers to society.**
1. Inaccurate polls influence the public as strongly as true ones. A newspaper poll wrongly gave O'Dwyer over 70 per cent (he got 57.3). The opposition's chairman wrote: "From that day on the campaign for good government was over for all practical purposes. Morale sagged, workers disappeared and revenues stopped."
2. Accurate polls can be just as damaging when published. A private Detroit poll showing a racial split in a candidate's support was printed by a newspaper, and the candidate blamed it for his defeat.
3. Stacked, phony and venal polls trade on the credence the public gives them.
4. Leaders who misread or distort polls "are a menace to society."

**The basic danger.** People take 51 per cent as "the will of the public". That habit displaces deciding "by accommodation and adjustment of the viewpoints of majority and minority groups": "Today the poll has muffled dissenting voices." Polls lull legislators and businessmen who ignore "passive or hidden points of view", and this leads to explosions when minorities turn articulate. Polls breed a "leadership of obedience to polls": "We are no longer led by men. We are led around by the polls." With a 70 per cent poll in hand the over-cautious ask, "Why should they stick their necks out by going against what seems to be majority opinion?" Real leaders are "almost invariably ahead of their followers", while **pseudo-leaders**, who are really followers, are encouraged by the polls. Leaders who follow polls "follow the past instead of advancing to the future", leave the public unprepared for change, and keep the status quo in place. Jefferson's "tyranny of the majority" throttles progressive minority ideas. "Majorities must be stimulated and educated to move ahead."

**The poll's proper role.** For leaders it is a tool of planning: it shows the attitudes to strengthen, change or preserve. For the public it should be "simply a thermometer". Democratic leadership works "through education, through persuasion", and a leader "can proceed no faster than his followers want to follow him."

**Recommendations.**
1. License pollsters, as doctors, lawyers, accountants and architects are licensed. A private supervisory body would lack the government's authority "to eliminate phony, stacked, venal, dishonest and inaccurate polls."
2. Educate the public and its leaders about polls. Releases about a poll should disclose any weighting and give regional and sectional breakdowns, so that readers can see the groups that make up the majority. Polls can then help decisions "represent the accommodation of many viewpoints."
