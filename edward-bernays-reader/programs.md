# Programs for particular clients

These are the ready-made programs Bernays worked out for kinds of clients. Each one applies the engineering of consent to a particular kind of client. Use the one that fits as a starting outline, then run the eight steps in SKILL.md on it.

## An industry or company: four steps essential to formulate a program (Molding Public Opinion, 1935)

1. **Formulation of objectives.** This is the basic step. The private profit motive and the public interest must coincide, or the program is abandoned.
2. **Analysis of public attitude.** Base it on "a broad and representative survey" (questions in research-and-polls.md). The analysis may call for changing the business to meet new demands, or for educating the public where its demands cannot be met.
3. **Study of analysis.** Keynote the approach "in terms of action of the industry." The action "must be continuous over a period of time. It must be overt wherever possible. It must be conceived in terms of the public's interest, in so far as this coincides with the industry's own private interest." Build the action into the whole functioning of the business. The policy "must reconcile the actual objective with the preconceptions, the preoccupations, and the existing attitudes of the many sections of the public."
4. **Use of media.** Project and interpret the industry "in terms of what the public is thinking and demanding". Keep up continuity. Dramatize the objective so that it "flow[s] naturally to the public through the accustomed channels". Develop events that symbolize the objectives, obtain the support of leaders and reflect that support to the public, and select the symbols.

Bernays gave the same outline to the milk dealers: objectives, a scientific analysis of the public, changes in policies, products or services based on the research, and then projection through the channels (1971).

## Banks and financial institutions (1935, told 1971)

This three-point program was meant to restore public opinion of banks in the depression:
1. public education on the need for banks;
2. public education on the function of banks, "in words the public understands";
3. community leadership by banks and bankers, so as to reestablish them in the public mind "through their deeds."

## A labor union (An Educational Program for Unions, 1947)

Public opinion on an issue evolves the way an iceberg comes into view. At first the public sees only the peak, and education shows it more and more of the submerged mass. "People must want cleanliness before they buy soap", and they must want unions before they will back specific union goals. Punitive legislation will not settle industrial conflict; education will. A union already educates its members, strengthens democracy in civic life, and, unannounced, "sells" itself to its rank and file. Add three programs:

1. **Educate the public on the value of sound unions and mature union leadership.** Most of what the public knows comes from headlines during disputes, and views aired in a controversy only harden existing attitudes, so supply facts between crises. Cover five subjects:
   - What is a union? Its organization, history, structure, kinds (industrial or craft; local, national or international; affiliated or not), jurisdiction, how officers are chosen, dues and finances.
   - The union's educational and welfare activities: the labor press, vocational training, apprenticeship, labor banking, insurance.
   - The facts of collective bargaining: what actually happens and how agreements are reached.
   - The facts of labor disputes: how they arise, their main causes, and whether there are as many as the public has been led to believe.
   - **What do the words mean?** "A whole vocabulary needs translating". The terms include wages, wage awards, wage differentials and wage stabilization; workload, work restriction and work sharing; and the closed shop, union shop and open shop.
2. **Educate the employer in unions and in "the science of humanics, the study of human relations."** Industry's use of technology "has far outstripped its use of the science of social behavior." By order of importance, the worker wants "a sense of security, an opportunity to advance, to be treated like a human being rather than as a number on the payroll", and the dignity of useful work. About half of workers would choose a sure low-paying job, and about a quarter a well-paid one with an even chance of losing it. Disruptions come from insecurity, from unfairness real or apparent, from assembly-line work, and from pay planned for individuals rather than groups. The employer program has eight points:
   1. the place of unions in the system;
   2. the human-relations data gathered by universities, unions, foundations and learned societies;
   3. more research by industrial-relations schools;
   4. technological research to improve working conditions;
   5. new approaches such as stabilized employment;
   6. honest, unbiased industrial-relations personnel;
   7. encouragement of responsible union leadership;
   8. support for housing, minimum-wage, social-security and other programs that strengthen democracy.

   Management fears that advice leads to control, but "just as the displacement of one log can break up a log jam, the winning over of one man may win over others." Reach employers through talks to employer groups, trade associations, chambers of commerce and service clubs; through radio and conversation ("a thought clearly expressed has a way of starting a chain reaction"); through factual stories, advertisements and pamphlets; and through letters to top management. Use reported talks to community groups, and material for women's clubs, lawyers and the clergy that reaches the businessman indirectly. Enlist colleges, foundations and progressive employers. "The educational process builds new points of view by continuous and repeated efforts."
3. **Educate members in economics and in their role in the industrial system.** Workers who understand management's problems "can bargain on a realistic basis". Some unions, such as the Amalgamated Clothing Workers, have even come to a struggling employer's aid, because years of member education taught the value of mutual assistance.

## A newspaper or other medium (The Press Must Act to Meet Postwar Responsibility, 1944)

Bernays asked three questions in order: what the paper's public-relations policies are (its platform), what the public thinks of it, and what issues the public cares about. He surveyed 169 publishers and compared the answers with a national survey of readers.

The findings showed a gap. Papers stated platforms of accuracy, impartiality and independence, with 91 per cent stressing no party tie or independence. Yet only 45.1 per cent of the public believed the news story usually accurate. About 50 per cent thought papers soft-pedal news unfavorable to friendly politicians or the publisher's friends, and about 40 per cent thought the same for big advertisers. Radio was preferred for interpretation and trusted more on prejudice (49 per cent against 17). Most platforms were local and physical (sewers, streets, parks), and 73 per cent showed little or no interest in national affairs.

The recommendations:
- Re-examine the platform against public attitudes and democratic goals. Stress national, international and local *social* goals, such as jobs, social security and aid to returning soldiers, alongside physical improvements.
- Adhere to the news planks and also "make a vigorous avowal of them". They "must be continually 'sold' to the public in every possible way."
- Lead through crusades, as the papers that fought the Stamp Act, slavery, the Klan and Teapot Dome did, and through public forums.
- Sell freedom from prejudice concretely. Run feature articles for each racial and religious group the paper reaches, and remove the racial angle from crime stories.
- Sell accuracy and independence specifically in the areas the public doubts: politics, labor, business, foreign affairs, religion and race, and independence from owners, politicians, capitalists, government and advertisers.
- If the paper serves the public as news purveyor and social instrument, "circulation and advertising follow."

## A nation at war: marketing national policies (The Marketing of National Policies, 1942)

"Ideas are weapons too." Psychological warfare has three main elements:
1. heighten the morale and unity of your own country;
2. weaken the morale of the enemy;
3. win over the morale of the neutrals.

**Lasswell's six factors** (reconstructed after the Great War):
1. fasten the war guilt on the enemy;
2. claim unity and victory in the names of history and deity;
3. state war aims (security, peace, a better social order, international law);
4. strengthen belief in the enemy's responsibility with examples of his depravity;
5. make the public believe unfavorable news is enemy lies, which prevents disunity and defeatism;
6. follow with horror stories, made to sound authoritative.

**Bruntz's classification** of techniques, also applicable at home:
1. *propaganda of enlightenment*: true facts to the enemy's people and army, against their own government's false information;
2. *propaganda of despair*: death, disaster and defeat ahead;
3. *propaganda of hope*: a promised land if they lay down arms (the Fourteen Points);
4. *particularist propaganda*: divide factions, such as Catholic against Protestant, or Alsace-Lorraine against Prussia;
5. *revolutionary propaganda*: break the enemy government from within.

**Lessons from the Great War.**
- Germany's handling was "desultory, segmented". It neglected the home front and used symbols, Machiavellianism, sabotage, terrorism and censorship, tried to split American groups, and tried to buy newspapers through false fronts. Its "deadly dull leaflets" and heavy academic books came out "too tactless, too open, too obvious, lacking in enthusiasm and 'inflammatory catchwords.' It antagonized more than it persuaded, and proved to be a boomerang." A German critic later judged that British propaganda was run by civilians and German by soldiers, and "it is not the soldier's but the psychologist's opinion that counts here."
- Britain's lasting contribution was the **group-leader approach**: personal correspondence with influential people, exchanges of visitors, and tours by leaders of neutral and allied countries. It "won leaders who influenced large groups."
- Both nations used censorship and propaganda as separate weapons. "They achieved unified fronts in military strategy not in psychological strategy."
- The American Committee on Public Information (April 13, 1917) worked democratically, "without threat, intimidation, and with only voluntary censorship". It had 14 divisions and adapted its material to each public. Its 75,000 **Four Minute Men**, the "spearhead of the assault on indifference and apathy", reached an estimated 400,000,000 listeners in movie houses and wherever audiences gathered. A scholars' division put out 75,000,000 pieces of literature. A single bright idea, inserts on war aims in every exporter's letters, spread by voluntary cooperation. But the effort was trial and error: staff were chosen for enthusiasm, not training, and "there never was a chart drawn in advance."
- Bad public relations damages morale. A British director of public relations who told the bombed public it lacked guts was denounced as unfit "to direct anything, least of all public relations."
- The totalitarian alternative centralizes every means of idea formation in one ministry and adds "brutality, threat, intimidation and censorship" to persuasion. Symbols are ground in: the compulsory salute, the flag in every window, the leader himself as "the most powerful symbol." Bernays set against it the democratic basis "of suggestion and persuasion."

**The engineering approach to national policy.** Ask "What are our goals? What are we selling? What are our aims? What is the time factor? Have we the men and materials we need?" Then study opinion, and work out strategy, planning and timing. "Every approach must be used to affect every kind of attitude as part of a broad integrated program, giving the proper relationship to each element", as an able army balances its weapons.

**Elements of a unified attack**, run concurrently:
1. **A psychological general staff.** It draws on social scientists, psychologists, ethnologists, adult educators, economists, the services, and experts in public opinion, communications, public relations and market research. It works to "a master plan of psychological approaches, just as there is a master plan for physical defense." It "should not make policy but it should advise on how to put policy into practice efficiently and democratically". It "should not be a propaganda bureau, not a publicity bureau" but "a planning, strategy and advisory body", with "no authority except the authority that is inherent in good advice."
2. **A program to strengthen faith in democracy.** Show "the true alternatives" between democracy and its rivals. Have everyone speak up for it, so as to create "a dynamic will for democracy instead of a passive acceptance."
3. **A program to strengthen democracy itself.** Make its ideals come true for all by increasing security, opportunity and education. "A happy healthy person has a strong morale." Belief must be founded on greater economic and psychological security; "psychological ramparts in this country must be as strong as our physical ramparts."

## A cause or ideology in peacetime (Public Education for Democracy, 1938; Speak Up for Democracy, 1940)

- An idea that is taken for granted needs an "inner bulwark of dynamic belief". "Lip service ... is not enough." The people must see the idea's "real values to them in every phase of their lives." Abstractions become persuasive when made concrete for the individual. One manufacturer explained what the American Way meant to him: mail uncensored, telephone untapped, free choice of party and vote, free reading, and open criticism of the government.
- Link the specific to the general value. The 1939 World's Fair was urged to show each exhibit "not of themselves alone but of their relation to the American system". Private enterprise was presented as a bulwark against state capitalism and the concentration of power.
- "In a dictatorship, propaganda is a function of the state. In a democracy it must be a function of the people." Run the campaign on two fields: the leaders of every kind of group, and every man and woman "to the limit of his own power". "Molding public opinion is not a gift or an inspiration. Like other professions—law, engineering, architecture—it combines a science and an art."
- **The group leader's local campaign.** Know what governs public action, the groups of the community, and "the value and effect of words and pictures." Then audit the local media:
  - what constituencies each newspaper represents, and its news and editorial policies;
  - who the editors are, and on what basis they want news;
  - the policies and personnel of the radio stations and press services;
  - the character and location of every public forum;
  - who books the important speaking engagements, and why and where;
  - who represents the newsreel companies.

  Plan the campaign "in terms of the kind of material these media carry". Join like-minded leaders of other groups in a joint executive committee.
- **Answering accusations.** "The most common accusations against democracy number less than a dozen. Each of us can have available strong and truthful answers to them, and help bury the accusations by incessant repetition of the answers." List the accusations, draft one strong, truthful answer to each, and repeat the answers everywhere. "Affirm the truth. Scratch the lie." Bernays's own list ran to eight accusations, each with its answer:
  1. *Too slow and inefficient.* Full, free discussion takes time and gives the greatest satisfaction to the greatest number. In an emergency democracy moves fast (recovery laws in 1933, defense laws in 1940) without the autocracies' abuse of power.
  2. *Free speech is meaningless or license.* Ask whether the accuser has ever criticized the government, and point to the concentration camp that awaits such criticism elsewhere. "If every man can say what he pleases, we have a fair chance at getting at the truth."
  3. *Security under a dictator beats liberty.* "Liberty and security are not mutually exclusive"; the Scandinavian democracies had both. A dictatorship offers no security, not even of life.
  4. *Dictatorships ended unemployment.* They create the illusion of jobs through forced labor. "Neither does a prison nor an army."
  5. *People are too ignorant to vote.* Lincoln answered that no man is good enough to govern another without that other's consent. Jefferson's remedy is "not to take it from them, but to inform their discretion by education."
  6. *Gangsterism shows democracy has rotted.* Here gangsters are illegal; there they are the government.
  7. *A strong man would decide better than representatives.* That presupposes he always knows the right answer, and benevolent despots have been very few.
  8. *Science will make dictatorship more efficient.* A dictator's errors carry the same force as his truths, as the Nazi blood theories show.

  Close with the vivid contrast: refugees' fear of the doorbell, the stranger's glance, the child late home, the telephone.
