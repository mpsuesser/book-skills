# The Edward Bernays Reader

An anthology of Edward Bernays's writings across four decades, from *Crystallizing Public Opinion* (1923) to "Human Engineering and Social Adjustment" (1979). One method runs through all of it. In 1947 he named it **the engineering of consent**: "the use of an engineering approach—that is, action based only on thorough knowledge of the situation and on the application of scientific principles and tried practices to the task of getting people to support ideas and programs." Any person or organization depends ultimately on public approval, so each one faces the problem of engineering the public's *consent* to its program or goal. Each idea here is tagged with the piece and year it comes from, so you can see how the method developed.

Work the user's case as Bernays would have worked it as **counsel on public relations**. Diagnose the relationship between the client and its publics, run the procedure below, and draw on the toolbox for each step. When a step reaches a branch below, open the reference file it names.

## The counsel's stance

- **Two-way interpreter.** The counsel's job is "to interpret his client to the public, and the public to his client" (Molding Public Opinion, 1935). In 1919 Bernays called his firm's work "publicity direction", meaning directing the client's actions so as to secure publicity. A year later he renamed it counsel on public relations, because the work only paid off on a **two-way street** that also carried the public's view back to the client (Emergence of the Public Relations Counsel, 1971).
- **Adjustment.** The profession deals with "a unit's relations with the publics on which it depends." It analyzes the **adjustments or maladjustments** between unit and publics, and it advises the unit on the attitudes and actions needed to meet its objectives (1971). It is "an instrument for achieving adjustment if any maladjustment in relationships exists" (The Engineering of Consent, 1947). Maladjustments come from the ignorance, prejudice or apathy of whoever acts on others (1979). Its goal is "the highest possible interrelationship between a unit and its publics, based on adjustment, information, and persuasion" (1971).
- **Action before words.** "Actions speak louder than words. Words occupy a secondary role. Unless actions are in the public interest, words in the long run fail" (1979). Historians said Wilson's words won the war and lost the peace. A program must be "integrated into the entire functioning" of the client. A program superimposed on the organization, offered as "lip worship to an idea" or made of mimeographed releases does not count. It may require the leaders to change their own thinking and action (1935). Even in 1923 the soap seller had to weigh "not only the quality of his soap but the working conditions, the hours of labor, even the living conditions of the men who make it." Bernays worked at policy level: he sat in on General Motors executive committee meetings and wrote the public-relations paragraph of its 1932 annual report (1971).
- **Coincidence of private and public interest.** "The private profit motive and the public interest must coincide. If the objectives are not in the public interest, the program should be abandoned" (1935). **Enlightened self-interest** is the working form. Life insurers profit from promoting public health. A radio maker sells good sets by stimulating "a more constructive and discriminating interest" in programs (1935). Brewers after Repeal backed the authorities in keeping the saloon from returning. Dixie Cup's interest in paper cups killed the communal drinking cup (1971).
- **Good will follows deeds.** Good will "can be preserved in the long run only by those whose actions warrant it", though the undeserving can win it "long enough to do a lot of damage" (1947). The counsel pushes only ideas he can respect (1947). Professional public relations "depends on reality, not on images" (1971).

## The engineering of consent, step by step

The backbone is the eight principles of 1979, filled in from the 1935 "Four Steps Essential to Formulate a Program" (objectives, analysis of public attitude, study of the analysis, use of media), the 1947 plan, the 1942 questions and the 1971 steps. Work every step and close each one on its criterion.

1. **Define your goals.** Before you define them, calculate your resources (the manpower, money and time available). Gain "as thorough knowledge of the subject as possible" by "collecting and codifying a store of information": the engineer of consent must be "powerfully equipped with facts, with truths, with evidence, before he begins to show himself before a public" (1947). Then state the goal exactly. A Red Cross drive sets its time limit and its money target from the start, and an appeal for one named country beats an appeal for "Europe" (1947). Ask "What changes in attitude and action are we seeking, in whom and when?" (1971), and settle "what is to be accomplished, with whom and through whom" (1947). Classify each goal by kind (1947):
   - *intensify* existing favorable attitudes;
   - *induce* those already favorable to take constructive action;
   - *convert* disbelievers;
   - *disrupt* antagonistic points of view.

   Goals run in **three time periods** (1979). Set the longtime goal first: the attitudes and actions you expect of your publics in X years, plus tangibles such as share of business, number and type of customers, employees, outlets and even stockholders. Set the immediate goal (about a year) and the intermediate goal (several years) after the research. "The more specific your goals, the more valuable the public opinion and/or market research will be." *Done when* each goal names the public, the attitude or action wanted, the kind of change and the date, and has passed the public-interest test.
2. **Research your publics** with public opinion or market research. Find what they know, their attitudes and the impulses behind them, whom they take their ideas from, the "flow of ideas—from whom to whom", the group formations, the leaders, the motives and the areas of ignorance, apathy and knowledge. *Done when* you can say who does what, where, when and why. For the full question list, the sources to query, the double-barreled survey and the rules for polls, open [research-and-polls.md](research-and-polls.md). Open it too whenever a poll is read, commissioned or published.
3. **Reorient your goals** in the light of the research. Goals may have "represented simply fulfilled desires instead of realistic appraisal." The path to the longtime goal "may not necessarily be logical. Illogical sequences may lead to ultimate goals" (1979). *Done when* each goal is realistic and attainable on the evidence, and the immediate and intermediate goals are set.
4. **Determine your strategy**: "how and when you will use your four M's—mindpower, manpower, mechanics, and money" (1979). Decide "to what extent should our activity stress adjusting to the public and to what extent should it attempt to modify public apathy, misunderstanding, or ignorance?" (1971). Choose "a blitzkrieg or a continuing battle, a combination of both, or some other strategy". An election is over in weeks, while cutting the tuberculosis death rate takes years (1947). Choose the basic policy (1935): "the most profound and widespread factfinding" and presentation of the facts, a program to educate the public on the broad issues, or "an active offensive on behalf of a particular fact". "Merely to express opinion, no matter how well, may be of little value or definitely harmful to the cause, if it does not specifically meet the underlying needs of the situation." *Done when* the policy, the tempo and the share of each M are fixed, and the strategy covers both intensifying favorable attitudes and "reversing or blanketing negative attitudes" (1971).
5. **Determine your themes and appeals.** The themes emerge from the survey. They "contain the ideas to be conveyed" and are "ever present but intangible—comparable to what in fiction is called the 'story line'" (1947). They must appeal to motives, which Bernays defines as "the activation of both conscious and subconscious pressures created by the force of desires" (1947). Resolve the case "into terms that will appeal to fundamental motives" (1935). An idea the public does not yet accept becomes acceptable "only if [it] can be shown to be of value to the public, and if [its] appeal can be related to acceptable facts, opinions, or customs. Symbols must be valid as well as vital" (1935). *Done when* every public has a theme tied to one of its motives and to something it already accepts (see the toolbox).
6. **Determine the organization.** Keep it minimal where authority at the top keeps everyone's conduct consistent. Make it complex where every contact counts, as in a hotel whose public relations depends on employee-customer relations throughout, or a corporation facing many groups and media. An outside counsel works like legal counsel, often jointly with an inside department (1971). Organize in advance, and correlate the specialists: opinion researchers, fund raisers, publicity men, radio and film experts, specialists for women's clubs and foreign-language groups (1947). Define each person's responsibilities. David Sarnoff's "We have no organization chart. This is a company of men, not of charts" taught Bernays the opposite (1971). *Done when* every planned action has an owner and the resources are in hand.
7. **Time and plan your tactics.** Draw a blueprint for each of the three time periods. It runs "from changing certain attitudes and actions to conform with the public interest to contacts with your publics through the media and opinion molders" (1979). Reach every channel your publics use. Shape tactics by the rules of **continuity**, **interlocking** and **timing**:
   - Think beyond **segmental approaches**. "The problem is not to get articles into a newspaper or obtain radio time", but "to set in motion a broad activity, the success of which depends on interlocking all phases and elements of the proposed strategy, implemented by tactics that are timed to the moment of maximum effectiveness." "An action held over but one day may fall completely flat" (1947).
   - Make the effort cumulative: "Isolated approaches are of little value", and the campaign must be "as progressive and unified as the erection of a building" (1935).
   - "Primarily, however, the engineer of consent must create news" (1947): build the overt act (see the toolbox).
   - Fit the material to the public: "simple language and sixteen-word sentences for the average school-age public", and some copy for readers with seventeen years of schooling (1947). State the objective in the terms of each medium, and pick the medium by the idea: "A thousand people marching may be more convincing than a thousand words, or vice versa" (1935).

   *Done when* each period has dated actions, each action carries a theme to a named public through a named channel, and the actions reinforce one another.
8. **Make up a budget.** Mechanics cost money. Mindpower and manpower can often be found as contributions for nonprofit work in the public interest, and you should budget in advance in every case (1979). *Done when* every M in the strategy is costed.

When the client is an industry, a union, a newspaper, a bank, a nation at war, or a cause or ideology, or when a group leader is running a local campaign, open [programs.md](programs.md). It holds the programs Bernays drew up for each, with the war-propaganda classifications and the method for answering accusations.

## The toolbox

**Publics and group leaders.** "What we call the public is made up of a great many smaller publics or groups banded together because of some common interest" (1935). A united public exists only in an emergency. Democratic society "is actually only a loose aggregate of constituent groups" (1947). Some are voluntary groups (professions, trade and farm associations, unions, women's clubs, churches, fraternal orders), some are formal ones such as parties, and some are unorganized voluntary groups such as the readers of one magazine or the listeners to one commentator. The individual, swamped by conflicting ideas, "is forced to look to others for authority and leadership", and "a credence in leaders is a sound shortcut (when leaders are sound)." So reach groups through their leaders: "If group leaders accept our ideas, the groups they dominate will respond." Leaders also interpret their groups back to you (1935). "Group adherence is the fulcrum around which broad acceptance for new ideas can most rapidly be moved. Certain small groups are important enough to influence the attitudes of large groups that overlap them" (Manipulating Public Opinion, 1928). Repeal of prohibition was won "not by directly converting millions of people, but by enlisting the active support of leaders of groups to which millions of people belonged" (1935). A **pressure group** is "any group of people interested enough in an idea to support it actively", even if only by writing a letter or attending a meeting (1935). **Opinion molders** are "those who through the spoken and written word have built constituencies of their own"; they reflect their constituencies and also affect them, and they "may often be the short cut to the publics you desire to adjust with." **Group leaders** are the elected or selected heads of interest groups, from the President to the local union president, the women's-club federation president or the cardinal (1979). Leaders "can only lead them as far as, and in the direction in which, they want to go" (1947).

**The overt act and the created circumstance.** "It is the overt act that makes news." The test of news is "whether the event juts out of the pattern of routine" (1947), after Lippmann's "any overt act that juts out of the routine of circumstance." Attitudes are "often created by a circumstance or circumstances of dramatic moment", so the propagandist "is called upon to create a circumstance that will eventuate in the desired reaction" (1928). Bernays defined it in 1928: "Modern propaganda is a consistent, enduring effort to create or shape events to influence the relations of the public to an enterprise, idea or group" (The New Propaganda). To build the event:
- bring the groups that carry authority for the idea "into juxtaposition", preferably "at one time and at one place, before an audience of those most concerned";
- put it in a place that itself symbolizes the idea, such as the Louvre for accredited beauty, Aeolian Hall for music, or the South for a Southern question;
- staff it with people who are "stereotypes for ideas that carried weight";
- "the remainder of the work was simply filling in the outline with real people" (1928).

The event reaches "infinitely more people than those actually participating", and "the imaginatively managed event can compete successfully with other events for attention" (1947). Set events in **chain reaction**: harness group leaders so that they stage their own subsidiary events on the theme (1947). Blanket a rumor or a typecast image with an overt act that contradicts it. Bernays held that this beats denial (as the Reader's introduction recalls him saying). Coolidge, said to have been "weaned on a pickle", breakfasted at the White House with Al Jolson and the Dolly Sisters. Fourteen Points, Four Freedoms and national anniversaries are overt acts too (1979). For a precedent to model an event, a sponsorship, a cliche change or a prejudice campaign on, open [case-book.md](case-book.md), which indexes Bernays's campaigns by technique.

**Motives.** These are "the psychological raw materials": "self-preservation, ambition, pride, hunger, love of family and children, patriotism, imitativeness, the desire to be a leader, love of play" (1935). Later lists add the desire to be a leader or a follower, the desire for immortality and parental love (1979). Find the quality of your case that serves a motive, and the authorities will press it "of *their own* accord", as health authorities did with milk and self-preservation (1935).

**Symbols, cliches, stereotypes.** "A symbol may be defined as a short-cut to understanding and to action. It is the currency of propaganda." Often it is a word: the "wets" tied "racketeer" to "prohibition." For a corporation the symbol may be its head, a slogan or one department that performs a public service. Its acceptance is "emotional and an expression of familiarity", and "the potency of symbols is constantly changing" (1935). The program's work is "to associate the special pleading with ideas to which the public is receptive" (1935). **Changing cliches** substitutes new ideas for old: renaming the "evacuation hospital" an "evacuation post" ended criticism, because no one expected more than emergency treatment from a post (1928). Groups "cling to [their] stereotypes" (Lippmann's word) with such tenacity that leaders become "mere bits of driftwood in the surf" (1928). Present a thing in terms of its larger meaning: the automobile shown as freedom of motion, exchange of ideas and taxes paid becomes "an instrument of democracy" (Public Education for Democracy, 1938). Words alone are weak. "People accept only what they *a priori* are willing to accept", and words that lack "factual evidence, authority, reason, tradition, or ... strong emotion" lose their impact on unbelievers. "The meaning of words is as fragile as a soap bubble or lace" (1979).

**Bases of acceptance.** "Acceptance of ideas comes through feeling, reason, custom, authority, persuasion and factual evidence" (The Marketing of National Policies, 1942). Weigh, for each public, "to what extent do authority, factual evidence, precision, reason, tradition, and emotion play a part" (1947). **Authority** is borrowed by placing the idea with its recognized sources: French couturiers for velvet, the Louvre for American silk ("It must be good, therefore!"), scientists for the panatrope, religious leaders, dietary officials and observant hospitals for a kosher shortening (1928). Choose a source whose authority rises because it speaks against type, such as a Southern city for a Southern reform (1928).

**Enlisting the established point of view.** "The influence of any force which attempts to modify public opinion depends upon the success with which it is able to enlist established points of view" (Crystallizing Public Opinion, 1923). Before advising any step that modifies an established belief, the counsel must weigh the public's *a priori* judgment. "It is seldom effective to call names or to attempt to discredit the beliefs themselves." Examine the sources of the belief, then "either discredit the old authorities or create new authorities by making articulate a mass opinion against the old belief or in favor of the new" (1923). Where opinion is split, lift the favorable segment away from the hostile one and let it sponsor you. *Damaged Goods*, a 1913 play about sex education, was presented under the sponsorship of groups that believed in education and truth, through a Sociological Fund whose members paid four dollars a performance. This was Bernays's standing principle, "to make a cause of our project" (1923, 1971). To diagnose why a public holds a belief and how far it can move, or to settle what propaganda is, open [public-opinion.md](public-opinion.md). It covers the group and the herd, *a priori* judgment, "logic-proof compartments", the public and the press leading each other, the new propaganda, and frustration and the demagogue.

**The 1928 repertoire.** The specialist has "methods adapted to":
- educating the public to new ideas;
- articulating minority ideas and strengthening them;
- making latent majority ideas active;
- making an old principle apply to a new idea;
- substituting ideas by changing cliches;
- overcoming prejudices;
- making a part stand for the whole;
- creating events and circumstances that stand for his ideas.

A **prejudice** is "often the application of old taboos to new conditions", and it outlives its cause, as the prejudice against margarine outlasted the impure margarine. Remove it by gathering and publishing the findings of medical and dietary authorities (1928). Sometimes the analysis points to a change in the client's own policy or product, as when the shortening was made kosher before it was publicized (1928).

**Media.** Know every one of the "physical organs of approach" and how to translate an idea "into terms that fit any given form of communication, and that his public can understand" (1928). The network has two levels: the commercial media, and the **specialized media** that organized groups run for themselves (house organs, labor papers, bulletins, meetings, rank-and-file conversation) (1947). "The old-time press agent sent pieces to the paper; the propagandist today may wage an entire campaign without sending a single release to the newspapers" (1935). Channels stay open to anyone "if he brings them the ideas and material their publics demand." Audit the local media before planning; the checklist is in [programs.md](programs.md) (Speak Up for Democracy, 1940).

## Diagnostics

- **Is it news?** Does the act jut out of the routine? If it does not, it will not travel past the people in the room.
- **Who leads this public?** If you cannot name the leaders and opinion molders whose word moves the public, step 2 is not done.
- **Crystallized or uncrystallized?** Deep-seated, well-crystallized attitudes resist change. It is the uncrystallized situations "that polls, leaders or any other influence can affect" (Hadley Cantril, quoted 1945). "The intensity with which an attitude is held indicates the potential of change" (1945).
- **Iceberg.** "Public opinion is like an iceberg. The visible portion is the expressed attitudes, but the submerged portion of public opinion is sometimes potentially the more powerful" (Attitude Polls—Servants or Masters?, 1945). Plan for the hidden minority that becomes "articulate, active and overt."
- **Integrated or superimposed?** If the program asks nothing of the client's conduct, it is lip worship.
- **Segmental or unified?** "Segmented attack may be of some little value, but integration and unified attack are fundamental" (1942). Germany's propaganda in the Great War failed as "a desultory, segmented handling of the marketing problem."
- **Done and seen to be done.** An obligation "must not only be fulfilled but must also be recognized by the public as having been fulfilled" (The Press Must Act to Meet Postwar Responsibility, 1944). Measure the gap between what the client stands for and what the public credits it with, and "sell" the planks the public doubts.
- **Calm or superheated?** Viewpoints voiced during a controversy "merely [intensify] existing attitudes. People are more receptive to facts when issues are not superheated by emotional pressures" (An Educational Program for Unions, 1947). Educate between crises.
- **Want before goal.** "People must want cleanliness before they buy soap", and they must want unions before they will back specific union goals (1947). Build the underlying want first.
- **Counter-propaganda free?** "Every one is a propagandist for some platform." The safeguard is that "there is always freedom of counter-propaganda available" (1935), so expect proponents and opponents of every propaganda and plan the answer to yours (1928).
