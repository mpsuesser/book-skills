# Public opinion: what it is and how it forms

Bernays's theory of the material the counsel works with. Use it to diagnose why a public believes what it does, how far that belief can move, and through what.

## Definitions

- **Public opinion (1923).** "A term describing an ill-defined, mercurial and changeable group of individual judgments". It is "the aggregate result of individual opinions—now uniform, now conflicting—of the men and women who make up society or any group of society." To understand it, go back to the individual (Crystallizing Public Opinion).
- **Public opinion (1928), narrow and broad.** Narrowly, "the thought of any given group of society at any given time toward a given object". Broadly, "the power of the group to sway the larger public in its attitude toward ideas" (Manipulating Public Opinion).
- **Propaganda.** "Modern propaganda is a consistent, enduring effort to create or shape events to influence the relations of the public to an enterprise, idea or group." In the broad sense it is "an organized effort to spread a particular belief or doctrine" (The New Propaganda, 1928). The dictionary sense Bernays endorses is "effort directed systematically toward the gaining of public support for an opinion or a course of action." The word comes from the College of Propaganda that Urban VIII founded in 1627 to train missionary priests. Whether a propaganda is good or bad "depends upon the merit of the cause urged, and the correctness of the information published". It turns vicious only when its authors knowingly spread lies or aim at effects they know harm the common good (*Scientific American*, quoted 1928). People call their own advocacy "a plain forceful statement of truth" and the other side's "propaganda". Bernays rejects the definition of propaganda as appeal to emotion instead of the brain: "the propagandist uses every sound method available to him" (1935).
- **Propaganda versus education.** Propaganda is "a species of education in that it presents new problems for study and consideration to the public, and leaves it free to approve or reject them. ... Education attempts to be disinterested, while propaganda is frankly partisan" (1928). The engineering of consent supplements education and never displaces it. It would stay essential even under perfect schooling, because there would always be "time lags, blind spots, and points of weakness" (1947). Bernays put the average adult at six years of schooling in 1947 and at eight by the 1940 census in 1949. Leaders facing a crisis cannot wait for general understanding, but they are obliged to use the educational processes too (1947).
- **The special pleader.** The innovator or leader of new ideas who "has through necessity developed a new technique—the psychology of public persuasion" (1928).

## Inertia and the minority

"Civilization ... is limited by inertia". Repeated beliefs and habits become "a cumulative retrogressive force", and against them stands "an active public opinion that has been directed consciously into movement against inertia" (1928). New ideas are usually minority ideas, "for naturally, but regrettably, majority ideas are most often old ones. Public opinion is slow and reactionary." The "intelligent minorities" must use propaganda "continuously and systematically", since "in the active proselytizing minorities in whom selfish interests and public interests coincide lie the progress and development of America" (1928). The new propaganda also gives voice to a majority. Millions of housewives may want harmful foods banned, but their "half-expressed demand" goes nowhere until it is "organized, made vocal, and concentrated upon the state legislature or upon the Federal Congress" (1928). Public relations "makes it possible for minority ideas to be more readily accepted by the majority" (1971). At the core of public opinion lies "a tenacious will to live, to progress, to move in the direction of ultimate social and individual benefit. He who seeks to manipulate public opinion must always heed it" (1928).

## The new propaganda and the invisible government (1928)

The steam engine, the multiple press and the public school took power from kings and gave it to the people, until "the masses promised to become king." Then "the minority has discovered a powerful help in influencing majorities", and "Propaganda is the executive arm of the invisible government." Universal literacy gave the common man "rubber stamps" instead of a mind. These are stamps "inked with advertising slogans, with editorials, with published scientific data, with the trivialities of the tabloids and the platitudes of history", so the same stimuli leave identical imprints on millions. Propaganda is universal and continuous, and in sum it "is regimenting the public mind every bit as much as an army regiments the bodies of its soldiers." Charities "have to work on public opinion just as though they had tubes of toothpaste to sell", and the falling infant death rate is propaganda's work too.

The wartime discovery came from appealing to individuals by every approach (visual, graphic and auditory) while also securing "the cooperation of the key men in every group—persons whose mere word carried authority to hundreds or thousands or hundreds of thousands of followers." Wartime propaganda also used "the mental clichés and the emotional habits of the public." The **new propaganda** "takes account not merely of the individual, nor even of the mass mind alone, but also and especially of the anatomy of society, with its interlocking group formations and loyalties." The individual is "a cell organized into the social unit. Touch a nerve at a sensitive spot and you get an automatic response from certain specific members of the organism." Hunt the anatomy for the vital spot. For velvet that spot was Paris and Lyons, where the "attack had to be made at the source."

A source's identity can matter more than its content. Half the front-page stories in one day's *New York Times* were propaganda, among them a State Department report on China: "What it says is less important than what it is. ... Its source gives it authority." A picture that fits a group's prejudices is grasped and made its own. The Klan leader offered a picture of a Nordic nation to men who felt "elbowed out" by newer immigrants (1928).

## The group and the herd (1923)

- The average man's judgments are "for the most part dogmatic expressions accepted on the authority of his parents, his teachers, his church, and of his social, his economic and other leaders." The counsel asks why one man joins one church, why Boston women prefer brown eggs and New York women white, and what converts a man from one party or one food to another.
- "Persons who have little knowledge of a subject almost invariably form definite and positive judgments upon that subject." Trotter's "mental furniture" of the average man is made of precise judgments on unsolved questions, where the rational attitude would be suspended judgment.
- "Men who know little are often intolerant of a point of view that is contrary to their own". In controversy, opponents "desert the main line of arguments in order to abuse each other", and "prejudice superseded logic."
- **Logic-proof compartments** (a psychologist's term) hold beliefs that sit beyond the reach of reasoning. The scientist who is open within his own field is closed outside it. Mothers feed their babies what they would forbid other mothers to give. Peoples keep dietary and hygienic laws long after the conditions behind them are gone.
- Each side believes its own position rational and finds the other's unreasonable. The difference lies not in logic but in hostile **fundamental assumptions** derived from **herd-suggestion**, which have become "*a priori* syntheses" (Trotter). Hence the rule: weigh the public's *a priori* judgment, do not attack the belief or call names, and work on its sources by discrediting the old authorities or creating new ones.

## Stubborn or malleable? (1923)

People call opinion uniformity "the public conscience" when it matches their own beliefs and "regimentation of the public mind" when it does not. Uniformity "is, in fact, largely natural and only partly artificial", and "public opinion may be as much the producer of 'insidious propaganda' as its product." There are three views of the press:
1. The public is stubborn and the press has little influence. New York elected mayors whom nearly every daily opposed, and educated men discount crusading papers.
2. The press merely reflects and intensifies established opinion.
3. The view "nearer the truth": the press and other media bring "a very definite change". The complete popular conversion on woman suffrage is the example, along with changing attitudes to birth control and health education.

So a malleable public opinion exists, and the counsel "simply utilizes existent channels". Much of what looks like the press's censorship is inadequate presentation. Lippmann noted that strikes get reported through the overt act, as interference with the reader's life, because that is where attention catches.

## Interaction: the public and its institutions lead each other (1923)

- "While it appears to be forming the public opinion on fundamental matters, the press is often conforming to it." What news is "fit to print" rests on "a consensus of public opinion on which the newspaper falls back for its standards." A newspaper must adapt its pleading to its readers' moral limits "just as the trial lawyer also must adapt his pleading to the jury's limitations" (Mencken). The editor "listens for the echo of his words" (Ogden).
- The counsel's office is to determine this interaction and weigh the relative powers "with fair accuracy". "It is as important to conform to the standards of the organ which projects ideas as it is to present to this organ such ideas as will conform to the fundamental understanding and appreciation of the public."
- "'Give the people what they want' is only half sound." The preacher leads his flock where it shows a willingness to be led, and Ibsen makes a revolution when society is ripe for it. "The press, the lecturer, the screen and the public lead and are led by each other."
- Film censorship lasts as long as the public accepts it to protect children. Books once stifled are later demanded when opinion shifts. A magazine backs light wine and beer because it judges that its readers favor it.
- Opinion is sometimes moved against the grain. Some papers hold a policy their public is cold to, "not entirely without success". Sustained, varied campaigns change habits: Safety First used posters, circulars, lectures, law enforcement, films and "safety weeks", and the hookworm campaign worked through analysis, investigation, applied medicine and public education.
- "No idea or opinion is an isolated factor. It is surrounded and influenced by precedent, authority, habit and all the other human motivations." Find "what the stimuli are to which public opinion responds most readily."

## Why the public demands to be heard (1923)

Three tendencies made the counsel necessary:
1. Small organizations aggregated into groups so large that the public regards them as semipublic services.
2. Literacy and democracy made the public feel entitled to a voice in the conduct of those aggregations, whether political, capitalist or labor.
3. Competition for public favor sharpened under modern "selling".

The public "asks questions, and if the answer in word or action is not forthcoming or satisfactory, it turns to other sources for information or relief." Lippmann named "the revolution which is taking place in the art of creating consent among the governed." Business moved in stages. In the **public-be-damned** period it ignored the public. In the **public-be-informed** period, ushered in by Ivy Lee's 1906 declaration, it answered the reformers "by meeting words with words" through press bureaus, which critics called "whitewashing" and "space grabbers". Then came counsel, which changes attitudes and actions as well (1971).

## Frustration, aggression and the demagogue (Why We Behave Like Inhuman Beings, 1949)

- "The key problem of the twentieth century is one of human relations": we have made "fabulous progress in technology without comparable progress in the ability to handle human relations."
- Societies are stable when individuals obtain "the physical, intellectual, emotional, esthetic, moral, and religious satisfactions which their natures require". Severe **frustration** throws people back to "primitive and infantile modes of conduct", rouses fear and the desire for revenge, and stimulates the aggressive impulses that a satisfying civilization sublimates.
- Carver classifies aggression as destructive (war, robbery, murder), deceptive (swindling, adulteration, false advertising) and persuasive (political, erotic, commercial and legal attacks).
- Children rejected or humiliated for their poverty, race, religion or looks grow into men who seek a "whipping boy". The frustrated man who leaves school unequipped becomes "the perfect sucker for the paranoid demagogue" with his promises of "revenge, conquest, and plunder". Barbarous aggression is "mistaken for a short cut to achievement."
- Environment outweighs heredity. Sumner's **folkways** are "the everyday incidents of our environment which pattern our thoughts and actions." The illiterate half of the world responds to words, pictures and actions "as these would be interpreted by primitive man". Those not educated to resist "succumb to words and pictures whose explosive meanings stimulate the inhuman behavior which is the stock in trade of bigotry, fanaticism, and the fantasy of world conquest."
- The remedy is "accelerated self-understanding": the social sciences, anthropology, psychiatry, psychoanalysis and sociology applied to human relations as physics is applied to nature.
- Postwar insight in the same vein: after the Great War "this powerful common man could be influenced by symbols, by words, pictures and actions. Appeals could be made to his prejudices, his loves and his hates, to his unfulfilled desires" (1942).

## The communications network

The size of the network is the argument for an engineering approach. "The United States has become a small room in which a single whisper is magnified thousands of times" (1947). Its organization lets "practically any person or any group or any movement ... be brought almost immediately into the closest juxtaposition with people almost anywhere" (1935). By 1979 one estimate counted over 400 impacts a day trying to bring "a coincidence of attitude and action between projector of idea and public." The counts Bernays cites:
