# Words and Expressions Commonly Misused

Many of these are "not so much bad English as bad style, the commonplaces of careless writing." As *feature* shows, the right correction is often the replacement of vague generality by definite statement, not of one word by another. Pairs read faulty form → correction.

**All right.** Idiomatic in familiar speech as a detached phrase meaning "Agreed" or "Go ahead." In other uses better avoided. Always two words.

**As good or better.** Correct by rearranging: *My opinion is as good or better than his.* → *My opinion is as good as his, or better* (*if not better*).

**As to whether.** *Whether* is sufficient (Rule 13).

**Bid.** Takes the infinitive without *to*. The past tense is *bade*.

**Case.** The Concise Oxford Dictionary defines it first as "instance of a thing's occurring; usual state of affairs." In these two senses the word is usually unnecessary.
- *In many cases, the rooms were poorly ventilated.* → *Many of the rooms were poorly ventilated.*
- *It has rarely been the case that any mistake has been made.* → *Few mistakes have been made.*

**Certainly.** Used indiscriminately by some, as others use *very*, to intensify any and every statement. A mannerism bad in speech and worse in writing.

**Character.** Often simply redundant, from a mere habit of wordiness: *Acts of a hostile character* → *Hostile acts*.

**Claim** (verb). With an object noun it means *lay claim to*. It may take a dependent clause only if that sense is clearly involved: *He claimed that he was the sole surviving heir* (even here, *claimed to be* is better). Not a substitute for *declare*, *maintain*, or *charge*.

**Compare.** *Compare to*: point out or imply resemblances between objects regarded as essentially of different order. *Compare with*: mainly point out differences between objects regarded as essentially of the same order. Life has been compared *to* a pilgrimage, a drama, a battle; Congress may be compared *with* the British Parliament. Paris has been compared *to* ancient Athens; it may be compared *with* modern London.

**Clever.** Greatly overused; best restricted to ingenuity displayed in small matters.

**Consider.** Not followed by *as* when it means "believe to be": *I consider him thoroughly competent.* With *as* it means "examined" or "discussed": *The lecturer considered Cromwell first as soldier and second as administrator.*

**Dependable.** A needless substitute for *reliable*, *trustworthy*.

**Due to.** Wrong for *through*, *because of*, or *owing to* in adverbial phrases: *He lost the first game, due to carelessness.* Right when related as predicate or modifier to a particular noun: *This invention is due to Edison*; *losses due to preventable fires*.

**Effect.** As a noun, *result*; as a verb, *to bring about, accomplish* (not to be confused with *affect*, "to influence"). As a noun it is often loosely used in perfunctory writing about fashions, music, painting, and the other arts: *an Oriental effect*, *effects in pale green*, *very delicate effects*, *broad effects*, *subtle effects*, *a charming effect was produced by*. "The writer who has a definite meaning to express will not take refuge in such vagueness."

**Etc.** Not used of persons. It means *and the rest*, *and so forth*, so do not use it where one of these would be insufficient, leaving the reader in doubt about any important particulars. Least open to objection when it stands for the last terms of a list already given in full, or for immaterial words at the end of a quotation. At the end of a list introduced by *such as*, *for example*, or a similar expression, *etc.* is incorrect.

**Fact.** Use only of matters capable of direct verification, not of matters of judgment. That an event happened on a given date, or that lead melts at a certain temperature, are facts. That Napoleon was the greatest of modern generals, or that the climate of California is delightful, "however incontestable they may be, are not properly facts." On *the fact that*, see Rule 13.

**Factor.** A hackneyed word; the expressions it forms part of can usually be replaced by something more direct and idiomatic.
- *His superior training was the great factor in his winning the match.* → *He won the match by being better trained.*
- *Heavy artillery is becoming an increasingly important factor in deciding battles.* → *Heavy artillery is playing a larger and larger part in deciding battles.*

**Feature.** Another hackneyed word; like *factor*, it usually adds nothing. For *A feature of the entertainment especially worthy of mention was the singing of Miss A.*, better to use the same number of words to tell what Miss A. sang, or, if the programme has already been given, something of how she sang. As a verb in the advertising sense (offer as a special attraction), avoid it.

**Fix.** Colloquial in America for *arrange*, *prepare*, *mend*. In writing, restrict it to its literary senses: *fasten*, *make firm or immovable*.

**He is a man who.** A common redundant type (Rule 13).
- *He is a man who is very ambitious.* → *He is very ambitious.*
- *Spain is a country which I have always wanted to visit.* → *I have always wanted to visit Spain.*

**However.** Meaning *nevertheless*, it does not come first in its sentence or clause: *The roads were almost impassable. However, we at last succeeded in reaching camp.* → *At last, however, we succeeded in reaching camp.* First in its sentence, *however* means *in whatever way* or *to whatever extent*: *However you advise him, he will probably do as he thinks best.* *However discouraging the prospect, he never lost heart.*

**Kind of.** Not a substitute for *rather* (before adjectives and verbs), nor, except in familiar style, for *something like* (before nouns). Restrict it to its literal sense: *Amber is a kind of fossil resin*; *I dislike that kind of notoriety*. The same holds for *sort of*.

**Less.** Not for *fewer*. *Less* refers to quantity, *fewer* to number: *He had less men than in the previous campaign* → *He had fewer men*. *His troubles are less than mine* means not so great; *fewer than mine* means not so numerous. Correct: *The signers of the petition were less than a hundred*, where the round number is something like a collective noun and *less* means a less quantity or amount.

**Line, along these lines.** *Line* meaning course of procedure, conduct, or thought is allowable but so overworked, especially in *along these lines*, that a writer aiming at freshness or originality had better discard it entirely.
- *Mr. B. also spoke along the same lines.* → *Mr. B. also spoke, to the same effect.*
- *He is studying along the line of French literature.* → *He is studying French literature.*

**Literal, literally.** Often wrongly used in support of exaggeration or violent metaphor: *A literal flood of abuse* → *A flood of abuse*; *Literally dead with fatigue* → *Almost dead with fatigue* (*dead tired*).

**Lose out.** Meant to be more emphatic than *lose*, but less so because of its commonness. The same holds for *try out*, *win out*, *sign up*, *register up*. With some verbs, *out* and *up* form idiomatic combinations each distinguishable in meaning from the simple verb: *find out*, *run out*, *turn out*, *cheer up*, *dry up*, *make up*. *Lose out* is not one of them.

**Most.** Not for *almost*: *Most everybody* → *Almost everybody*; *Most all the time* → *Almost all the time*.

**Nature.** Often simply redundant, like *character*: *Acts of a hostile nature* → *Hostile acts*. Often vague, as in *a lover of nature* or *poems about nature*: unless more specific statements follow, the reader cannot tell whether the poems concern natural scenery, rural life, the sunset, the untracked wilderness, or the habits of squirrels.

**Near by.** An adverbial phrase not yet fully accepted as good English, though the analogy of *close by* and *hard by* seems to justify it. *Near*, or *near at hand*, is as good, if not better. Not to be used as an adjective; use *neighboring*.

**Oftentimes, ofttimes.** Archaic forms, no longer in good use. The modern word is *often*.

**One hundred and one.** Keep the *and* in this and similar expressions, following the unvarying usage of English prose since Old English times.

**One of the most.** Avoid opening essays or paragraphs with this formula (*One of the most interesting developments of modern science is...*; *Switzerland is one of the most interesting countries of Europe*). Nothing is wrong with it; "it is simply threadbare and forcible-feeble."

**People.** *The people* is a political term, not to be confused with *the public*: from the people come political support or opposition; from the public, artistic appreciation or commercial patronage. Do not use *people* with words of number in place of *persons*: "If of 'six people' five went away, how many 'people' would be left?"

**Phase.** A stage of transition or development: *the phases of the moon*; *the last phase*. Not for *aspect* or *topic*: *Another phase of the subject* → *Another point* (*another question*).

**Possess.** Not a mere substitute for *have* or *own*: *He possessed great courage.* → *He had great courage* (*was very brave*). *He was the fortunate possessor of* → *He owned*.

**Respective, respectively.** Usually better omitted.
- *Works of fiction are listed under the names of their respective authors.* → *...under the names of their authors.*
- *The one mile and two mile runs were won by Jones and Cummings respectively.* → *...were won by Jones and by Cummings.*
Some formal writing, such as geometrical proofs, may need *respectively*; writing on ordinary subjects should not have it.

**So.** Avoid in writing as an intensifier: *so good*, *so warm*, *so delightful*. On *so* introducing a clause, see Rule 5.

**Sort of.** See *kind of*.

**State.** Not a mere substitute for *say* or *remark*. Restrict it to the sense *express fully or clearly*: *He refused to state his objections.*

**Student body.** Needless and awkward; it means no more than *students*: *A member of the student body* → *A student*; *Popular with the student body* → *Liked by the students*; *The student body passed resolutions* → *The students passed resolutions*.

**System.** Frequently used without need: *Dayton has adopted the commission system of government* → *Dayton has adopted government by commission*; *The dormitory system* → *Dormitories*.

**Thanking you in advance.** It sounds as if the writer meant, "It will not be worth my while to write to you again." Write simply *Thanking you*, and if the favor is granted, write a letter of acknowledgment.

**They.** A plural pronoun with a distributive antecedent (*each*, *each one*, *everybody*, *every one*, *many a man*) is a common inaccuracy: these imply more than one person but require a singular pronoun. With even less justification, the plural is used with *anybody*, *any one*, *somebody*, *some one*, to avoid the awkward "he or she" or to avoid committing oneself to either. "Some bashful speakers even say, 'A friend of mine told me that they, etc.'" Use *he* with all these words unless the antecedent is or must be feminine.

**Very.** Use sparingly. Where emphasis is necessary, use words strong in themselves.

**Viewpoint.** Write *point of view*, and do not misuse it, as many do, for *view* or *opinion*.

**While.** Avoid using it indiscriminately for *and*, *but*, and *although*. Many writers use it for *and* or *but* from a mere desire to vary the connective, or from uncertainty which of the two is more appropriate; in that use a semicolon is the better replacement. The paraphrase is the test:
- *The office and salesrooms are on the ground floor, while the rest of the building is devoted to manufacturing* paraphrases cleanly as *The office and salesrooms are on the ground floor; the rest of the building is devoted to manufacturing.*
- As a near-equivalent of *although*, *while* is allowable where it leads to no ambiguity or absurdity: *While I admire his energy, I wish it were employed in a better cause* holds up as *I admire his energy; at the same time I wish it were employed in a better cause.*
- *While the temperature reaches 90 or 95 degrees in the daytime, the nights are often chilly* → *Although the temperature reaches...*: the paraphrase *...in the daytime; at the same time the nights are often chilly* shows why *while* is wrong here.
In general, use *while* only with strict literalness, meaning *during the time that*.

**Whom.** Often wrongly used for *who* before *he said* or a similar expression, when it is really the subject of a following verb:
- *His brother, whom he said would send him the money* → *His brother, who he said would send him the money*
- *The man whom he thought was his friend* → *The man who* (*that*) *he thought was his friend* (*whom he thought his friend*)

**Worth while.** Overworked as a term of vague approval and, with *not*, of disapproval. Strictly applicable only to actions: *Is it worth while to telegraph?* *His books are not worth while.* → *His books are not worth reading* (*not worth one's while to read*; *do not repay reading*). Before a noun (*a worth while story*) it is "indefensible."

**Would.** A conditional statement in the first person requires *should*, not *would*: *I should not have succeeded without his help.* After a verb in the past tense, the equivalent of *shall* in indirect quotation is *should*: *He predicted that before long we should have a great surprise.* For habitual or repeated action the past tense without *would* is usually sufficient and, being briefer, more emphatic: *Once a year he would visit the old mansion.* → *Once a year he visited the old mansion.*
