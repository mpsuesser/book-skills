# Elementary Principles of Composition (Rules 9–18)

Full detail for each principle of composition: the paragraph plans, the analyzed model paragraphs, the tests, and the book's before-and-after pairs (faulty form → correction).

## 9. Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to each topic.

**When one paragraph is enough.** A subject of slight extent, or one treated very briefly, may need no division: a brief description, a brief summary of a literary work, a brief account of a single incident, a narrative merely outlining an action, the setting forth of a single idea. After writing it, check whether dividing it would improve it.

**Ordinarily, divide.** A subject usually needs dividing into topics, each the subject of a paragraph. The object is to aid the reader: the beginning of each paragraph signals that "a new step in the development of the subject has been reached." The amount of division varies with the length of the composition.

**Paragraph plans the book gives:**

- Short notice of a book or poem: a single paragraph.
- Slightly longer notice, two paragraphs: (A) Account of the work. (B) Critical discussion.
- Report on a poem for a literature class, seven paragraphs: (A) Facts of composition and publication. (B) Kind of poem; metrical form. (C) Subject. (D) Treatment of subject. (E) For what chiefly remarkable. (F) Wherein characteristic of the writer. (G) Relationship to other works.
  - C and D vary with the poem. C usually gives the actual or imagined circumstances of the poem (the situation), if they need explaining, then states the subject and outlines its development; for a third-person narrative throughout, C needs only a concise summary of the action. D gives the leading ideas and shows how they are made prominent, or the points in the narrative chiefly emphasized.
- A novel: Setting. Plot. Characters. Purpose.
- A historical event: What led up to the event. Account of the event. What the event led up to.
- For either of these last two, one or more topics will probably need further division.

**Single sentences.** As a rule, a single sentence is not written or printed as a paragraph. The exception is a sentence of transition, showing the relation between the parts of an exposition or argument.

**Dialogue.** Each speech, even a single word, is a paragraph by itself: a new paragraph begins with each change of speaker. Where dialogue and narrative combine, learn the practice from well-printed works of fiction.

## 10. As a rule, begin each paragraph with a topic sentence; end it in conformity with the beginning.

The object again is to aid the reader: he can discover the purpose of each paragraph as he begins it and keep that purpose in mind as he ends it. The most generally useful kind of paragraph, particularly in exposition and argument:

1. the topic sentence comes at or near the beginning;
2. the succeeding sentences explain or establish or develop the statement made in the topic sentence; and
3. the final sentence either emphasizes the thought of the topic sentence or states some important consequence.

Ending with a digression, or with an unimportant detail, "is particularly to be avoided."

**Linking to the whole.** In a larger composition, the paragraph's relation to what precedes, or its function in the whole, may need stating. Sometimes a mere word or phrase in the topic sentence does it (*again*; *therefore*; *for the same reason*). Sometimes one or more sentences of introduction or transition should precede the topic sentence; if more than one is needed, it is generally better to set them apart as a separate paragraph.

**Ways to relate the body to the topic sentence**, chosen by the writer's purpose:

- make its meaning clearer by restating it in other forms, by defining its terms, by denying the converse, or by giving illustrations or specific instances;
- establish it by proofs;
- develop it by showing its implications and consequences.

A long paragraph may use several of these.

**Model paragraph 1: Stevenson, *Walking Tours*** (nine sentences), the function of each:

1. Topic sentence: a walking tour should be gone upon alone.
2. The meaning made clearer by denial of the contrary: in company it becomes something in the nature of a picnic.
3. The topic sentence repeated in abridged form and supported by three reasons (freedom is of the essence; you should stop and go on "as the freak takes you"; you must have your own pace). The third reason is made clearer by denying the converse.
4. A fourth reason, stated in two forms: be open to all impressions.
5. The same reason in still another form ("as a pipe for any wind to play upon").
6–7. The same reason as stated by Hazlitt.
8. A paraphrase of the Hazlitt quotation.
9. A final statement of the fourth reason, "in language amplified and heightened to form a strong conclusion."

**Model paragraph 2: Lecky, *The Political Value of History*** (six sentences):

1. Topic sentence: chiefly in the eighteenth century a very different conception of history grew up.
2. The meaning made clearer; the new conception defined (the historian's task is to solve a problem, not to paint a picture).
3. The definition expanded (morals, industry, intellect, art, manners, beliefs, constitutions).
4. The definition explained by contrast (a history of peoples rather than of kings).
5. The definition supplemented with another element (the chain of causes and effects).
6. Conclusion: an important consequence of the new conception.

**Narration and description.** A paragraph sometimes opens with a concise, comprehensive statement that holds the following details together: *The breeze served us admirably.* *The campaign opened with a series of reverses.* Used too often, this device becomes a mannerism. More commonly the opening sentence simply shows by its subject what the paragraph is principally about: *He picked up the heavy lamp from the table and began to explore.* *Another flight of steps, and they emerged on the roof.* The brief paragraphs of animated narrative often lack even this semblance of a topic sentence; the break between them serves as a rhetorical pause, throwing some detail of the action into prominence.

## 11. Use the active voice.

The active voice is usually more direct and vigorous than the passive. *I shall always remember my first visit to Boston* is much better than *My first visit to Boston will always be remembered by me*, which is less direct, less bold, and less concise. Dropping "by me" makes it indefinite: is it the writer, some person undisclosed, or the world at large that will remember?

**When the passive is right.** The passive is frequently convenient and sometimes necessary. *The dramatists of the Restoration are little esteemed to-day* belongs in a paragraph on the dramatists; *Modern readers have little esteem for the dramatists of the Restoration* belongs in a paragraph on the tastes of modern readers. "The need of making a particular word the subject of the sentence will often... determine which voice is to be used."

**Habitual active voice makes for forcible writing**, in writing of any kind, not only narrative. A tame sentence of description or exposition comes alive when a transitive verb in the active voice replaces a perfunctory expression such as *there is* or *could be heard*:

- *There were a great number of dead leaves lying on the ground.* → *Dead leaves covered the ground.*
- *The sound of the falls could still be heard.* → *The sound of the falls still reached our ears.*
- *The reason that he left college was that his health became impaired.* → *Failing health compelled him to leave college.*
- *It was not long before he was very sorry that he had said what he had.* → *He soon repented his words.*

**Fault: one passive depending directly on another.** The word properly related to the second passive has been made the subject of the first.

- *Gold was not allowed to be exported.* → *It was forbidden to export gold* (*The export of gold was prohibited*).
- *He has been proved to have been seen entering the building.* → *It has been proved that he was seen to enter the building.*

**Fault: the action noun as subject.** The subject of the passive is a noun that expresses the entire action, leaving the verb nothing to do but complete the sentence.

- *A survey of this region was made in 1900.* → *This region was surveyed in 1900.*
- *Mobilization of the army was rapidly carried out.* → *The army was rapidly mobilized.*
- *Confirmation of these reports cannot be obtained.* → *These reports cannot be confirmed.*

The test: in *The export of gold was prohibited*, the predicate *was prohibited* expresses something not implied in *export*, so it stands.

## 12. Put statements in positive form.

Make definite assertions. Avoid tame, colorless, hesitating, non-committal language. "Use the word *not* as a means of denial or in antithesis, never as a means of evasion."

- *He was not very often on time.* → *He usually came late.*
- *He did not think that studying Latin was much use.* → *He thought the study of Latin useless.*
- A remark that *The Taming of the Shrew* is "rather weak in spots," that Katharine is not portrayed as very admirable and Bianca does not remain long in memory → *The women in The Taming of the Shrew are unattractive. Katharine is disagreeable, Bianca insignificant.* The original was indefinite as well as negative, so the correction "is simply a guess at the writer's intention."

The weakness is inherent in *not*: consciously or unconsciously, the reader "is dissatisfied with being told only what is not; he wishes to be told what is." So, as a rule, express a negative in positive form:

- *not honest* → *dishonest*
- *not important* → *trifling*
- *did not remember* → *forgot*
- *did not pay any attention to* → *ignored*
- *did not have much confidence in* → *distrusted*

The antithesis of negative and positive is strong: *Not charity, but simple justice.* *Not that I loved Caesar less, but Rome the more.* Negative words other than *not* are usually strong: *The sun never sets upon the British flag.*

## 13. Omit needless words.

"Vigorous writing is concise." A sentence should contain no unnecessary words and a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or avoid all detail and treat subjects only in outline, "but that every word tell."

**Common expressions that violate it:**

- *the question as to whether* → *whether* (*the question whether*)
- *there is no doubt but that* → *no doubt* (*doubtless*)
- *used for fuel purposes* → *used for fuel*
- *he is a man who* → *he*
- *in a hasty manner* → *hastily*
- *this is a subject which* → *this subject*
- *His story is a strange one.* → *His story is strange.*

**The fact that** "should be revised out of every sentence in which it occurs":

- *owing to the fact that* → *since* (*because*)
- *in spite of the fact that* → *though* (*although*)
- *call your attention to the fact that* → *remind you* (*notify you*)
- *I was unaware of the fact that* → *I was unaware that* (*did not know*)
- *the fact that he had not succeeded* → *his failure*
- *the fact that I had arrived* → *my arrival*

See also *case*, *character*, *nature*, and *system* in [words-misused.md](words-misused.md).

**Who is, which was,** and the like are often superfluous:

- *His brother, who is a member of the same firm* → *His brother, a member of the same firm*
- *Trafalgar, which was Nelson's last battle* → *Trafalgar, Nelson's last battle*

Positive statement is more concise than negative, and the active voice more concise than the passive, so many examples under Rules 11 and 12 illustrate this rule too.

**Fault: one complex idea told step by step.** A series of sentences presents a single complex idea that could be combined into one. The book's example tells the Macbeth story (ambition, the witches, Duncan, the murder, the succession) in six sentences of 55 words; combined, it takes 26: *Encouraged by his wife, Macbeth achieved his ambition and realized the prediction of the witches by murdering Duncan and becoming king of Scotland in his place.*

## 14. Avoid a succession of loose sentences.

The rule refers especially to loose sentences of one type: two co-ordinate clauses, the second introduced by a conjunction or relative. A single sentence of this type may be unexceptionable (Rule 4); a series soon becomes monotonous and tedious.

An unskilful writer sometimes builds a whole paragraph of them, using as connectives *and*, *but*, and less often *who*, *which*, *when*, *where*, and *while* (these last in non-restrictive senses, Rule 3). The book's example is a five-sentence notice of a concert, every sentence two clauses hinged on *and*, *while*, or *when*. Apart from its "triteness and emptiness," it is bad because of its sentence structure, "with their mechanical symmetry and sing-song." For contrast, see the model paragraphs under Rule 10, or any good English prose, such as the preface (*Before the Curtain*) to *Vanity Fair*.

**Fix.** Recast enough of the sentences to remove the monotony, replacing them with simple sentences, sentences of two clauses joined by a semicolon, periodic sentences of two clauses, or sentences, loose or periodic, of three clauses, "whichever best represent the real relations of the thought."

## 15. Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form.

This is the principle of **parallel construction**: "expressions of similar content and function should be outwardly similar." The likeness of form helps the reader recognize the likeness of content and function. Familiar instances from the Bible: the Ten Commandments, the Beatitudes, the petitions of the Lord's Prayer.

The unskilful writer often violates the principle from "a mistaken belief that he should constantly vary the form of his expressions." Varying the form is right when repeating a statement for emphasis (see the Stevenson paragraph under Rule 10); otherwise, keep to parallel construction.

- *Formerly, science was taught by the textbook method, while now the laboratory method is employed.* → *Formerly, science was taught by the textbook method; now it is taught by the laboratory method.*

The first gives the impression that the writer is "undecided or timid," unable or afraid to choose one form of expression and hold to it; the second shows he has made his choice and abided by it.

**Articles and prepositions in a series** must be used either only before the first term or before each term:

- *The French, the Italians, Spanish, and Portuguese* → *The French, the Italians, the Spanish, and the Portuguese*
- *In spring, summer, or in winter* → *In spring, summer, or winter* (*In spring, in summer, or in winter*)

**Correlative expressions** (*both, and*; *not, but*; *not only, but also*; *either, or*; *first, second, third*; and the like) should be followed by the same grammatical construction. Rearranging the sentence corrects many violations:

- *It was both a long ceremony and very tedious.* → *The ceremony was both long and tedious.*
- *A time not for words, but action* → *A time not for words, but for action*
- *Either you must grant his request or incur his ill will.* → *You must either grant his request or incur his ill will.*
- *My objections are, first, the injustice of the measure; second, that it is unconstitutional.* → *My objections are, first, that the measure is unjust; second, that it is unconstitutional.*

See also the Taming of the Shrew example under Rule 12 and the Macbeth example under Rule 13.

**Many similar ideas.** What if a writer has twenty similar ideas: must he write twenty consecutive sentences of one pattern? The difficulty is probably imaginary. The ideas can likely be classified in groups, with the principle applied only within each group. Otherwise, put the statements in the form of a table.

## 16. Keep related words together.

"The position of the words in a sentence is the principal means of showing their relationship." So bring together, as far as possible, the words and groups of words related in thought, and keep apart those not so related.

**Subject and principal verb** should not, as a rule, be separated by a phrase or clause that can be moved to the beginning:

- *Wordsworth, in the fifth book of The Excursion, gives a minute description of this church.* → *In the fifth book of The Excursion, Wordsworth gives a minute description of this church.*
- *Cast iron, when treated in a Bessemer converter, is changed into steel.* → *By treatment in a Bessemer converter, cast iron is changed into steel.*

The objection is that the interposed phrase or clause needlessly interrupts the natural order of the main clause. It does not usually hold when the interruption is only a relative clause or an expression in apposition, nor in periodic sentences where the interruption deliberately creates suspense (Rule 18).

**The relative pronoun** should, as a rule, come immediately after its antecedent:

- *There was a look in his eye that boded mischief.* → *In his eye was a look that boded mischief.*
- *He wrote three articles about his adventures in Spain, which were published in Harper's Magazine.* → *He published in Harper's Magazine three articles about his adventures in Spain.*
- *This is a portrait of Benjamin Harrison, grandson of William Henry Harrison, who became President in 1889.* → *This is a portrait of Benjamin Harrison, grandson of William Henry Harrison. He became President in 1889.*

If the antecedent is a group of words, the relative comes at the end of the group, unless that would cause ambiguity:

- *The Superintendent of the Chicago Division, who* (stands)
- *A proposal to amend the Sherman Act, which has been variously judged* → *A proposal, which has been variously judged, to amend the Sherman Act*, or *A proposal to amend the much-debated Sherman Act*
- *The grandson of William Henry Harrison, who* → *William Henry Harrison's grandson, Benjamin Harrison, who*

A noun in apposition may come between antecedent and relative, since no real ambiguity can arise: *The Duke of York, his brother, who was regarded with hostility by the Whigs.*

**Modifiers** should come, if possible, next to the word they modify. If several expressions modify the same word, arrange them so that no wrong relation is suggested:

- *All the members were not present.* → *Not all the members were present.*
- *He only found two mistakes.* → *He found only two mistakes.*
- *Major R. E. Joyce will give a lecture on Tuesday evening in Bailey Hall, to which the public is invited, on "My Experiences in Mesopotamia" at eight P. M.* → *On Tuesday evening at eight P. M., Major R. E. Joyce will give in Bailey Hall a lecture on "My Experiences in Mesopotamia." The public is invited.*

## 17. In summaries, keep to one tense.

- Summarizing the action of a drama: always the present tense.
- Summarizing a poem, story, or novel: preferably the present, though the past is allowed.
- In a present-tense summary, express antecedent action by the perfect; in a past-tense summary, by the past perfect. (In the book's present-tense example, the plot moment when Friar Lawrence's letter fails to reach Romeo, the earlier action is put in the perfect: Juliet "has been compelled to drink the potion.")
- Whichever tense the summary uses, a past tense in indirect discourse or indirect question stays unchanged: *The Legate inquires who struck the blow.*
- Apart from these exceptions, use the chosen tense throughout. Shifting from one tense to the other "gives the appearance of uncertainty and irresolution" (compare Rule 15).

**Reporting someone else's statements or thought** (summarizing an essay, reporting a speech): avoid interjecting "he said," "he stated," "the speaker added," "the speaker then went on to say," "the author also thinks," or the like. State clearly at the outset, once for all, that what follows is summary, and waste no words repeating it.

**Summary versus criticism.** Summaries may be indispensable in notebooks, newspapers, and handbooks of literature, and retelling a story is a useful exercise for children in primary schools. But in the criticism or interpretation of literature, avoid dropping into summary. One or two sentences may indicate the subject or opening situation of the work; numerous details may be cited to illustrate its qualities. Aim at "an orderly discussion supported by evidence, not a summary with occasional comment." When discussing several works, do better than taking them up singly in chronological order: aim from the beginning at establishing general conclusions.

## 18. Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end.

The proper place for the word or group of words the writer most wants to make prominent is usually the end of the sentence:

- *Humanity has hardly advanced in fortitude since that time, though it has advanced in many other ways.* → *Humanity, since that time, has advanced in many other ways, but it has hardly advanced in fortitude.*
- *This steel is principally used for making razors, because of its hardness.* → *Because of its hardness, this steel is principally used in making razors.*

The words entitled to that position are usually "the logical predicate, that is, the new element in the sentence," as in the second example.

**The periodic sentence** is effective because of the prominence it gives the main statement, held to the close. The book's examples: a long sentence about Columbus, his background and purpose, that ends on *lighted on America*; and an appeal that runs through its qualifying phrases to end on *the vigorous and successful prosecution of this war*.

**The beginning** is the other prominent position. Any element other than the subject becomes emphatic when placed first:

- *Deceit or treachery he could never forgive.*
- *So vast and rude, fretted by the action of nearly three thousand years, the fragments of this architecture may often seem, at first sight, like works of nature.*

A subject coming first may be emphatic, but hardly by its position alone: in *Great kings worshipped at his shrine*, the emphasis on *kings* comes largely from its meaning and the context. For special emphasis, the subject must take the position of the predicate: *Through the middle of the valley flowed a winding stream.*

The principle that the end is the place for what is most prominent applies equally to the words of a sentence, the sentences of a paragraph, and the paragraphs of a composition.
