# The Elements of Style

William Strunk Jr.'s handbook gives "in a brief space the principal requirements of plain English style." It concentrates on a few essentials, the rules of usage and principles of composition most commonly violated, and adds a few matters of form, a list of words and expressions commonly misused, and a list of words commonly misspelled. Its examples run across literary criticism, history, narrative, description, reports, notices, and letters.

## Working with the rules

- **The numbers are the marks.** "The numbers of the sections may be used as references in correcting manuscript." The rules run 1 to 18 straight through: 1–8 are the rules of usage, 9–18 the principles of composition. When correcting a draft, mark each fault with its rule number ("5" on a comma splice, "13" on a needless phrase) and give the corrected form beside it. For a misused word, name the word's entry ("due to," "while").
- **Compensating merit.** The best writers sometimes disregard the rules, but then the reader usually finds "some compensating merit, attained at the cost of the violation." Unless the writer is certain of doing as well, follow the rules. Write plain English adequate for everyday uses first; the secrets of style come afterwards, from study of the masters of literature.
- **Definite statement over vague generality.** Many faults are "not so much bad English as bad style, the commonplaces of careless writing." The correction is often "not the replacement of one word or set of words by another, but the replacement of vague generality by definite statement": say what was sung, not that the singing was "a feature."
- **Revising a draft.** Work down from the whole: paragraphs (9, 10), then sentence shape and force (11–18), then punctuation and grammar (1–8), then the words list, form, and spelling. Rules 3, 4, 5, and 6 are the most important principles of punctuation in ordinary sentences; master them until applying them is second nature.

## Elementary Rules of Usage (1–8)

Each rule with its test. Exceptions, all examples, and the word-division lists: [rules-of-usage.md](rules-of-usage.md).

1. **Form the possessive singular of nouns with 's,** whatever the final consonant: *Charles's friend, Burns's poems.* Exceptions are ancient proper names in -es and -is, *Jesus'*, and *for conscience' sake*; *Achilles' heel* is commonly replaced by *the heel of Achilles*. *Hers, its, theirs, yours, oneself* take no apostrophe.
2. **In a series of three or more terms with a single conjunction, use a comma after each term except the last:** *red, white, and blue.* Business firm names drop the last comma (*Brown, Shipley and Company*). A comma always precedes *etc.*
3. **Enclose parenthetic expressions between commas.** If the interruption is slight, both commas may go; never omit one and leave the other. **Non-restrictive** clauses (introduced by *which, when, where*) are set off: they "do not limit the application of the words on which they depend," and the sentence splits into two independent statements. **Restrictive** clauses are not set off: *The candidate who best meets these requirements will obtain the place* cannot be split. Before a conjunction, put the first comma before the conjunction.
4. **Place a comma before *and* or *but* introducing an independent clause.** Such a sentence makes complete sense at the comma, so the second clause looks like an afterthought, and *and* is "the least specific of connectives": it shows a relation without defining it. Where the relation is cause and result or concession, rewrite with *as* or *although*, or a phrase. An occasional loose sentence gives relief from a too formal style; too many break Rule 14.
5. **Do not join independent clauses by a comma.** Two grammatically complete clauses with no conjunction take a semicolon, or become two sentences. With a conjunction, a comma (Rule 4). With an adverb (*accordingly, besides, so, then, therefore, thus*), the semicolon is still required. Very short clauses alike in form may take a comma: *Man proposes, God disposes.*
6. **Do not break sentences in two:** do not use periods for commas. An emphatic word may stand as a sentence (*Again and again he called out. No reply.*) only when the emphasis is warranted and cannot be mistaken for a blunder.
7. **A participial phrase at the beginning of a sentence must refer to the grammatical subject.** The same holds for an opening phrase after a conjunction or preposition, a noun in apposition, an adjective, or an adjective phrase. *On arriving in Chicago, his friends met him* becomes *When he arrived in Chicago, his friends met him.* Violations are often ludicrous: *Being in a dilapidated condition, I was able to buy the house very cheap.*
8. **Divide words at line-ends, in accordance with their formation and pronunciation.** Never cut off a single letter, or only two letters of a long word. Divide by formation (*know-ledge*), "on the vowel" (*edi-ble*), and between double letters unless they end the simple form (*refer-ring*, but *tell-ing*).

## Elementary Principles of Composition (9–18)

Each principle with its test. The paragraph plans, the analyzed model paragraphs, and every before-and-after pair: [principles-of-composition.md](principles-of-composition.md).

9. **Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to each topic.** A brief subject may take one paragraph; ordinarily divide the subject into topics, one paragraph each, since the beginning of a paragraph signals "a new step in the development of the subject." As a rule, a single sentence is not a paragraph, except a sentence of transition. In dialogue, each change of speaker begins a paragraph.
10. **As a rule, begin each paragraph with a topic sentence; end it in conformity with the beginning.** The most generally useful paragraph puts the topic sentence at or near the beginning, lets the following sentences explain, establish, or develop it, and ends by emphasizing its thought or stating an important consequence. Never end on a digression or an unimportant detail. The body relates to the topic sentence by restating it, defining its terms, denying the converse, giving illustrations, proving it, or showing its implications and consequences.
11. **Use the active voice.** It is "usually more direct and vigorous than the passive." Keep the passive where the sentence must make a particular word its subject. Replace perfunctory *there is* and *could be heard* with a transitive verb in the active voice (*Dead leaves covered the ground*). Two faults: one passive depending directly on another, and a passive whose subject is a noun expressing the entire action (*A survey of this region was made* → *This region was surveyed*).
12. **Put statements in positive form.** "Make definite assertions." Avoid "tame, colorless, hesitating, non-committal language." "Use the word *not* as a means of denial or in antithesis, never as a means of evasion": the reader "wishes to be told what is" (*did not remember* → *forgot*). The antithesis of negative and positive is strong; negative words other than *not* are usually strong.
13. **Omit needless words.** "Vigorous writing is concise." A sentence should hold no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, as a drawing has no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. This asks not that every sentence be short or every subject outlined, "but that every word tell." Revise *the fact that* out of every sentence in which it occurs; cut superfluous *who is, which was*; combine a single complex idea told step by step across several sentences into one.
14. **Avoid a succession of loose sentences**: two co-ordinate clauses, the second introduced by a conjunction or relative (*and, but, who, which, when, where, while*). One is unexceptionable; a series produces "mechanical symmetry and sing-song." Recast enough of them, using simple sentences, two clauses joined by a semicolon, periodic sentences of two clauses, or sentences of three clauses, "whichever best represent the real relations of the thought."
15. **Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form.** **Parallel construction**: expressions of similar content and function should be outwardly similar, so the reader recognizes their likeness. Varying the form for its own sake makes the writer seem "undecided or timid." An article or preposition applying to a whole series goes before the first term only or before every term. Correlatives (*both, and; not, but; not only, but also; either, or; first, second, third*) take the same grammatical construction after each member.
16. **Keep related words together.** "The position of the words in a sentence is the principal means of showing their relationship." Keep subject and principal verb together unless the interruption is a relative clause, an appositive, or deliberate suspense in a periodic sentence. Place a relative pronoun immediately after its antecedent, and a modifier next to the word it modifies (*He found only two mistakes*).
17. **In summaries, keep to one tense.** Summarize a drama in the present; a poem, story, or novel preferably in the present. Shifting tenses gives "the appearance of uncertainty and irresolution." State once at the outset that what follows is summary, then drop every "he said" and "the author also thinks." In criticism, write "an orderly discussion supported by evidence, not a summary with occasional comment."
18. **Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end.** What should stand out most is usually "the logical predicate, that is, the new element in the sentence." The beginning is the other prominent position: any element other than the subject becomes emphatic when placed first. The same principle governs the sentences of a paragraph and the paragraphs of a composition.
