# A Few Matters of Form; Words Commonly Misspelled

## Headings

Leave a blank line, or its equivalent in space, after the title or heading of a manuscript. On succeeding pages, if using ruled paper, begin on the first line.

## Numerals

Do not spell out dates or other serial numbers. Write them in figures or in Roman notation, as appropriate: *August 9, 1918*; *Chapter XII*; *Rule 3*; *352d Infantry*.

## Parentheses

Punctuate a sentence containing a parenthesis, outside the marks, exactly as if the parenthesis were absent. Punctuate the expression inside as if it stood by itself, except that its final stop is omitted unless it is a question mark or an exclamation point.

- *I went to his house yesterday (my third attempt to see him), but he had left town.*
- *He declares (and why should we doubt his good faith?) that he is now certain of success.*

When a wholly detached expression or sentence is parenthesized, the final stop comes before the closing mark of parenthesis.

## Quotations

- **Formal quotations cited as documentary evidence:** introduced by a colon and enclosed in quotation marks. *The provision of the Constitution is: "No tax or duty shall be laid on articles exported from any state."*
- **Quotations in apposition, or the direct objects of verbs:** preceded by a comma and enclosed in quotation marks. *I recall the maxim of La Rochefoucauld, "Gratitude is a lively sense of benefits to come."* *Aristotle says, "Art is an imitation of nature."*
- **An entire line or more of verse:** begun on a fresh line and centred, without quotation marks. (*Wordsworth's enthusiasm for the Revolution was at first unbounded:* followed by the two lines of verse, set off and centred.)
- **Quotations introduced by *that*:** indirect discourse, no quotation marks. *Keats declares that beauty is truth, truth beauty.*
- **Proverbial expressions and familiar phrases of literary origin:** no quotation marks. *These are the times that try men's souls.* *He lives far from the madding crowd.* The same holds for colloquialisms and slang.

## References

- In scholarly work requiring exact references, abbreviate titles that occur frequently and give the full forms in an alphabetical list at the end.
- As a general practice, give references in parenthesis or in footnotes, not in the body of the sentence.
- Omit the words *act*, *scene*, *line*, *book*, *volume*, *page*, except when referring by only one of them.
- Punctuation by example: *In the second scene of the third act* → *In III.ii* (still better, simply insert *III.ii* in parenthesis at the proper place in the sentence). *After the killing of Polonius, Hamlet is placed under guard (IV. ii. 14).* *2 Samuel i:17-27.* *Othello II.iii 264-267, III.iii. 155-161.*

## Titles

For titles of literary works, scholarly usage prefers italics with capitalized initials. Editors and publishers vary: some use italics with capitalized initials, others Roman with capitalized initials, with or without quotation marks. Use italics (shown in manuscript by underscoring), except when writing for a periodical that follows a different practice. Omit an initial *A* or *The* when you place the possessive before the title.

*The Iliad*; *the Odyssey*; *As You Like It*; *To a Skylark*; *The Newcomes*; *A Tale of Two Cities*; *Dickens's Tale of Two Cities*.

## Words Commonly Misspelled

accidentally · advice · affect · beginning · believe · benefit · challenge · criticize · deceive · definite · describe · despise · develop · disappoint · duel · ecstasy · effect · existence · fiery · formerly · humorous · hypocrisy · immediately · incidentally · latter · led · lose · marriage · mischief · murmur · necessary · occurred · parallel · Philip · playwright · preceding · prejudice · principal · privilege · pursue · repetition · rhyme · rhythm · ridiculous · sacrilegious · seize · separate · shepherd · siege · similar · simile · too · tragedy · tries · undoubtedly · until

**Hyphens and two-word forms:**

- Write *to-day*, *to-night*, *to-morrow* (but not *together*) with a hyphen.
- Write *any one*, *every one*, *some one*, *some time* (except in the sense of *formerly*) as two words.

## Books recommended for reference or further study

- With the rules of usage and matters of form: F. Howard Collins, *Author and Printer*; Chicago University Press, *Manual of Style*; T. L. De Vinne, *Correct Composition*; Horace Hart, *Rules for Compositors and Printers*; George McLane Wood, *Extracts from the Style-Book of the Government Printing Office*.
- With the principles of composition and the misused words: Sir Arthur Quiller-Couch, *The Art of Writing*, especially the chapter "Interlude on Jargon"; George McLane Wood, *Suggestions to Authors*; John Leslie Hall, *English Usage*; James P. Kelly, *Workmanship in Words*.
