# Social sources: motivation (3) and ability (4)

Tactics for when the people around the target, including you, either encourage the wrong behavior (source 3) or fail to enable the right one (source 4). "No source of influence is more powerful and accessible than the persuasive power of the people who make up our social networks." Influencers enlist it rather than lament it. They ensure the right people provide encouragement, coaching and accountability during crucial moments.

## Source 3. Social motivation: provide encouragement

**The principle:** "ensure that people feel praised, emotionally supported, and otherwise encouraged by those around them every time they enact vital behaviors," and "discouraged or even socially sanctioned when they choose unhealthy behaviors."

The power is enormous. In Milgram's obedience study, psychologists predicted that 1.2 percent of subjects would deliver the maximum shock; 65 percent did, prodded only by a man in a lab coat. Three best practices harness that power for good: the power of one, formal and opinion leaders, and new norms.

### The power of one

Across more than a thousand subjects, the variable that mattered most was "the presence of one more person." When a confederate gave the full shock first, compliance rose to 90 percent; when the confederate refused, it fell to 10 percent. So "find or better yet, *be*—the one respected individual who flies in the face of what everyone else has done ... and model the new and healthier vital behaviors."

- A defense-contractor CEO told 200 managers, "I've been told that I'm unapproachable ... I'd appreciate feedback." One manager, Ken, raised his hand. The CEO met him privately and, with Ken's permission, e-mailed everyone the feedback. He committed to changes, thanked Ken, and stayed non-defensive. Candor spread across the company within months. Lip service or a speech would have doomed it; he "encouraged candor, embraced it, celebrated it, and rewarded the first person."
- Modeling works only "to the extent that the person who is modeling the vital behaviors is truly respected." A plywood-mill president's video endorsement of a training program drew jeers from employees who saw him as a hypocrite. Some messengers "actually poison the project."
- A nurse manager opens each staff meeting with "the good, the bad, and the ugly," starting with her own. She answers others' bad news with "Thanks for noticing that!"

### Lead the way: sacrifice

"The first place to look for social influence is in the mirror." People asked to change first ask, "Why should I follow *you?*"

**The sad truth:** "in an environment of mistrust (i.e., the known universe), all ambiguous behaviors are interpreted negatively. And by the way, all behaviors are ambiguous." A CEO proudly showed off rebranded coffee mugs, and an employee concluded, "It means there will be layoffs!" because the new mugs were smaller. People read your behavior to confirm their existing mistrust, so you must generate "clear, unambiguous evidence."

**Sacrifice breathes life into dead values.** "Give it time" is largely wrong; *sacrifice* is the influence accelerant. Mohammad Siddiqui inherited a cynical workforce at MTN South Sudan after the currency lost 85 percent of its value. His vital behavior: "speak up irrespective of the level or position of the person you need to address." He listened, and publicly said "I am sorry." On weekends he and his seven-year-old daughter visited the homes of informal leaders, starting with a janitor 10 levels below him. Within months he was seen as a man who *deserved* support. You make credible a new value by sacrificing old ones. Four kinds of sacrifice work:

1. **Time.** People wanted to know how *long* he stayed; "there is no such thing as *quality* time." An hour over tea says what five minutes cannot.
2. **Money.** After 9/11, Hertz let the authors drive 2,500 miles home free, with no drop-off fee. "Anyone who sacrifices money to serve customers cares about customers."
3. **Ego.** "You're going to screw up ... You'll be a hypocrite." Liz, a facility manager in Kuala Lumpur, snapped at an audience question. The next week she stepped from behind the podium and said, "Last week I behaved despicably ... I beg your forgiveness ... I will not do that again." The apology built more trust than flawless behavior would have, because it subordinated "ego to integrity."
4. **Previous priorities.** Dain Hancock of Lockheed Martin was seen as all about image. His secretary interrupted an employee feedback session: "The prince is here two hours early" (a multibillion-dollar F-16 order). He sent the COO and kept listening, and the story crossed the 13,000-person facility in minutes. "By sacrificing a previously important value for a newer one, the newer one gained credibility."

### Engage formal and opinion leaders

- **Formal leaders.** Engage the chain of command. Spend disproportionate time with formal leaders, and give them specific plans to regularly teach, model, praise and hold people accountable for the vital behaviors.
- **Opinion leaders** (Everett Rogers). Rogers couldn't sell Iowa farmers better corn as a university "city slicker." His first adopter, a farmer in Bermuda shorts who drove a Cadillac, made things worse. "The merit of an idea did not predict its adoption rate." Lime juice prevented scurvy in 1601 yet took almost 200 years to spread. Who adopts first is what matters:
  - *Innovators* are open to new ideas and smarter than average, but visibly different. "Find out who these innovators are *and avoid them like the plague*. If they embrace your new idea, it will surely die."
  - *Early adopters*, many of them *opinion leaders*, are about 13.5 percent of the population. They are smarter than average and open, and above all socially *connected* and *respected*. "The rest of the population—over 85 percent—will not adopt the new practices *until opinion leaders do*."
- **Choose messengers by standing, not just expertise.** "The message ... is no more important than the messenger" (Donald Hopkins). Guinea worm teams start with the chief, who names respected members of each clan to teach. IHI engages the physicians' *guilds*. Markman trains clergy to teach couples. Mao's 1.8 million "barefoot doctors" were locals, recommended by peers, committed to serving, and slightly better educated than their neighbors. A plant manager made foremen (formal leaders) and union officials (opinion leaders) co-lead a quality initiative, and the officials had credibility no foreman could match. Danny Meyer makes respected "hospitality legend" servers mentors to new hires.
- **Media characters become opinion leaders.** After the Indian drama *Tinka, Tinka Sukh*, villagers vowed to educate daughters and delay marriage. Its epilogue had a respected community figure ask questions and call for action.
- **Find them by asking.** "Ask people to make a list of the employees who they believe are the most influential and respected. Then gather the lists and identify those who are named most frequently." In a 10,000-person company, find the roughly 500 who matter; in a team of 20, the 2 or 3. "Spend disproportionate time with them. Listen to their concerns. Build trust with them. Be open to their ideas. Rely on them to share your ideas." You have no choice about involving them: "By definition, they will always be engaged," judging your plan thumbs-up or thumbs-down. "Ignore opinion leaders at your own peril."

### Create new norms

A warden's answer to the fastest way to change behavior: "Laugh at them." Social cues tell people what is *normal*, and unhealthy norms are among the greatest barriers to change. You must "create *a new sense of normal*," in two ways:

**1. Make the undiscussable discussable.** Unhealthy norms are "almost always sustained by what we call a culture of silence." In neonatal units, everyone whispered stories of the physician who skipped gowning and the nurse who clipped a glove fingertip to find a baby's vein. The norm was "deferring to power players ... is more important than protecting patients." In a project-management study, 88 percent of respondents worked on projects they expected to fail ("a slow-motion train wreck"). Yet fewer than 1 in 10 said it was politically acceptable to speak openly about what was going wrong. "If you can't talk about it, you can't change it." "Changes in behavior must be preceded by changes in the public discourse."

- Use stories rather than preaching to move a topic into the open. *Tinka, Tinka Sukh*'s likable family debated the pros and cons of child marriage at home while thousands listened, and a narrator "merely asked questions."
- A chief medical officer read 50 collected patient horror stories one Friday night. By Monday, physicians' indifference to treating patients, previously only whispered about, was being openly debated.
- Spectrum Health's physician leaders phoned every doctor who hadn't completed mandatory training on a new prescription system. That broke the "professional prerogatives" norm, and over 90 percent adopted it within days.
- "If you want to change an old norm, you have to talk about the old norm. You have to talk about the new norm. You have to talk." Make it safe to discuss concerns about the change itself.

**2. Create 200 percent accountability.** "The pace of change is determined by the speed with which you can get everyone to hold everyone accountable." Everyone is responsible "not just to *enact* the vital behaviors—but to *hold others accountable* for them as well." Delancey Street immerses residents in "an unrelenting wave of social praise and social punishment" from peers who have lived the same life. Graduation applause comes from all 500 residents, and 20 to 30 formal and informal leaders know everything happening with each resident. Change becomes "the path of least resistance." The same move worked elsewhere: villagers holding every infected neighbor away from the water, everyone confronting anyone who skips hand hygiene, and brothel managers and opinion leaders enforcing 100 percent condom use. "New norms take hold the instant people begin to defend them."

## Source 4. Social ability: provide assistance

Encouragement isn't enough "when people need permission, information, coaching, or hands-on help." Build *social capital*, "the profound enabling power of an essential network of relationships." Ask what "help, authority, consent, or cooperation" people need to succeed in crucial moments. Even behaviors done alone and in private, like dieting, go better with an enabling group: Bandura's cholesterol subjects did better when their spouses joined the program.

Muhammad Yunus (Grameen Bank) shows the core move. His vital behaviors were securing a business loan and repaying it. Every borrower forms a group of five neighbors who submit plans and cosign each other's loans, so they co-invent plans and "don't put up with any half-baked ideas." Tanika's group turned her wig-making sideline into a business: children traded the hair from family hairbrushes for cheap plastic toys, and she resold it. Ninety-eight percent repay with full interest. Groups really can be "smarter than the smartest people in them." Galton's 787 fairgoers averaged 1,197 pounds for an ox that weighed 1,198. The five women weren't pooling ignorance; they knew their village.

Provide assistance in these circumstances:

### When others are part of the problem

When people feed one another's bad habits, "the group would have to be involved in changing it." Turn "a *me* problem into a *we* problem." Fight the urge to attack the people contributing to the problem; co-opt them instead.

- In one 1,500-person software group, everyone played "project chicken": claiming to be on schedule while hoping someone else would ask for more time first. Training, opinion leaders and surveys hadn't fixed it, because managers and even the VP had rewarded good-news-only reporting. The fix: train people to hold high-stakes conversations about project problems, with each manager teaching the class to his or her own people. That was the very manager who had suppressed candor. Engineers spoke up by the third session, and within a year releases came in on time and on budget.
- Garth Japhet's *Soul City* TV drama, against violence toward women in South Africa, showed neighbors silently banging pots and pans outside an abuser's door. Real townships copied it. "If bad behavior is reinforced by a web of players, all the players have to be engaged."
- In personal change, turn enablers (the friends and family who stock the junk food) into helpers through genuine dialogue rather than recrimination.

### When you can't succeed on your own

- **Interdependence.** When no one person can succeed alone, build the ability to work as a team. A plant's switch to just-in-time inventory exposed that stacked inventory had masked people's inability to cooperate; bickering stopped only after everyone was trained in interpersonal problem solving.
- **Novelty.** Novel or turbulent problems need multiple heads. Yunus's groups meet and brainstorm every week: "synergy through forced interaction." When problems call for creativity and multiple views, place people in teams.
- **Risk.** The higher the stakes, the more assistance is needed. Delancey Street has no professional staff, yet its movers have never had a loss or theft, and recovering addicts serve alcohol in its restaurant without relapse. The structure behind this:
  - "Each one teach one": a resident of a few days takes the next newcomer "under your wing," and is then asked how the crew is doing rather than how they are doing.
  - Nine-person mixed-race dorms, and *minyans* of 10 under a minyan leader.
  - A *barber* (from "haircut," a scolding) who ensures everyone challenges everyone.
  - Peer crew bosses and peer tutoring, which takes residents from a seventh-grade education to high-school equivalency.
  - Couples' groups taught by slightly more experienced couples, and chaperones on first dates.

  "Teachers learn more than students, mentors more than mentees." Turn your experienced people into coaches, trainers, instructors and mentors.
- **Blind spots.** "Since you're on the wrong side of your eyeballs," seek real-time feedback from an expert. A speech coach's live corrections ("Pick up the speed by 10 percent." "Pause after the word 'successful.'") transformed Lauren, an executive with "the uncanny ability to whip a crowd into a nap," in four hours. At crucial moments, buddy up: police call for backup, recovering spendaholics shop with a sponsor, new teachers pair up for difficult parents.
- **Group solidarity.** Some behaviors are all-or-nothing. If one person breaks ranks, the effort collapses, as in the "tragedy of the commons."
  - New supervisors trained to confront violations were ridiculed as too tough while some colleagues held back, until every supervisor promised in advance to step up.
  - A client turned away by one sex worker simply goes to another, so Wiwat met first with every sex-business owner, then with every worker. He explained why everyone had to participate. Compliance went from about 14 percent to over 90.
  - Parents' "no means no" works only when both stand unified.
  - A shelter beat bedbugs only by treating every room and washing every item on the same day.
  - Menlo Innovations paired programmers at one computer. Time spent fixing errors went from 40 percent to none, knowledge silos disappeared, and "we've never had to turn down a vacation request."

For source 4 overall: the myth of the rugged individualist dampens people's willingness to draw on others. Supply the concrete assistance each crucial moment needs.
