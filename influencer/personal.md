# Personal sources: motivation (1) and ability (2)

Tactics for when the diagnosis finds the vital behavior is unpleasant to the people who must do it (source 1), or they can't yet do it (source 2). The two often look alike: check whether "won't" is really "can't" before adding motivation.

## Source 1. Personal motivation: help them love what they hate

The core problem: "good behaviors feel bad while bad behaviors feel good." Yet people can learn to enjoy what isn't inherently enjoyable. Hand washing can feel like a tedious distraction or a sacred duty, and finishing tasks on time like a bureaucratic bore or a demonstration of integrity. The work is to infuse the crucial moment with moral significance, because the person is in *moral slumber*, not morally defective.

**The one-sentence test (Biff).** Ask: "If you had to craft one sentence to influence Biff, what would it be?" Then read your lips. Most sentences are "some form of nag, guilt, or threat." They come from two automatic leaps: the person isn't motivated, and the reason is a moral defect. Coercion rarely works, and "the more we push others to comply, the less it works," especially with people *addicted* to the wrong behavior.

Four tactics, in this order:

### Tactic 1. Allow for choice

This is "the gateway to all other methods of influencing personal motivation. Compulsion first replaces then erases motivation." There is no commitment without permission to say no. Peter Block: *Your "yes" means nothing if you can't say "no."*

- **Motivational interviewing** (William Miller). Miller found that therapy length and method barely mattered for addicts, and confrontation actually *increased* bingeing in one study. What works: "replace judgment with empathy, and lectures with questions." Use "open and nondirective questions" so people reach their own conclusions about what they value and what changes living by those values would require. "The instant you stop trying to impose your agenda on others, you eliminate the fight for control." Leaders get the same results on safety, cost cutting and productivity "when they replace dictates with dialogue."
- **Listen, then frame the change in their terms.** Ralph Heath had 18 months to move the F-22 to production with 4,500 engineers. He "didn't sell; he listened," interviewing for weeks, then framed directives around their needs and goals.
- **Ask for help rather than mandating.** Facing three-shift, seven-day weeks over the holidays, Guidant CEO Ginger Graham did several things in turn. She praised the work, shared the sales data, read doctors' stories of patients saved, and showed the unmet need. Then she asked employees to rise to the challenge and promised to make their lives easier. Within half an hour employees had listed what management could do (shop for presents, late-night taxis, pizza). Sales almost tripled in a quarter. "A change of heart can't be imposed; it can only be chosen."

### Tactic 2. Create direct experiences

*Direct experience* is "the gold standard of tactics for engaging personal motivation." "Let people feel, see, and touch things for themselves." People abstracted from consequences, by "numbers, statistics, and charts which sometimes inform but never inspire," wake up when they meet the consequences firsthand. "Transform numbers into names, statistics, faces, and charts into human conditions."

- **Field trips to the consequences.** Berwick told hospital CEOs: "find an injured patient, someone who was harmed while in your hospital. And *you* investigate the injury. Do not delegate it." They returned in tears and stayed safety champions for their whole careers. A TI Automotive team spent a day fixing the home of a coworker disabled on the job, and safety rules "became moral commitments."
- **Invite people to try it.** People are "crystal clear about what they'll lose by changing but uncertain about what they'll gain." They also mispredict what will make them happy: Daniel Gilbert's subjects bet on $30,000 more income over a daily 30-minute walk, and the walk is the better bet. Describing the benefits fails because people have no frame of reference for them. Ask them to try it instead. Mimi Silbert: "You can hate opera, but not until after you've *been to an opera.* ... Coming in, our residents hate everything. But of course they've never done *anything!*" Over 90 percent come to enjoy dozens of behaviors they never imagined they would.

### Tactic 3. Tell meaningful stories

Use stories when direct experience can't reach everyone or people refuse to "just try it." A *vicarious experience* lies between verbal persuasion and direct experience.

- **Bandura's snake phobics** show the sequence. First, a clear, measurable result: sitting with a six-foot boa constrictor across the lap. Never rush or coerce; offer options so subjects stay "in control at all times." Next, subjects watch a model handle the snake, from the doorway or through glass. Then come graduated direct steps: enter, wear protective gear, stand by the tank, lift the lid, touch with gloves, then without. Lifelong phobias were cured in three hours, and subjects gained confidence that they could make other changes.
- **Put a face on the numbers, then give the logic.** Berwick opens with Josie King, 18 months old, who died of thirst and misused narcotics at Johns Hopkins despite her mother's pleas. "Now that medical mistakes have a face, a name, and a soul ... Dr. Berwick digs into the logic and order of the topic."
- **Stories must be poignant, believable and compelling**, with believable models the audience identifies with. The radio serial *Twende na Wakati* in Tanzania (Martha Swai, with entertainment-education expert David Poindexter) followed the philandering truck driver Mkwaju and his independent wife Tutu. One listener, a father, saw "a part of him was Mkwaju" and stopped abusing alcohol and his family. In the first controlled national field experiment, one-fourth of the broadcast population changed behavior to avoid HIV. Stories change "people's moral arithmetic about their choices."
- **Everyday use.** Biff's supervisor didn't nag. She described a three-year-old sweeping her hand across a ketchup-smeared table and licking it, and Biff grabbed a rag before the story ended. Gold-mine drivers each told an accident story with a title ("Family man runs over and kills his neighbor's little girl"), and speeding moved from a legal to a moral issue. Leaders who build a sense of mission "were *always* storytellers."
- A story alone is ephemeral. Combine it with the other sources.

### Tactic 4. Make it a game

"Almost any activity can be made engaging if it involves reasonably challenging goals and clear, frequent feedback" (Csikszentmihalyi's "flow"). Take the scoreboard off a basketball court and see how long fans or players last. Build in four elements:

- **Keeping score**: clear, frequent feedback that turns tasks into accomplishments.
- **Competition**: Am I doing better than *before*? Than *others*? The second "can lead to unhealthy rivalry"; competition with oneself is the safer engine.
- **Constant improvement**: watch the slope. "The actual numbers ... aren't as important as the overall slope. In what direction are you heading?"
- **Control**: points and rewards must be in the participant's control. Avoid folding a person's or team's progress into a larger unit's results; "Create and record measures over which individuals have complete control. Let them see the impact of their work."

## Source 2. Personal ability: help them do what they can't

Leaders "assume that *knowing* the training content and *doing* it are one and the same." Training full of theories, models and cases with no practice changes little. "Many of the profound and persistent problems we face stem more from a lack of skill (which in turn stems from a lack of deliberate practice) than from a genetic curse, a lack of courage, or a character flaw."

- **Much of will is skill.** In Walter Mischel's marshmallow studies, four-year-old "delayers" later scored 210 points higher on the SAT. But Mischel and Bandura showed that "grabbers" who watched adult models nap or distract themselves became stars at delaying after one exposure, and kept the skill for months. Delayers are "simply more *skilled* at avoiding short-term temptations."
- **Much of prowess is practice.** Anders Ericsson found elite performance comes from *deliberate practice*, not genes: "*perfect* practice makes perfect." Olympic-hopeful skaters practice skills they haven't mastered, club skaters practice skills they have, and amateurs spend half their rink time chatting. Time in a profession doesn't correlate with elite performance. Software engineers plateau about five years in; a 20-year surgeon isn't better than a 5-year one by virtue of time. "It's the *skill* of practice that makes perfect." Most activities hit "arrested development" after about 50 hours, once they become automatic.
- **Fixed vs growth mindset** (Carol Dweck). Believing you can't improve creates a self-fulfilling prophecy; people need to learn how to learn.
- **Interpersonal skills need practice most and get it least.** Examples: speaking up when a surgeon errs, disagreeing with a boss, confronting an abusive spouse, saying no to drugs. "Speaking up to an authority figure requires skill, and skill requires practice." Wiwat had seasoned sex workers teach young ones actual scripts for holding the line without offending a customer, and practice them with feedback until confident. Condom compliance rose from 14 to 90 percent. Ghana gold-mine drivers were speeding because executives cut airport trips too close. Their vital behavior was calling the executive an hour ahead. They scripted the call, practiced it (including what to say if the leader was late anyway), and role-played it with the actual senior leaders.

### Deliberate practice: four elements

1. **Demand full attention for brief intervals.** It needs "steely-eyed concentration," usually sustainable for about an hour at a stretch. Elite performers rarely exceed five hours of practice a day.
2. **Provide immediate feedback against a clear standard.** Feedback matters far more than hours. Chess players compare each move to a master's and study what they missed. Natalie Coughlin counts strokes per lap and adjusts her hand pitch. Ethna Reid's teachers test right after teaching. Leadership skills need the same treatment: addressing controversial topics, confronting bad behavior, building coalitions, running a meeting, disagreeing with authority, influencing change. Each is a performance to practice, not a case to read.
3. **Break mastery into mini goals.** Stroke patients skipped pills with nasty side effects until given pill packets, a blood-pressure monitor and a logbook for daily recording: small goals they could see and control. Top performers set specific *process* goals, not outcome goals. Free-throw shooters above 70 percent think "Keep the elbow in," "Follow through"; those at 55 percent or below think "make 10 in a row." After misses, masters name the technique they got wrong, while poor shooters say "I lost concentration." For fearful learners, Bandura's rule: give "short-term, specific, easy, and low-stakes goals that specify the exact steps." "Take complex tasks and make them simple; long tasks and make them short; vague tasks and make them specific; and high-stakes tasks and make them risk free." At Delancey, a newcomer's first lesson is setting a table, maybe just the forks.
4. **Prepare for setbacks; build in resilience.** Only easy early wins create a false expectation, and the first failure then discourages. Gradually introduce tasks needing more effort and persistence. Teach people to read setbacks as *guides*, not brakes: "Aha! I just discovered what doesn't work," not "Oh no! Once again I'm an utter failure." Failure signals more effort, or a change of tactics, and rarely that success is impossible. If you're staring at a half-eaten ice cream cone, change your route home past the parlor.

Quick example: executives launching a quality initiative feared employees would call it "the flavor of the month." They listed the four or five toughest questions they'd face, then practiced two-minute answers in pairs: deliver, get feedback, deliver again. Half an hour gave them a consistent, credible voice.

### Build emotional skills: go and know

People run on two systems. The *hot* or *go system* (amygdala) is fast, reflexive and built for fight or flight. The *cool* or *know system* (frontal lobe) is slow, contemplative and handles higher reasoning. The go system fires at mere hints of threat (a colleague mocks your idea) and at cravings (fresh doughnuts: "Eat now before it's too late"). That is why people committed to a vital behavior crumble at stressful moments. Moving from go to know is a learnable *intrapersonal* skill.

- **People won't attempt a behavior unless (1) they think it's worth it and (2) they think they can do what's required.** Children wouldn't wait for the second marshmallow if they doubted the researcher would return.
- **Distract, and focus on the task, not the reward.** Delayers covered their eyes, turned their chairs away, sang and invented games. Children taught these tactics delayed longer, while those who glanced at the reward most were least persistent. Exhorting "willpower" (dig down, suck it up) didn't work, and neither did dwelling on the cost of failure. Better still: "transform the difficult into the easy, the aversive into the pleasant, and the boring into the interesting," using structure (see structural.md) and short-term goals.
- **Cognitive reappraisal: argue with your feelings.** Give the frontal lobe a problem to solve, using four moves:
  - **Label** the urge ("I have a craving for a cream cheese-covered bagel. Bad.").
  - **Debate** it with a competing goal ("What I *really* want is to be proud of myself after lunch when I write down what I ate.").
  - **Distract** with a potent image (your belt feeling loose).
  - **Delay**: "the go system can often be outwaited." Therapists teach obsessive-compulsives to wait 15 minutes.

  These moves "change what you think ... by changing *where* you think."
- Wealth managers who avoided giving clients bad news practiced recognizing the emotion and reframing it around the positive potential of addressing the issue respectfully. Client loyalty and business rose.

**For source 2 overall:** spend more time on practice than you think you'll need. Use realistic conditions, coaching and feedback. Break vital behaviors into smaller actions people can judge themselves on. Practice recovering from setbacks, and practice managing the emotions that undermine change, so vital behaviors become vital habits.
