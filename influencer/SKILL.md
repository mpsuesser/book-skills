# Influencer

*Influence* is the ability to create changes in human behavior; *influencers* are the people who do it. What qualifies people as leaders is their capacity to influence others to change their behavior in order to achieve important results. This is not persuasion or verbal tricks: success "has little to do with pep talks, bribes, or sermons" and relies on "the capacity to systematically create rapid, profound, and sustainable changes in a handful of key behaviors." The authors found it learnable by studying people who succeeded where most fail: fewer than one in eight workplace change efforts produce anything other than cynicism, and fewer than 10 percent of people succeed at changing habits like overspending or inadequate exercise.

Three keys run every influence strategy, in order:

1. **Focus and measure.** Be crystal clear about the result and zealous about measuring it.
2. **Find vital behaviors.** Focus on the two or three high-leverage actions that produce the greatest amount of change.
3. **Engage all six sources of influence.** *Overdetermine change*: identify every force shaping the behavior and get each working for you rather than against you. Influencers who get all six sources working in their favor increase their odds of success *tenfold*.

The user's situation may be a company, a hospital, a school, a village, a family, or their own habit. The method is the same for all of them. Work the steps below with the user's own challenge in hand.

## Step 1. Focus and measure

Most influence attempts fail at the outset through one of three early mistakes:

- **Fuzzy, uncompelling goals**: "Empower our employees," "Help inner city kids," "Build the team." "Improving customer service" could mean anything from answering by the second ring to giving a free monkey with every purchase over $10.
- **Infrequent or no measures**: the leader relies on "anecdotal evidence and gut impressions rather than reliable measures," like the boot-camp chief who knew recruits' confidence improved because one introduced him to his parents.
- **Bad measures**: measuring the wrong variable drives the wrong behavior. Soviet nail factories measured on tonnage made railroad spikes; measured on units, they made billions of tiny brads. Charities that count services delivered focus on activities, not results.

Do this:

- **Dig until you find what you really want.** Executives who wanted "their quality numbers to improve 30 percent by a certain date" found that what they really wanted was "a place filled with employees who care about quality." A client who says they want to lose a certain number of pounds really wants energy, flexibility and self-esteem, so focus on what they'll gain as well as what they'll lose.
- **Write a clear, compelling, time-bound goal statement** in value-based language. Contrast "We will reduce preventable harm in hospitals" with Don Berwick's "We will save 100,000 lives from medical mistakes by June 14, 2006. By 9 a.m.": "You're saving lives, not simply changing numbers on a chart. And you know exactly how many and by when." Fundación Paraguaya stopped counting loans and set out to "help 5,000 poor families to earn $5 per day per person [the national poverty line] or more before the end of the year." More than 6,000 did.
- **Operationalize soft results.** Morale, engagement, satisfaction and confidence have to be turned into something you can measure: "You have to pick something to represent the idea."
- **Measure frequently.** "A measure won't drive behavior if it doesn't maintain attention." Soft measures taken every two years lose to quality measured every 10 minutes and cash flow every two hours. Measurement is part of the influence effort itself, not separate from it.
- **Measure the measure by the behavior it drives.** "The measure of a measure is its actual influence." Tell Army officers to make the number of reported sexual assaults go down and the number will drop. Yet if most assaults go unreported, the first sign of progress may be that reports go *up*. So measure the real target, people's thoughts and actions: unit by unit, whether people feel safe *from* assault and safe *reporting* it. Wherever measuring results might drive the wrong behavior, also measure the actions that produce the results.

Done when the goal states exactly what, how much and by when, and each measure is refreshed often enough to hold attention. You should also be able to name the behavior each measure will drive.

## Step 2. Find vital behaviors

Influencers "don't create methods for changing behavior until they've carefully identified the exact behaviors they want to change." Typically one or two vital behaviors, well executed, yield a big difference. That's because every result has moments of *disproportionate influence*.

- A **crucial moment** is a point where someone's choice either leads toward great results or sets up a cascade of negative behaviors. Examples: a guest's problem at a restaurant, a physician entering a patient's room, a Guinea worm sufferer's urge to plunge the burning limb into the village pond.
- A **vital behavior** is the specific, high-leverage action that must follow the crucial moment. It is an action someone takes, not a value, an attitude or an outcome. Mimi Silbert of Delancey Street: focus on "behavior, not values, homilies, or emotional appeals," because "You can't succeed by trying to change 20 things at the same time!"

Apply the 80-20 rule hard. If 10 behaviors affect the result, focus on the top 2, "and only the top 2"; working on 4 or 5 spreads you too thin. Choosing wrong wastes everything: one researcher taught alcoholics to relax and ended up with "a bunch of very relaxed drunks."

Search with four strategies:

1. **Notice the obvious.** Look for behaviors that are obvious, at least to experts, but underused, usually because they are difficult or unpleasant. Apply what you know, then search for credible research or ask experts. If you find quick consensus, you have a vital behavior. Examples: for health, exercise, eat better, don't smoke. For first-year college students: attend class, complete assignments, make friends. For type 1 diabetes: test blood sugar four to seven times a day and adjust insulin. Much of this research has already been done for problems that affect many people.
2. **Look for crucial moments.** "Most of us are mostly successful most of the time." Noble Drilling: "98 percent of our people do 98 percent of the right stuff 98 percent of the time. And that's not good enough!" Study the 2 percent. Noble mapped its violations and found the crucial moments: a rig down, a storm approaching, a climb above deck. The vital behaviors became (1) when the rig is down, behind schedule or facing a storm, hold a safety meeting and take extra care with every precaution; (2) when climbing, tie off the ladder, wear the harness and never carry items in your hands. To find your own:
   - List the *failure modes*: what failure actually looks like.
   - Map when they happen by time, day, people, place and condition.
   - Examine everyone's behavior in those moments, including your own.

   A fast-food chain found that more than 80 percent of service failures happened when the restaurant was short by two or more people, an oven was down, or an unexpected rush hit. In those moments the owner had been stepping into an employee's job. The vital behaviors became "bring in your on-call team members" and "become the quarterback and run a quick practice with the new staff configuration."
3. **Learn from positive deviants.** A *positive deviant* is "a person who, by all rights, ought to have a problem but for some reason doesn't."
   - Find the people who succeed under the same conditions.
   - Have the group and the deviants observe each other, especially in crucial moments. Let the people themselves lead the observing, because "Evidence is more compelling when you discover it for yourself."
   - Spread the behaviors across the population.

   Medical transcriptionists whose productivity fell after voice recognition arrived found three colleagues whose productivity had instead improved tenfold. Each had independently created keyboard shortcuts. The three compared notes, picked the best shortcuts and taught the team a short lesson, and within the week everyone's productivity had improved by a factor of 10.
4. **Spot culture busters.** Look for crucial moments that call for behaviors that are currently taboo, punished or against the norm. Hospital hand hygiene needed a tandem: (1) anyone who sees a colleague skip washing reminds them, regardless of rank; (2) the reminded person says "Thank you." Such behaviors "take an unwritten rule, and ... turn it on its head with a written, trained, and practiced one."
   - One of the most consistent vital behaviors across hundreds of cases is stepping up to a *crucial conversation*: speaking up about a previously emotionally or politically risky issue.
   - Culture is invisible to those inside it ("fish discover water last"), so map it by **collecting stories, not opinions or suggestions**. Gold-mine truck drivers' accident stories surfaced the unwritten rules: "Obey speed limits—unless you're driving bosses who are in a hurry," "Pedestrians never have the right of way."

**Test your results.** All four strategies yield plausible behaviors, not proven ones. When stakes and resources allow, track the vital behaviors *and* the results, or compare against a control group, to confirm that more behavior brings more result. Ethna Reid found teachers' vital behaviors by watching positive deviants: praising far more often than punishing, and rapidly alternating teaching and testing with immediate correction. She then proved them in experimental schools.

Vital behaviors from the book, as models of the right grain:

- YMCA lifeguards: *10-10 scanning*. Scan your section every 10 seconds and reach anyone who *might* be in trouble within 10 seconds. YMCA drownings fell by two-thirds.
- Danny Meyer's restaurants: ABCD, "Always Be Collecting Dots." Dots are information about a guest's needs and desires, which staff connect into special experiences.
- Delancey Street, reversing the code of the street ("Care only about yourself, and don't rat on anyone"): (1) take responsibility for someone else's success; (2) confront everyone about every violation or concern.
- Thailand AIDS (Dr. Wiwat): two years of awareness posters and education sessions failed. The data showed 97 percent of new infections came from heterosexual contact with sex workers. That located the crucial moment, when a sex worker does or doesn't demand a condom, and the vital behavior: 100 percent condom use.
- A nurse manager who tripled patient experience scores: (1) nurses lead a meaningful discussion with each patient every morning and write the Goal for the Day on the whiteboard; (2) every caregiver, physicians included, reads and updates the whiteboard before leaving.
- Couples (Howard Markman): in the crucial moment of disagreement, avoid blaming, escalation, invalidation and withdrawal, and take time out to communicate respectfully.
- Budget padding and cutting cycle: "Whenever you think a budget is unrealistic, speak up with honesty and respect."
- Software teams: admit when you have problems, and immediately speak up when you won't meet a deadline.

Done when you have no more than two or three vital behaviors, each a concrete action tied to its crucial moment and found by at least one of the four strategies. You also need a way to track each one alongside the result.

## Step 3. Diagnose before you prescribe

"Diagnose before you prescribe. Anything else is malpractice." Before choosing any tactic, find out which sources of influence are behind the current behavior. Most leaders skip this and apply the latest technique they heard about.

Every force that shapes behavior works on two drivers. People ask themselves "Will it be worth it?" (*motivation*) and "Can I do what's required?" (*ability*). Each driver operates at three levels, drawn from psychology, social psychology and organization theory. That gives the six sources of influence, each with its label in the model and its diagnostic question:

| | Motivation | Ability |
|---|---|---|
| **Personal** | 1. **Help Them Love What They Hate.** *Do they enjoy it?* Is the vital behavior intrinsically pleasurable or painful? | 2. **Help Them Do What They Can't.** *Can they do it?* Do they have the skills, talent and understanding? |
| **Social** | 3. **Provide Encouragement.** *Do others encourage them to enact the wrong behavior?* | 4. **Provide Assistance.** *Do others enable them?* |
| **Structural** | 5. **Change Their Economy.** *Do rewards and sanctions encourage them?* | 6. **Change Their Space.** *Does their environment enable them?* Look at systems, processes, reporting structures, visual cues, layouts, tools, data. |

For each vital behavior, ask all six questions about the people who must enact it. For each source, name what is working *against* the behavior and what is already working *for* it. In the canonical example, Guinea worm eradication needed three vital behaviors: filter water; if infected, stay away from the water supply; confront neighbors who don't. Each source worked against them:

1. Soaking the emerging worm in water is the only relief from the agony.
2. "Filtering is a skill. There are more ways to get it wrong than get it right."
3. Outsiders aren't trusted, and two of a village's three tribes resist anything the chief backs.
4. The infected need neighbors to fetch their water, and households must pair up to have enough pots.
5. The infected live hand to mouth and must keep working at the water source.
6. Villagers lack filters and wound care, and the village layout makes the pond the easiest place to go.

Listen for the real causes rather than asking "What the heck is wrong with her?" Dr. Warren Warwick's teenage cystic fibrosis patient had stopped her treatments. The causes were many: a new boyfriend she stayed with half the time, a night job, a school rule that a nurse must give her medicine, and feeling fine despite losing 20 percent of her lung capacity. Knowing these let them build a tailored plan that saved her life.

Two diagnostic errors recur, and both send you to the wrong source:

- **The fundamental attribution error**: attributing others' bad behavior to a character flaw ("he's just lazy") and then reaching for coercion. Influencers instead assume *moral slumber*. People are capable of caring; they just aren't thinking about the consequences in that moment.
- **Treating an ability problem as a motivation problem.** "Much of will is skill." Self-discipline, once thought a trait, turns out to be learnable technique. Piling motivation on people who lack ability creates "resentment and depression."

Done when every vital behavior has all six cells filled in, with the forces working against it named specifically enough to target.

## Step 4. Engage all six sources

Single-source strategies fail: "just pass a law, just threaten a consequence, or just offer a training program." The authors liken this to "hiking the Himalayas with only a bag lunch." If six sources drive a bad habit and you address only one, nothing happens: "The problem is not a mystery. It's math. Five sources are usually stronger than one." Turn each source that is working against you, and add sources pulling for you. Think of six hulking behemoths on one end of the rope and a fourth-grader on the other: remove the forces pulling against you and add forces pulling for you, or better yet, do both.

Read the tactics file for each source the diagnosis flagged:

- **Sources 1 and 2, personal motivation and ability**: [personal.md](personal.md). Use it when people dislike the behavior, coercion or nagging has failed, people lack the skill or self-control, or emotions derail them in the moment. It covers allowing choice, direct and vicarious experience, stories, making it a game, deliberate practice and the go/know systems.
- **Sources 3 and 4, social motivation and ability**: [social.md](social.md). Use it when you or other leaders must model the change, opinion leaders, norms or a culture of silence sustain the problem, others enable the bad behavior, or no one can succeed alone. It covers the power of one, sacrifice, opinion leaders, making the undiscussable discussable, 200 percent accountability and social capital.
- **Sources 5 and 6, structural motivation and ability**: [structural.md](structural.md). Use it when rewards, punishments or perverse incentives are in play, or when the environment, data, layout or tools shape the behavior. It covers rewards used third, rewarding behaviors, the shot across the bow, making the invisible visible, the data stream, propinquity, making it easy and making it unavoidable.

The sources interlock; most strong tactics serve several. KIPP schools, with the vital behaviors "Work hard" and "Be nice," used all six:

1. Hard work tied to futures ("I'm Clifton, class of 2018"), and times tables in percussive rap.
2. Deliberate practice with clear feedback. Levin says results show in "One hour."
3. Pennants from colleges their own graduates attend.
4. Teacher home visits ending with the teacher's mobile number, and "Try three before me!"
5. The invitation-only monthly "Attend-Dance."
6. Home environments reorganized for study, down to removing a 36-inch TV: "TV or KIPP. Which is it?"

Done when every source working against a vital behavior has at least one tactic aimed at it, and the strategy as a whole overdetermines the change.

## Step 5. Trial and learn

Expect to find the right combination through personal research and adjustment. "Put a method into play, observe the impact, learn from the effort, make changes, and repeat until perfected." Watch the vital-behavior and result measures from Step 1.

- **Try adding a source.** Several sources may already work in your favor, and change may be "just one source away." Don't quit after "99 miles of a 100-mile journey." Pick the sources best suited to the circumstances, the most powerful and the easiest to implement.
- **Use a la carte for simple challenges.** Getting someone to attend a meeting doesn't need the full model, so a single tactic is fine. When the simple effort fails, use the full model to diagnose why and supplement it.
- **Make a genuine effort, not tinkering.** Most leaders of failed change efforts had used only one or two methods. Tinkering comes in three forms:
  - "Let's try a couple of things and see what happens," which protects the leader from losing face.
  - Covering yourself: "They know better than to curse. I recently wrote a memo!"
  - Change "on the cheap," adopting one element of a proven program, which then fails and gets blamed as unscalable.

  Each failure costs credibility, and the next idea is tagged "program-of-the-month" and left to fail.

## Lenses to hold throughout

- **Verbal persuasion has feet of clay.** Words fail when people distrust the speaker's motives or expertise, or lack any frame of reference for what is described. *Direct experience* is the gold standard for changing minds, and *vicarious experience* (watching a credible model, or a well-told story) is the tactic in between.
- **Nag, guilt and threaten** are the reflexive default. Each one signals that the diagnosis was skipped.
- **Bad behaviors are "real, fun, and now"**; their consequences are "fuzzy, maybe not so bad after all, and most certainly a long time off." Strategies must bring the long-term into the crucial moment.
- **Things are easier to change than people**, and once changed they stay put. Most people ignore structure, so check sources 5 and 6 before you settle on training or speeches.
