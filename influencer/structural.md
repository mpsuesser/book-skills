# Structural sources: motivation (5) and ability (6)

Tactics for when *things* shape the behavior: rewards, perks, punishments and incentive systems (source 5), and buildings, layout, tools, data, processes and routines (source 6). Most people overlook these sources. "Things are far easier to change than people," and once changed, "they stay put."

## Source 5. Structural motivation: change their economy

The goal "should not be to overwhelm people to change. Rather, it should be primarily to remove disincentives," which the authors call changing the economy. When bad behavior is entrenched, "odds are that the current economic system people live in is positively encouraging what you don't want." "Most leaders run the risk of relying too much on incentives rather than too little." The heavy lifting belongs to sources 1 and 3. Danny Meyer simply pools tips: "people don't serve customers just to get tips. They do it because they enjoy it and because they're part of a great culture that promotes it."

### Use extrinsic rewards third

Backfires come from using rewards first. The order is: (1) connect vital behaviors to intrinsic satisfaction; (2) line up social support; double-check both; (3) only then add extrinsic rewards. "Don't use incentives to compensate for your failure to engage personal and social motivation."

- **The overjustification hypothesis** (Mark Lepper). Nursery-school children rewarded for playing with a toy they already loved played with it *less* once the reward stopped. People infer they did it for the bonus. A daughter paid $5 per book reads less once the pay ends. People are often happy to be paid for what they love, like a harpist hired for a wedding; the risk comes when the reward redefines why they act.
- **Rewards that punish.** Employee of the month can make someone "a winner and a loser at the same time," with "four weeks of unrelenting ridicule." Typically half of awards-ceremony attendees think they were better qualified and were passed over for politics.
- **Rewards that redefine the job.** A cash-for-ideas program ended with Charlie in the emergency room. He had submitted his team's idea under his own name and kept the $5,000. Once the program was dropped, suggestions stopped entirely, because paying for ideas had signaled they were outside the normal job.
- **Rewards that break teamwork.** Anesthesiologists paid on personal production became less willing to help with a colleague's patient in trouble.

### Use incentives wisely

Make rewards **come soon, be gratifying, and be clearly tied to vital behaviors**. Then even small ones move hard problems:

- Johns Hopkins alcoholics learning moderation had food, phone and visiting privileges set by each day's drinking, and were 60 percent more likely to hit their target.
- Stephen Higgins gave cocaine outpatients a voucher whenever all three weekly urine samples were clean: 90 percent finished the 12-week program against 65 percent without vouchers. This worked with patients "already morally and socially invested" and combined with traditional treatment.
- A group home for teenage girls tried everything against suicide attempts, then denied a week of TV to any resident who attempted. Attempts dropped to zero: "small incentives that are immediately linked to vital behaviors can yield amazing results."
- Influencers who lead with sources 1 and 3 still use rewards and sanctions. A Grameen borrower group pays if you don't, and Guinea worm programs give a villager who reports a neighbor's hidden infection a T-shirt with the eradication logo.
- Cedars-Sinai caught doctors using hand sanitizer and gave them $10 Starbucks cards. Compliance rose from 65 to 80 percent. (Doctors had believed the problem was someone else's: 73 percent said they washed effectively, and 9 percent met the standard.)

**If you're doing it right, less is more.** "It's often the thought, not the gift, that counts," because the thought carries symbolic and social weight. Yunus's bank officers, already devoted to their mission, raced for colored stars awarded for mission results like loans made and borrowers' children enrolled in school; large cash might have undercut their motivation. Consulting-firm executives competed for an inexpensive brass goose statuette. Delancey stacks small privileges: better jobs, smaller rooms, then an apartment, then "walk-around money" (WAM). Symbolic awards carry enormous value once personal and social motives are in place. Without them, "extrinsic rewards can become a source of ridicule and cynicism." An open-pit mine scored each driver daily with GPS. Each five-person team's score was its *worst* driver's score, and teams that scored well won "Master Driver" caps and mugs. That combines pride, peer pressure and moderate rewards.

### Reward vital behaviors, not just results

- **Reward small improvements in behavior along the way.** Don't wait for phenomenal results. Employees' top complaint is lack of recognition, and praise is handed out "as if it were being rationed," though we cheer every approximation of a baby's "mama." This isn't rewarding mediocrity. If performance is unacceptable and you can't wait, terminate the person or move them. If overall performance meets expectations, set goals in the lagging areas and reward small improvements there.
- **Reward right results and right behaviors.** A plant's celebrated production record turned out to come from lowered quality standards, stock left unreplaced for the next shift, and a deliberately poor day before the record. "They had rewarded results without giving any thought to the behaviors that drove them." Outcomes can hide inappropriate behaviors.
- **Reward vital behaviors alone** when results depend on outside variables: "behavior is the one thing people have under their control." At a Matsushita plant, waitresses who calculated how much tea each table needed halved tea-leaf use. They won the presidential gold medal over far bigger savings because they best exemplified the Kaizen process. "If you reward the actual steps people follow, eventually results take care of themselves."

### Watch for perverse incentives

"When behaviors are out of whack, look closely at your rewards." This is Steve Kerr's "On the Folly of Rewarding A, While Hoping for B." Some examples:

- Coaches preach teamwork and then celebrate individual scoring.
- A family tells its addicted son to stop while giving him free rent, cars and bail.
- U.S. tax rules taxed savings interest and deducted consumer-debt interest; Japan for years did neither.
- World War II soldiers went home when the war was won; Vietnam soldiers went home when their tour ended, and some learned to "search and escape."
- Soviet oil crews paid by feet drilled dug many shallow holes.
- A firm's $50,000 "Road Warrior of the Year" prize, for the most days on the road, went to winners who quit the same year, four years running.

### Punishment sends a message, and so does its absence

Punishment suppresses behavior "but only temporarily" and unpredictably. It may win compliance, provoke pushback or rebellion, or cost the relationship. Random punishment produces *learned helplessness* (Seligman's dogs cowering on a randomly shocked grid).

- **Before punishing, place a shot across the bow.** "Provide a clear warning to let them know exactly what negative things *will* happen ... but don't actually administer discipline yet." In North Carolina's offender notification forums, dealers about to be arrested are promised safety for a 90-minute meeting. Family, friends and community opinion leaders ask them to change. Officials explain the laws and penalties, ex-offenders describe staying straight, and agencies offer job programs. Then dealers are told, "Find your poster": a photo of themselves dealing, with the case binder beside it. They watch a video and are asked to "Raise your hand when you see yourself committing a felony." Unlike *Scared Straight*, which lets people conclude only saps get caught, this makes it certain they *will* be caught. "The authorities never bluff": no-shows and relapsers are arrested immediately. Small crimes fell 35 percent in some neighborhoods, and 24 of 40 alleged dealers stayed clear of the law.
- **When all else fails, punish.** When warnings, incentives, social pressure and appeals to values have all failed, use discipline judiciously. Russian oil fields announced random drug and alcohol testing, fired anyone found impaired, and added safety training; accidents dropped. In Ethiopia, bride abduction had been rewarded with marriage. After the radio drama *Yeken Kignit* opened public discussion, communities began imprisoning the abductors.
- **The absence of punishment is a message.** Ask: "What does it take to get fired around here?" Answers like "Embarrass the boss" reveal that "if you aren't willing to go to the mat when people violate a core value ... that value loses its moral force." A Georgia company attacking centuries-old racism picked one behavior, racist jokes. It announced that anyone telling one would be fired on the spot, without warning or grace period. Then it fired the first violator, and racist jokes ended.

## Source 6. Structural ability: change their space

"Rarely does the average person conceive of changing the physical world as a way of changing human behavior." Fred Steele calls most of us "environmentally incompetent." To become environmentally competent: (1) remember to think about things, and (2) form theories of how changing things will change behavior.

The classic case: William Foote Whyte stopped postwar shouting matches between cooks and waitresses with no team-building or pay changes. He brought in a 50-cent spindle for written orders, filled roughly first-in, first-out, after a 10-minute training. Conflict fell at once, and the spindle became the order wheel. He "chose not to confront norms, history, or habit"; he changed *things*.

### Learn to notice

"Fish discover water last." Take "your laserlike attention off people," and look at their physical world for the subtle features silently driving misbehavior.

- An insurance president who wanted candor sat at the end of six hallways, past four secretary stations, behind a desk "the size of a 1964 Caddy," with low chairs for visitors. He said, "I get the feeling that people around here are scared to talk to me." Move decision-making into spaces that don't shout "Behold, the great and mighty Oz!"
- George Kelling's "broken windows theory": "A broken window left in disrepair ... suggests that no one is in charge and no one cares." New York's subway crews rolled paint over graffiti the moment a car came in for service. Along with prosecution of minor offenses, petty and violent crime fell.
- Brian Wansink's moviegoers ate 53 percent more stale popcorn from big buckets. Diners with secretly refilling soup bowls ate 73 percent more and felt no fuller: "We eat until small things from our environment make us *think* we're full."

### Make the invisible visible

"Provide actual cues in the space around you to remind people of the behaviors." Most people aren't resisting the behavior; they "were not thinking of the behaviors in the moment."

- Wansink colored every tenth chip in a stack, and people ate 37 percent fewer.
- Emery Air Freight's shipping containers were filled properly 45 percent of the time despite training. A "fill to here" line inside each one raised that to 95 percent.
- Powderless latex gloves cost over 10 times more than regular disposable ones, yet clinicians used them even for short tasks despite pleas to cut costs. A price sign on each box made the gap obvious at the moment of choice, and use of the expensive gloves dropped dramatically.
- Cedars-Sinai's epidemiologist cultured physicians' palms on agar plates ("I would love to culture your hand"). The photos of bacterial colonies became screen savers. Once opinion leaders came "face to colony," hand hygiene reached nearly 100 percent and stuck.
- Bogotá's mayor replaced the busy signal with "Sorry. The line is busy at the moment. But remember to conserve water!" Water use fell 40 percent.

### Mind the data stream

"Information affects behavior. People make choices based on cognitive maps." Frequent data feels representative even when it isn't (the *representative heuristic*: people judge homicide and fire deadlier than suicide and drowning because the news shows them more). Influencers serve up "visible, timely, and accurate information that supports their goals" and "manage data religiously."

- **Gather a baseline with eye-popping numbers.** Nigeria's leaders assumed a few thousand Guinea worm cases. Village coordinators reported over 650,000, and support skyrocketed. Data must be "fresh, consistent, and relevant."
- **Harness comparison.** The monthly *Guinea Worm Wrap Up* includes a "Guinea worm race" with each country as a runner. Burkina Faso's president: "They can't stand to be at the bottom."
- **Balance the streams.** People advocate for whatever their data shows: frontline staff for customers, executives for shareholders, quality inspectors for quality. Executives who saw only weekly production numbers noticed morale and customers only after a crisis. Add customer and employee data to the executives' weekly review, and cost and profit data to the customer-passionate staff, and both groups broaden their attention.
- **Curate.** Too many reports become "numbing and incoherent background noise." "The only reason for gathering or publishing any data is to reinforce vital behaviors." One condo owner tagged 400 trees in a database and found a swampy area where every replacement tree had died, year after year.

### Use space: propinquity

*Propinquity* is physical proximity, and Festinger found the frequency and quality of interaction is largely a function of distance. People living by stairwells and mailboxes know the most neighbors, and bosses nearest their reports have the best relationships. Distance breeds silos and "them" and "they." "If you want to predict who doesn't trust or get along with whom in a company, take out a tape measure."

- At Bell Labs, scientists in adjacent offices were three times as likely to collaborate as those 30 feet apart; at 90 feet, they were no more likely than colleagues miles away.
- HP fostered informal contact with messy desks (visible work invites help) and a mandated daily break in a common area.
- Delancey puts former enemies (Mexican Mafia, Crips, Aryan Brotherhood) in the same room with interdependent roles. When the tattooed newcomer Kurt shattered a plate, his black roommate and former gang rival knelt beside him to help.
- The dining table vanishing from homes, with microwaved single portions and separate TV rooms, has cost parents influence.
- Grameen's 30-member units meet weekly in a simple, award-winning building, reciting the "16 Decisions," including "I will neither give nor receive dowry."
- A hospital system overrode its architect's "visual flow" objection to sanitizers at every door: "bacteria flow is more important than visual flow." Hand hygiene rose above 90 percent.

### Make it easy

Rather than "constantly finding ways to motivate people to continue with their boring, painful, dangerous, or otherwise loathsome activities," change *things* so the right behavior is easier and the wrong one harder. "When it comes to enabling others, we often turn to training before we look for ways to make the task easier to perform."

- The Carter Center got DuPont to donate 2 million square yards of nylon filter cloth that wouldn't rot in the tropics. In India, where clean water lies near the surface, engineers drilled capped bore-hole wells, so the Guinea worm lost its hosts.
- Frederick Taylor sized shovels so every load weighed 21.5 pounds.
- Delancey residents "changed their zip code": eight dorm-mates, 50 floor-mates and 200 building-mates who don't use drugs make the drug much harder to reach.
- A graffiti campaign's vital behavior was painting over new tags within 24 hours. Free paint matched to each storefront made it easy.
- Wansink again: smaller plates, opaque packaging, candy moved from the desk to a shelf.
- Keep the exercise bike in the TV room, not the basement. Inventory the bad and good choices within reach at each hour, and "discover how many items in your home you can simply *move*."
- Colored pill bottles and better labels cut medication errors. Moving pet treats down one shelf raised sales to children and the elderly. Cozy casinos make gambling more fun, "a surrogate for easy."

### Make it unavoidable

The strongest structural move "eliminates human choice entirely": machine guards, pilot checklists, fast-food picture buttons and registers that make change.

- For social behaviors, "structure drives out lack of structure. Meetings happen." Build the vital behavior into a special meeting or hardwire it into an existing agenda. A defense CEO who calendared regular idea sessions with employee groups saw a surge in innovation.
- Turn the calendared event into a *ritual*. Delancey's "Games" meet three times a week with each minyan; anyone can summon anyone, Silbert included, by handing over a slip of paper, and the invitee must show up. A disinterested person keeps things from getting physical. Left alone, people "toggle from silence ... to violence"; the ritual makes candid feedback inevitable, and its quality rises as the volume falls. "Not always fun, but it's always done."
