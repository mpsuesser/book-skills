# Act 3: Refine and Rehearse

Scenes 14 to 18 and the Encore. How you say something is as important as what you say, if not more so: body language and verbal delivery account for 63 to 90 percent of the impression you make. Great slides mean little without great delivery; it is a package deal.

## Scene 14: Master Stage Presence

Jobs has a commanding presence: his voice, gestures and body language communicate authority, confidence and energy. The cautionary contrast is **"Who's Mr. Note Card?"** At Macworld 2007 the Cingular/AT&T CEO followed Jobs with his hands in his pockets, read note cards word for word in a low-key monotone, stumbled and lost eye contact. Six minutes seemed like thirty, bloggers asked "Who's Mr. Note Card?", and the appearance was described as completely negating Jobs's reality distortion field. Forty-two years at the company, and that was the legacy. Gil Amelio, by contrast, was "hooked by Steve's energy and enthusiasm."

Three body-language techniques carry the presence:

- **Eye contact.** Great communicators make appreciably more eye contact than average presenters. Jobs rarely reads from slides or notes. He glances at a slide and turns back to the audience, and during demos his notes stay tucked out of view. Mediocre presenters break eye contact during demos. Eye contact signals honesty, trustworthiness, sincerity and confidence; avoiding it signals a lack of confidence and leadership. Jobs can hold it for two reasons: he practices for weeks, so the content is internalized, and his slides are highly visual, often wordless, which forces him to deliver to the audience.
- **Open posture.** Jobs rarely crosses his arms or stands behind a lectern; nothing stands between him and the audience. He used a lectern in 1984 and has abandoned it since (the 2005 Stanford address is the exception). During demos he sits parallel to the computer, performs the function, and turns to the audience to explain it.
- **Hand gestures.** Jobs emphasizes nearly every sentence with a complementary gesture. Hands at your sides are "the kiss of death": stiff, formal, weird. Extraordinary communicators use more gestures, not fewer. David McNeill (University of Chicago) found gestures and language intimately connected: gesture helps clear the thought process, it takes effort *not* to gesture, and the gestures of disciplined, confident thinkers act as a window onto clear thought. Keep gestures authentic rather than robotic or overrehearsed, and don't copy Jobs's.

Cisco's John Chambers shows the range: he roams the audience like a preacher, calls people by name and puts a hand on a shoulder, a confidence that comes from relentless practice.

**Say it with style.** A monotone destroys all the work of the script. Vary four things:

- **Inflection:** raise and lower pitch. Jobs's pitch rises on "Are you getting it?" and "This is one device." His favorite descriptors (*unbelievable*, *awesome*, *cool*, *huge*) each need a distinct tone.
- **Pauses:** "Nothing is more dramatic than a well-placed pause." A third kind of notebook [pause], it's called the MacBook Air [pause], it's the world's thinnest notebook. Let the message breathe; stay quiet for several seconds. Most presenters rush because they scripted more than their time allows.
- **Volume:** lower it while building up, then raise it to hit the big note, or do the opposite. For the iPod, Jobs raised his voice on "quantum leap," then lowered it for the knockout: your whole music library fits in your pocket.
- **Rate:** speak at a normal rate through demos and slow considerably for the headline or key message.

The word-and-gesture, pause-and-volume and objection-then-whisper patterns from three keynotes are in [script-and-slide-patterns.md](script-and-slide-patterns.md).

Albert Mehrabian found that nonverbal cues have the most impact, tone of voice is second, and the words themselves come third. These are not "soft skills." Audiences form their judgments in the first ninety seconds. Read Jobs's own transcript in Ben Stein's "Bueller . . . ?" monotone and it becomes the dullest presentation ever: the words matter, but delivery makes the difference.

**Director's Notes.** Check your body language: eye contact, open posture and hand gestures. Gestures reflect complex thinking and give the listener confidence in you. Vary your vocal delivery by adding inflection and changing volume and speed. Let the content breathe; pause. Record yourself; video is the best way to improve.

## Scene 15: Make It Look Effortless

Jobs rehearses "many, many hours over many, many days." His informality comes only after grueling hours of practice. He treats keynotes as a competitive weapon. A former Apple product manager described the keynotes as an "incredibly complex and sophisticated blend of sales pitch, product demonstration, and corporate cheerleading, with a dash of religious revival," and saw his team spend hundreds of hours preparing a five-minute demo. Jobs rehearsed the whole presentation for two full days, asked the product managers for feedback, and wrote and designed his own slides. The day before, he ran one or two complete dress rehearsals. For the 1999 iMac reveal he had the lighting rerun until it came up brighter and sooner: "Let's keep doing it till we get it right, OK?"

**Deliberative practice.** K. Anders Ericsson found that star performers set specific goals, ask for feedback and continually strive to improve over the long run, which is more than repetition. Churchill practiced quips and parries against possible interjections, so they seemed extemporaneous. "Practice is essential, particularly if you want to sound spontaneous": spontaneity is the result of planned practice. A NeXT executive: "Every slide was written like a piece of poetry."

**Ten thousand hours to mastery.** There are no "naturals." Daniel Levitin puts world-class expertise in anything at ten thousand hours, roughly three hours a day, twenty hours a week, for ten years. Before their first success in 1964, the Beatles had performed live together some twelve hundred times. Jobs went from the Homebrew Computer Club in 1974 to the Macintosh launch in 1984, to Macworld Boston 1997 (the lectern gone, the slides more visual), to Macworld 2007, his greatest. "The chief misconception about Jobs is that he is a natural presenter."

**Practice the coordination, not just the words.** One executive spent $25,000 on animated slides, never rehearsed timing his words to the animation, and lost his place. Practice, practice, and practice some more. Rehearse **contingency plans** too: when a CEO's clicker failed at CES, he motioned to an assistant as rehearsed, and when the computer then locked up he delivered the rest without slides. Employees in the audience didn't notice.

**Make video your best friend.** A camcorder (under $300), objective friends and an external clip-on microphone. The first five minutes of footage is enough. Watch for five areas:

1. **Eye contact.** Commit most of the content to memory and let the slides cue you. Notes destroy the intimacy between speaker and audience. Strive to need no notes at all.
2. **Body language.** Strong and confident? Arms crossed, hands in pockets? Fidgeting or rocking? Gestures natural and purposeful, or stiff?
3. **Filler words.** "Um," "ah," "you know." Words should not fill every pause. "Awareness is more than 90 percent of the solution!"
4. **Vocal delivery.** Vary volume, inflection and cadence. Pause for impact. Let it breathe.
5. **Energy.** Vibrant and enthusiastic? Energy makes a person likable, and likability is key to persuasion. Professionals underestimate the energy a presentation requires.

**The energy scale.** "On a scale of one to ten—one being fast asleep and ten being wildly pumped up like motivational speaker Tony Robbins—tell me where you are right now." Most people say three; honest presenters land between three and six. The exercise: record a few minutes normally and play it back with an observer, asking "Where am I on the energy scale?" Redo it and ham it up (raise your voice, use broad gestures, a big smile) to the point of slight awkwardness. Watch it again. The energy is usually just right. People underestimate how little energy they have.

**Eliminate filler words.** A steady stream of fillers damages credibility, though a few ums are fine. Caroline Kennedy said "you know" more than thirty times in a two-minute interview and soon withdrew her name from consideration for a Senate seat. Three fixes:

- **Ask for feedback.** Colleagues won't volunteer it because they are afraid to offend you.
- **Tap the glass.** A second person taps a water glass with a spoon each time you use a filler.
- **Record yourself and play it back in the presence of others.**

**The bucket method** prepares "off-the-cuff" answers to tough questions. CEOs and politicians use it, and so does Jobs:

1. **Identify the most common questions** you are likely to be asked.
2. **Place them into "buckets,"** or categories. The majority fall into about seven categories, which reduces preparation to a manageable load.
3. **Create the best answer for each category,** one that makes sense regardless of *how* the question is phrased. Don't get pulled into the details by the questioner's wording. Asked about her husband's foundation in her confirmation hearing, Hillary Clinton gave her prepared bucket answer, that she was very proud to be the nominee and very proud of what the foundation had accomplished, whatever the specific question.
4. **Listen carefully for a key word, a trigger,** that tells you which bucket to draw from.
5. **Look the person in the eye and respond with confidence.**

You prepare categories, not hundreds of answers, and each answer becomes a minipresentation within the presentation. Use it to reframe the question in your favor. A customer asks why you charge 10 percent more for the same product they can get elsewhere. "Charging more" is the trigger for the price bucket, so you answer "You're asking about price," then say the product is competitively priced, improves clients' bottom line by 30 percent on average, and comes with the best service team, available 24-7, which no competitor can claim. For the trigger "competition," a CEO answered that the company plays with class, offers superior service, and sees more competitors as it gets more successful, which is part of being a leader. Henry Kissinger opened a press conference with "What questions do you have for my answers?"

**The best antidote to nerves** is relentless preparation: know what you will say, when you will say it and how. Inward focus ("Is my shirt wrinkled?") creates anxiety, so go from "me" to "we" and shift your focus to what the product means to the listeners. The more you practice, the less nervous you will be. One famous leader knows every slide, arrives early to test the audio and projector and advance the slides, and knows where the lights are so he is never in shadow.

**Scale of practice.** For the iPad launch's roughly ninety-minute presentation, seven speakers each had a set time and practiced onstage in front of Apple staff, taking "not a second more." A hundred hours of rehearsal for a five-minute demo is not uncommon.

**Director's Notes.** Practice, practice, and practice some more. Review every slide, every demo and every key message; know exactly what you will say, when you will say it and how. Record your presentation (a camera costs a couple of hundred dollars) and review it with someone, watching the first five minutes for distracting body language, verbal tics and fillers. Use the bucket method for tough questions: most will fall into one of seven categories.

## Scene 16: Wear the Appropriate Costume

Jobs is the "anti-Cher": where Cher and her dancers had 140 costume changes in her Vegas concert, he has one costume for every performance: a black mock turtleneck (a St. Croix sweater), Levi's 501 jeans and New Balance running shoes. "He can get away with it because he's Steve Jobs and you're not." He hasn't always dressed that way. He wore a double-breasted blazer, white shirt and green bow tie for the 1984 Macintosh launch, and put on a Brioni suit to pitch NeXT to Bank of America ("Hey, ... we're going to the bank today"). His wardrobe always reflected the leader he wanted to become.

A U.S. Army Ranger leader, Matt Eversmann, says great leaders dress a little better than everyone else: shinier shoes, whiter whites, better pressed. Men's Wearhouse founder George Zimmer adds that it should be "appropriate for the culture." Apple's culture is rebellious, so informal works there. The rule: **always dress a little better than everyone else, but appropriate for the culture.**

**Director's Notes.** Dress like the leader you want to become, not for the position you have, and a little better than everyone else in the room (Jobs wore a suit to the bank). Dress appropriately for the culture: Jobs's brand is built on disrupting the status quo. "If you're going to dress like a rebel, dress like a well-off rebel."

## Scene 17: Toss the Script

Audiences want conversations, not lectures. Jobs's casual style comes from practice. He uses notes only as cue cards during demos, concealed and never read word for word, and he performs largely without them. Slideuments are a crutch, and readers turn their backs on the audience. Jobs's script lives largely in his head, and his visual slides act as the prompter: "Each slide has one key idea and one idea only." Table 17.1's MacBook Air run shows how, in [script-and-slide-patterns.md](script-and-slide-patterns.md).

**Five steps to tossing the script.** Actors memorize their lines yet sound natural; this gets a presenter there:

1. **Write your script in full sentences in the "notes" section** of the presentation software. No extensive editing, and no more than four or five sentences per slide.
2. **Highlight or underline the key word from each sentence, and practice,** glancing only at the key words.
3. **Delete extraneous words, leaving only key words,** and practice again.
4. **Memorize the *one* key idea per slide.** Ask "What is the one thing I want my audience to take away from the slide?" The visual complements that idea and becomes your prompter. Behind the MacBook Air's processor photo, the one thing was ultrathin with no compromise in performance.
5. **Practice the entire presentation without notes, using the slides as your prompter.**

Rehearse each slide four times this way.

**Worked example.** A financial firm's slide shows two glasses of water, one nearly empty and one full, under the headline "The lower the cost, the more you keep."

1. Full sentences: investment costs are important; the lower the cost, the more you keep; other funds charge six times more; invest $10,000 for twenty years at 8 percent and you keep $58,000 more than at the industry average.
2. Key phrases marked: *investment costs*, *important*, *lower the cost, the more you keep*, *six times more*, *keep $58,000 more*.
3. Reduced to four lines: "Investment costs important / Lower the cost, the more you keep / Six times more / Keep $58,000 more."
4. One idea: "The lower the cost, the more you keep."
5. Rehearse with no notes. The water-glass slide prompts all four lines.

If notes give you peace of mind, keep them available (presenter view shows them to you alone). Practice enough and you won't need them. If you forget a supporting point but hit the main theme, the slide has done its job. Notes interfere with the emotional connection.

**When notes are a must.** Notes are not inherently bad. For step-by-step passages such as a demo, Jobs used bound notes with color-coded tabs: four categories in bold, large font (Mail, Safari, Widgets, Maps), each with two to five supporting points. The MAPS card read: Moscone West / Starbucks, order 4,000 lattes to go / Washington Monument / Show satellite / Eiffel Tower, Colosseum. The latte "gag" that played as spontaneous was on the card. Pastor Joel Osteen, who preaches to some forty-seven thousand people a week, starts on Wednesday and practices for four days. He keeps notes on a lectern he never stands behind, glances at them as he walks, and never reads a full sentence.

**Director's Notes.** Don't read from notes except for a step-by-step process such as a demo. When you must use notes, put no more than three or four large-font points on a card or sheet, one card per slide; in speaker's notes, three or four bullet points, and one is even better. Use the visuals to prompt the one key theme of each slide: one theme per slide.

## Scene 18: Have Fun

At WWDC 2002 Jobs held a funeral for Mac OS 9: smoke, a casket, a pipe organ, and a eulogy for a friend who "occasionally... forgot who he was and needed to be restarted," survived by Mac OS X. He committed to the joke, with a moment of silence and a rose.

**Glitches.** Things go wrong despite all the preparation, and Jobs doesn't let the small stuff get to him. When the clicker failed at Macworld 2007, he said "The clicker is not working. They're scrambling backstage right now," smiled, and told a one-minute story about the TV jammer he and Wozniak built in high school. It made him human, and he was never flustered. The difference between a master and a mediocre presenter: when the demo fails, the master shows cool confidence, doesn't dwell on it or call undue attention to it, "smiles, has fun, explains to the audience what they should have seen, and moves on." When Flickr showed a black screen for about twenty seconds in 2008, Jobs said "Well, I'm afraid Flickr isn't serving up photos on that one," continued, summarized what the feature does when it works, and never lost his enthusiasm. Handing a balky camera to an employee: "I need an expert to fix it. It's too technical for me. It's pretty awesome when it works."

Something goes "wrong" only when you call attention to it or let it ruin the rest of the presentation. Useful lines: "Oops, that's not what I wanted," or "I need some help here, guys; this thing isn't working." Like an ice skater who falls, nobody expects perfection except you, and the audience forgives a blooper if you get back up.

**Take control when the room is the problem.** At the iPhone 4 launch the hall's WiFi was jammed. Jobs first explained, "Our networks in here are always unpredictable," and asked people to get off. He moved on to the photography segment he had planned to show later, then came back and took charge. With 570 WiFi base stations operating in the room, he asked "Would you like to see the demos?" and told the audience to turn off their devices and police each other: "Go ahead, I've got time." Internalized content is what lets you handle a curve ball and rearrange the order on the fly.

**Infotainment.** Audiences want to be informed *and* entertained: a presentation that teaches something new while having fun. Most professionals don't smile; they get caught in "presentation mode" and lose their enthusiasm. Jobs has a broad smile, an easy laugh and a joke or two, often at Microsoft's expense. When he announced iTunes for Windows, his slide read "Hell froze over," and he called it "probably the best Windows app ever written!" Answering rumors about his health, his slide read "The reports of my death are greatly exaggerated." Richard Branson: "Most of all, though, I try to have fun."

**Director's Notes.**
- **Treat presentations as "infotainment."** Have fun.
- **Never apologize.** There is little to gain by calling attention to a problem. Acknowledge it, smile, and move on. If it isn't obvious to anyone but you, don't call attention to it.
- **Change your frame of reference.** It didn't "go wrong" unless you allow it to derail the rest of the presentation. Keep the big picture in mind and let the small stuff roll off your back.

## Encore: One More Thing

Jobs frequently, but not always, ends a presentation with "just one more thing." "A surprise would fail to surprise if everyone knows it's coming!" Use it sometimes, for news big enough to carry the close.

**The 2005 Stanford commencement** shows the same methods at work in a commencement address:

- **A road map of three.** "Today I want to tell you three stories from my life. That's it. No big deal. Just three stories." Then an opening, three stories and a conclusion: connecting the dots (dropping out of Reed, a calligraphy class that shaped the Mac's fonts ten years later), love and loss (fired at thirty from the company he founded: "You've got to find what you love"), and death (his pancreatic cancer diagnosis, with humor even there: "Even people who want to go to heaven don't want to die to get there").
- **Anaphora,** the repetition of the same word or words in consecutive sentences ("Your time is limited . . . Don't be trapped by dogma . . . Don't let the noise of others' opinions drown out your own inner voice"). Churchill, King, Reagan and Obama used it, and it is available to anyone.
- **The headline repeated three times** in the concluding paragraph: "Stay hungry, stay foolish."

The secret beneath every scene: do what you love, view setbacks as opportunities, and dedicate yourself to the passionate pursuit of excellence. It is "the power of believing in yourself and your story." The products change from launch to launch, but the storytelling techniques remain the same.
