# Act 1: Create the Story

Scenes 1 to 7 and Intermission 1. Creating the plot comes first. Effective communicators plan, develop compelling messages and headlines, make the narrative easy to follow, and introduce a common enemy to build the drama.

## Scene 1: Plan in Analog

John Sculley: "Marketing is really theater." Jobs's presentations have the elements of a great play or movie (conflict, resolution, villains and heroes), and like a film director he storyboards the plot before picking up the "camera," that is, before opening the presentation software. He is involved in every detail: taglines, slides, demos, even the lighting.

**Time budget.** Nancy Duarte: up to ninety hours to build an hour-long, thirty-slide presentation, with only a third of it spent building slides. The first twenty-seven hours go to researching, collecting expert input, organizing ideas, collaborating, and sketching the structure of the story. Gallo's Wal-Mart client spent the whole first day sketching the story on a whiteboard and ended up needing five slides for fifteen minutes. Once the narrative was written, designing the slides was easy.

**Bullets Kill.** The default slide template offers a title, a subtitle and Bullets & Numbering, the opposite of what a Jobs presentation needs: very few words and no bullet points. Text and bullets are the least effective way to deliver information meant to be recalled and acted on. "Save your bullet points for grocery lists."

**Writing → Sketching → Producing** (Cliff Atkinson, *Beyond Bullet Points*). Write the script first and set fonts, colors, backgrounds and transitions aside. Writing defines the purpose before the design starts. The script must come first.

**The Napkin Test.** A picture is the most powerful way to convey an idea. Sketch it on a napkin. Rollin King drew three circles (Dallas, Houston, San Antonio) for Herb Kelleher, and they founded Southwest Airlines. Cranium's founder sketched a board game where everyone gets a chance to shine. Jobs, back at Apple and facing more than forty confusing products, drew a two-by-two grid on a whiteboard (Consumer / Professional across the top, Portable / Desktop down the side) and cut the line to four computers. "Never underestimate the power of a vision so simple that it can fit on a napkin!"

**The wrong planning questions** are about the slides: which font, bullets or dashes, a graph here or a picture there.

### Nine elements of great presentations

Consider each before opening the software. Incorporate some, if not all.

1. **Headline.** The one big idea: short (140 characters or less), memorable, in subject-verb-object order. "Today Apple reinvents the phone!" Read *USA Today* for models ("Apple Shrinks iPod"). See Scene 4.
2. **Passion statement.** Aristotle's pathos. Fill in: "I'm excited about this product [company, initiative, feature, etc.] because it ______." Then share it.
3. **Three key messages.** Recallable without notes, because listeners hold only three or four points in short-term memory. Each one carries supporting points. See Scene 5.
4. **Metaphors and analogies.** For Aristotle, metaphor is "the most important thing by far." A *metaphor* uses a word or phrase that denotes one thing to designate another, for comparison: a computer is "the equivalent of a bicycle for our minds." Sports metaphors work, but break away from what the audience expects. Kaspersky's ads showed a dejected medieval knight, casting rival security software as obsolete armor, and carried the image to its website. An *analogy* compares two different things to highlight a similarity: "the microprocessor is the brain of your computer." When an analogy works, stick with it across presentations, website and marketing.
5. **Demonstrations.** Script one in if the product allows. Leopard had three hundred new features. Jobs demonstrated ten, the ones *he* wanted the press to highlight. A chip start-up's CEO played music through a fingernail-sized chip in an investor pitch, given a more dramatic buildup. See Scene 12.
6. **Partners.** Share the stage with people who contribute to your success, as Madonna did by webcam and the CEOs of Intel, Fox and Sony did in person. See Scene 11.
7. **Customer evidence and third-party endorsements.** Few customers want to be pioneers, and word of mouth is the number one influencer. Escalate from a quote, to a recorded video testimonial, to the customer present in person or by webcam. Always use third-party reviews when you have them.
8. **Video clips.** Ads, employee testimonials, the product in use, customer endorsements. Keep clips to two or three minutes at most; the average YouTube clip viewed is 2.5 minutes. The California Strawberry Commission introduced its clip with "we decided to bring the farmers to you," and it was the most memorable part of the presentation.
9. **Flip charts, props, and show-and-tell.** Reach visual, auditory and kinesthetic learners with whiteboards, flip charts and physical products people can see and touch. Apple handed out unibody frames to the audience.

### Aristotle's outline for persuasive arguments

1. Deliver a story or statement that arouses the audience's interest.
2. Pose a problem or question that has to be solved or answered.
3. Offer a solution to the problem you raised.
4. Describe specific benefits of adopting the course of action in your solution.
5. State a call to action ("Now go out and buy one!").

**Director's Notes.** Plan before opening the software, sketching on paper or whiteboards. Incorporate some or all of the nine elements. Speaking like Jobs depends on how you craft and deliver the story, not on PowerPoint versus Keynote.

## Scene 2: Answer the One Question That Matters Most

Jobs at WWDC 1997: "You've got to start with the customer experience and work back toward the technology—not the other way around." The audience wants to be *informed* about the product, *educated* on how it works and *entertained* while learning. Above all it wants the answer to **"Why should I care?"** "It's not about you. It's about them."

**Connect the dots.** Jobs says "what that means is . . ." and never leaves the audience guessing why a feature matters. Introducing the iMac, he named the target use (getting on the Internet "simply and fast"), listed what was wrong with every competing consumer computer, said what that meant for the user, and only then said "So, let me tell you about iMac." Announcing the switch to Intel, he raised the audience's objections himself ("Isn't the business great right now?"), then gave reasons in rising order of importance, ending with the products Apple could not build on the PowerPC road map.

**The two salespeople.** A customer asks for a light, fast notebook with a DVD drive. The first salesperson says "Intel Core 2 Duo" and explains two performance engines processing data simultaneously. That is technically accurate, and the customer leaves. "The brain is a lazy piece of meat that tries to preserve energy." Make it work too hard and you lose it. The second salesperson uses an analogy and then the benefit: "Think of the microprocessor as the brain of your computer . . . you get two brains," and "*what that means to you*" is downloading music during a full virus scan without slowing down, faster applications, better DVD playback and longer battery life. Plain English, tangible examples, relevance.

**Rewrite the self-description.** A CEO's opener ("premier developer of intelligent semiconductor intellectual property solutions") became: everyone hold up your cell phone; our software builds the chips inside many of them, and as those chips shrink your phones get smaller, last longer and play music and video. Strip words like *intelligent* and *solutions*. Reporters do this daily. Wikipedia's definition of server virtualization is opaque; the *New York Times* said it lets companies run many applications on each server, saving electricity and getting more out of their hardware.

**Say it outright.** If the product helps them make money, tell them. If it saves them money, tell them. If it makes a task easier or more enjoyable, tell them. "Tell them early, often, and clearly." Jobs explains the "why" before the "how."

**How Jobs sells the benefit.** Each line names the product and then the concrete gain to the user: Keynote is "like having a professional graphics department to create your slides"; the iPod nano gives "twice the storage capacity at the same price" and a twenty-four-hour battery; with Time Capsule "all your irreplaceable photos, movies, and documents are automatically protected"; iPhone 3G is "twice as fast at half the price"; Genius makes playlists of songs "that go great together, with just one click."

**Avoid self-indulgent, buzzword-filled wastes of time.** Answer the one question in every website page, slide and press release. Most press releases fail it: distribution agreements, awards and appointments nobody cares about (the "look at us" category). The fix is to lead with what the reader gets: a discount to celebrate the award, one eye-opening finding from the new report, the one thing the new CEO will do at once to improve service.

**Sell dreams, not products.** Asked why the iPhone mattered to Apple, Jobs skipped shareholder value and described a better experience: the Internet in your pocket, ten times easier to use. Guy Kawasaki: evangelism "is the purest and most passionate form of sales because you are selling a dream, not a tangible object." "Your audience doesn't care about your product. People care about themselves."

**Director's Notes.** Ask "Why should my listener care about this idea/information/product/service?" and name the one thing to take away. Make the one thing as clear as possible and repeat it at least twice. Eliminate buzzwords and jargon. Keep the one thing consistent across press releases, web pages and presentations.

## Scene 3: Develop a Messianic Sense of Purpose

"We're here to put a dent in the universe." To recruit John Sculley from PepsiCo, Jobs asked whether he wanted to sell sugared water for the rest of his life or have a chance to change the world. The **reality distortion field** (Bud Tribble's term) is Jobs's ability to convince anyone of practically anything. Its source is passion: the rhythm of his speech and an infectious enthusiasm for whatever he is talking about.

- **Do what you love.** "The only way to do great work is to love what you do. If you haven't found it yet, keep looking. Don't settle." Chasing money for its own sake is a sure way to lose your purpose. Jobs was worth over $100 million at twenty-five, and "it wasn't that important, because I never did it for the money." *Built to Last*: people are inspired by "core values and a sense of purpose beyond just making money." Chris Gardner: "Find something you love to do so much, you can't wait for the sun to rise to do it all over again."
- **Great presenters are passionate because they follow their hearts.** Their conversations become platforms to share that passion. Passion stirs listeners when it paints a picture of a more meaningful world that customers or employees can help create.
- **Rally people to a better future** (Marcus Buckingham's definition of leadership). A leader is "restless for change, impatient for progress, and deeply dissatisfied with the status quo." The friction between "what is" and "what could be" drives them. Jobs dreamed of an Apple computer for every person, and he shared the dream with anyone who would listen.
- **The vision is bigger than the product.** Jobs was never motivated to build computers; he wanted to create tools to unleash human potential, to make the world a better place. Howard Schultz rarely said the word *coffee* in an interview; his vision was the experience and the people. What you are passionate about is usually not the widget but how it improves customers' lives. "People want to be moved and inspired, and they want to believe in something. Make them believe in you." Everyone has a story to tell, in agriculture, cars, technology, finance or anything else.

**Director's Notes.** Dig deep for your true passion: ask "What am I really selling?" It is the dream of a better life, not the widget. Write a personal **passion statement**: one sentence telling prospects why you are genuinely excited about working with them, which will be remembered long after the mission statement is forgotten. If you are not doing what you love, consider a change. You can be financially successful in a job you hate, but you will never be considered an inspiring communicator.

## Scene 4: Create Twitter-Like Headlines

Reporters and audiences look for a category to place your product in and a one-sentence description of it. "Take the work out of it and write the headline yourself." Jobs's headlines work so well that the media run them word for word. "MacBook Air. The world's thinnest notebook." is thirty characters.

**Criteria.** Concise (140 characters maximum, counting letters, spaces and punctuation), specific, and offering a personal benefit, written from the user's perspective so that it answers "Why should I care?" "iPod. One thousand songs in your pocket." is concise (twenty-seven characters), specific (one thousand songs) and personal (*you* carry them). Compare the spec version Jobs could have given: an ultraportable MP3 player with a 6.5-ounce design and a 5 GB hard drive. Other headlines that meet all three: "The industry's greenest notebooks," "iPhone 3G. Twice as fast at half the price," "Apple reinvents the phone," and for the iMac, "The excitement of the Internet. The simplicity of Macintosh."

**The one-liner test.** Google described itself to Sequoia Capital as providing "access to the world's information in one click." Sequoia has asked entrepreneurs for their one-liner ever since. One investor: if you cannot describe what you do in ten words or less, "I'm not investing, I'm not buying, I'm not interested. Period." Other world-changing ten-word lines: "Cisco changes the way we live, work, play, and learn"; "Starbucks creates a third place between work and home"; Gates's "a PC on every desk, in every home." Most presenters cannot describe their company, product or service in one sentence.

**Write it early, build around it, repeat it everywhere.** The headline is set in the planning stage, before the slides, press release and marketing are done, and the rest of the presentation is built around it. On launch day the same line runs in the keynote, on the slide, in interviews, on the home page, in the press release, on billboards and in ads, reworded only slightly ("In a sentence, it's the world's thinnest notebook"; "We decided to build the world's thinnest notebook"). Jobs said "reinvent the phone" five times in the iPhone keynote.

**Headline sequence.** Deliver the headline before the details, describe the product (usually with a demo), and repeat the headline as soon as the explanation ends. For GarageBand he asked "What is GarageBand?" and answered "a major new pro music tool. But it's for everyone." The slide read "GarageBand. A major new pro music tool." He followed with a longer sentence (it turns your Mac into a pro-quality instrument and recording studio). "He reveals the headline, expands on it, and hammers it home again and again."

**Director's Notes.** Create the headline, a one-sentence vision statement for the company, product or service: concise, specific, offering a personal benefit. Repeat it consistently in conversations and in every piece of material. It offers a vision of a better future: "It's not about you. It's about them."

## Scene 5: Draw a Road Map

A **verbal road map** is a preview of coming attractions that tells listeners where you have been and where you are going. Jobs builds them in threes: "Today we are introducing three revolutionary products." There are no agenda slides in a Jobs presentation, only verbal road maps ("I've got four things I'd like to talk to you about today, so let's get started"), and when he names a numeral the slide shows only the number.

**Why three.** George Miller's 1956 "Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two" put short-term memory at seven to nine digits. Contemporary scientists put easy recall closer to three or four, and in Jobs's presentations three is far more common than four. "Three is the magic number." Comedians know three is funnier than two, writers that three is more dramatic than four, Jobs that three is more persuasive than five: the three musketeers, Goldilocks's three bears, "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness." The U.S. Marine Corps found three more effective than two or four and organizes in threes. So few communicators use the rule of three that you will stand apart simply by doing so. When you give three or four points in a media interview, reporters notice a missing one: "Didn't you say you had three points? I heard only two."

**The recipe.** Outline three (at most four) points, return to the first, explain each in depth, then summarize each. Write the way a *USA Today* reviewer does: headline, introduction, three points, conclusion. *USA Today* reviewers break a complex product into three named benefits, each with why it matters, such as battery life, graphics and wireless for a laptop chip.

**Building the three.** Number the items aloud and make each bigger than the last, so the third lands as the news. Jobs at the Intel announcement: the Mac has had two major transitions, 68K to PowerPC (a "good move") and OS 9 to OS X (even bigger), and "today it's time to begin a *third* transition." The theme sets up the count, and the count sets up the reveal. The 1984 Macintosh launch worked the same way: there had been only two milestone products in the industry, and today came the third.

**Threes everywhere.** Jobs described features in threes ("three major breakthroughs in iPod") and opened his Stanford commencement address with "three stories from my life." Steve Ballmer, unlike Gates, used groups of three at least five times in one keynote. JFK's speechwriter Ted Sorensen wrote "for the ear and not for the eye," listing goals in numbered sequence. Obama writes paragraphs of three sentences and puts three points inside sentences. Jimmy Valvano told his audience to laugh, think and cry every day. In 2008 DuPont asked each of its sixty thousand employees for three things they could do at once to conserve cash; people overwhelmed by more take no action. A golf coach's "hips, shift, follow" fixed a swing.

**Applying it to an interview.** Write a headline of 140 characters or less, then three big ideas, then under each the stories, examples and facts. Gallo's own interview opens with the headline ("deliver a presentation like Steve Jobs"), repeats it at least twice, announces "three steps" by name, then says "Let's talk about the first step." The same three-part map outlines a short interview, a long one or a whole presentation.

**Director's Notes.** List every key point you want the audience to know. Categorize the list until only three major message points remain; they are the verbal road map. Under each, add rhetorical devices: personal stories, facts, examples, analogies, metaphors and third-party endorsements.

## Scene 6: Introduce the Antagonist

In every classic story the hero fights the villain. "Every protagonist needs an enemy." The **antagonist** is an enemy, a problem in need of a solution. Introducing it rallies the audience around the hero. In 1983 Jobs cast IBM as Big Blue bent on dominating the industry ("Was George Orwell right?") and then showed the *1984* ad.

**Problem + solution = classic Jobs.** Jobs spent three minutes of the iPhone keynote on the villain: today's smartphones, which are "not so smart and . . . not so easy to use." He describes the pain the audience feels, gives a reason for it (usually competitors), and then offers a cure. He moves the story forward with rhetorical questions ("Why do we need a revolutionary user interface?") and even raises problems with his own solution (no keyboard, so how do we point? a stylus? "Who wants a stylus?") before answering them. How the slides track this argument is in [script-and-slide-patterns.md](script-and-slide-patterns.md). "In order to explain what our product is, we have to contrast it to what products are out there right now." Once the pain is established, listeners are receptive to what relieves it.

**"Why do we need this?"** One sentence is enough to introduce the antagonist. It lets you review the current state of the category and set the stage for the solution. "Why do we need our own browser?" led to two shortfalls of the existing browsers, speed and innovation, before the Safari demo.

**Will it eat me?** The brain needs a bucket, a category, for a new idea. It craves meaning before details. John Medina: primitive man facing a saber-toothed tiger asked "Will it eat me?", not "How many teeth does it have?" Start with the big picture, the problem, and arrange the details hierarchically around it.

**The cup for the coffee.** Venture capitalists at pitch events say most entrepreneurs jump to the product without setting up the problem: they have the coffee but no cup to pour it into. One investor: "You need to create a new space in my brain to hold the information you're about to deliver." TravelMuse's founder ran three steps: the category (online travel, over $90 billion in the United States), the problem (booking is the last 5 percent of the process; the 95 percent before it is the heavy lifting), the solution (planning tools integrated with content). Investors want the pervasive problem first. Then they move on to market size, competition and business model.

**The ultimate elevator pitch.** The antagonist takes Jobs a few minutes and can take you thirty seconds. Write a one-sentence answer to each of four questions: (1) What do you do? (2) What problem do you solve? (3) How are you different? (4) Why should I care? LanguageLine: the world's largest provider of phone interpretation for companies with non-English-speaking customers [what]; someone who doesn't speak English enters the country every twenty-three seconds [problem]; when that person calls a hospital, bank, insurer or 911, a LanguageLine interpreter is likely on the line [different]; we help you talk to customers in 150 languages [why care].

**A shared enemy builds belief.** Martin Lindstrom (*Buyology*): strong brands and religions share an unambiguous vision and a common enemy. The us-versus-them strategy attracts fans, creates loyalty and drives buying. Al Gore establishes a problem everyone can agree on, names the villain (fossil fuels), and makes the data physical: he rides a lift up his CO2 graph and keeps rising "off the charts."

**Director's Notes.** Introduce the antagonist early, and always establish the problem before revealing the solution. Paint a vivid picture of the customer's pain point and ask "Why do we need this?" Spend time on the problem: make it tangible and build the pain. Create the four-question elevator pitch, paying most attention to question 2, "What problem do you solve?" Nobody cares about your product; people care about solving their problems.

## Scene 7: Reveal the Conquering Hero

Once the antagonist of the moment (the limitation of current products) is in place, Jobs reveals the hero: the solution that makes life easier and more enjoyable.

**The villain narrative.** Before the 1984 ad, Jobs told IBM's history decade by decade. IBM passed on xerography, dismissed the minicomputer, then dismissed the personal computer Apple invented. Then David matched Goliath, and now IBM "wants it all." Apple was the only force that would ensure the dealers' future freedom. Each beat raises the stakes until the hero has to act.

**The hero's mission** is not necessarily to slay the bad guy but to make our lives better. For the iPod, Jobs first asked "Why music?" (everyone loves it, it is part of everyone's life, a huge market with no leader; "We found the recipe"), then toured the landscape by cost per song: a $75 CD player at about $5 a song, a $150 flash player at about $10 a song, a $150 MP3 CD player at $1 a song, a $300 hard-drive jukebox at thirty cents a song. "That's where we want to be." The hero, the iPod, holds a thousand songs, and its biggest quality is restated: your entire music library fits in your pocket, which was never possible before.

**The benefit has to be immediate.** In the Get a Mac ads the PC is stuffy, slow and frustrated and the Mac is friendly. Out of the box, the Mac is ready to make a movie and a website, while the PC has drivers to download and manuals to read. Microsoft's "I'm a PC" answer lacked emotional punch because it had no villain. App Store ads set a problem and solve it in thirty seconds: you know when a song is playing and you can't name it? Hold up the phone. "Don't spend too much time getting to the punch line."

**Sell the experience.** After the villain and the hero, show how the hero offers the victim, the consumer, an escape from the villain's grip, simply and without jargon. Apple's site lists why you'll love a Mac ("It's gorgeous inside and out; it does what a PC does, only better") rather than gigahertz and SDRAM. Customers buy the experience the processor provides, not the processor. Introducing the Intel MacBook Pro, Jobs named the "pesky little problem" (no G5 would fit in a PowerBook; "We've consulted every possible higher authority," over a photo of the pope), then the hero: four to five times faster, the thinnest, a built-in camera, "Videoconferencing to go." Know your audience well enough to voice the problem it really has.

**Passion drives it.** Jobs said perseverance separates successful entrepreneurs from the rest, and it comes from passion: you need "an idea or a problem or a wrong that you want to right."

**Director's Notes.** Describe the industry or product category as it stands now, then your vision of where it could be. Once the antagonist (the customer's pain point) is established, describe in plain English how your company, product or service cures that pain. Unless you are passionate about a problem you want to make right, you won't have the perseverance to stick it out.

## Intermission 1: Obey the Ten-Minute Rule

"Your audience checks out after ten minutes. Not in eleven minutes, but ten." Medina's peer-reviewed sources and his own classes agree: the brain gets bored on a stubborn timing pattern. Jobs never gives it time. Within a thirty-minute stretch he includes demonstrations, a second or third speaker, and video clips. Exactly ten minutes into Macworld 2007 he showed a new iPod ad, an intermission between his first act (music) and his second (Apple TV). Never speak for more than ten minutes without a break in the action: a video, a story, another speaker or a demo.
