# Script and Slide Patterns

Patterns distilled from Jobs's keynotes for drafting a presentation as a two-column script: what the speaker says on the left, what the slide shows on the right, with emphasis, pauses and gestures marked in brackets. Each pattern gives the move and a short example. Use them while drafting the script, and check the finished script against them. Benefit lines and channel-consistent headlines are in Scenes 2 and 4 of [act-1-create-the-story.md](act-1-create-the-story.md).

## The slide holds the fragment; the speaker says the sentence

The slide carries a three-to-five-word piece of the line being spoken, never the full sentence. Launching Keynote, Jobs called it an app "for when your presentation really counts" while the slide read "When your presentation really counts"; "built for me" sat beside him on a slide that said "Built for me." Then, rather than slides about slides, he went to the demo. The press release used the same line.

| Said | Slide |
|---|---|
| It's a presentation app for when your presentation really counts. | When your presentation really counts |

## The echo build (introducing the antagonist)

The slide shows one word or short phrase echoing the speaker's key term, and each beat adds one line or swaps one image. Rhetorical questions move the story: raise the problem with your own solution, then answer it. The iPhone's villain section ran this way for three minutes:

| Said | Slide |
|---|---|
| The most advanced phones are called smartphones. | Smartphone |
| They're a phone plus e-mail plus a baby Internet. | Smartphone / Phone + Email + Internet |
| They're not so smart and not so easy to use. | Not so smart. Not so easy to use. |
| Here are four of them, the usual suspects. | [photo of four competitor phones] |
| The problem is the bottom forty: the keyboards are there whether you need them or not. | [top halves fade, leaving only keyboards] |
| How will we communicate with it? A stylus? Who wants a stylus? | Who wants a stylus? |
| We're born with ten pointing devices: our fingers. | [stylus fades out, a finger fades in] |
| A new technology called multi-touch. | Multi-Touch |

The pattern scales to any category villain. Name the category, define it in the speaker's mocking shorthand, state its failings as a short phrase, show the rivals, fade away everything but the flaw, then pose the question the hero answers.

## One stat per slide, in the spoken words

Each number gets its own slide, and the slide shows the same words the speaker is saying. When the speaker says "music," the only word on screen is "Music." Jobs opened Macworld 2008 with a date ("2007"), a two-word thanks, the numeral "1" as he named the first of four topics, and then one figure on a slide of its own ("5,000,000 copies delivered in first 3 months"). Seven words, three numbers and one date in four slides. An iTunes update ran the same way:

| Said | Slide |
|---|---|
| Let's start with iTunes. | iTunes |
| We now offer over eight and a half million songs. | 8,500,000 songs |
| (each category in turn) | 125,000 podcasts / 2,600 Hollywood movies / … |
| That's sixty-five million customers. | 65,000,000 accounts with credit cards |

Slide words that don't match the speech split the audience's attention. A slide can also answer something without a word being spoken about it. Facing rumors about his health, Jobs gestured at a slide reading "The reports of my death are greatly exaggerated," said "Enough said," and moved on to "Music."

## Name it, define it, show it, signal it, diagram it

For an abstract feature, Jobs used a fixed sequence. Genius ran like this:

1. **Headline word.** "Genius is pretty cool." The slide shows only "Genius."
2. **One-sentence definition,** condensed on the slide.
3. **Show it:** a screenshot of a song selected.
4. **Signal it:** an animated circle surrounds the Genius button as he names it.
5. **Diagram the mechanism in line drawings, one element per sentence.** A cloud appears for the store; an arrow rises from your library to the cloud as he says your information goes up anonymously; many libraries appear for millions of users; a dozen arrows rise for "and so are they"; the logo is replaced by "Smarter" as it gets smarter and smarter; an arrow comes back down for the tailored results.
6. **Restate the definition,** then go to the demo.

Line drawings, few words and colorful images carry the explanation, so the audience never hunts for its place.

## One feature, one photo

Jobs introduced the iPhone 3G in eleven slides, and only one of them had words. The title opens and closes the run, and each spoken feature gets a single photograph from the angle that proves it:

| Said | Slide |
|---|---|
| It's iPhone's first birthday. | [cake with one candle] |
| Introducing the iPhone 3G. | iPhone 3G |
| It's thinner at the edges. | [edge-on view, barely visible: the empty space shows thinness] |
| A solid metal button. / Camera. / A flush headphone jack. | [one close-up per line] |
| Improved audio. Dramatically improved audio. | [top view] |
| It's really quite wonderful. The iPhone 3G. | iPhone 3G |

The mediocre version is one slide titled "iPhone 3G" with seven bullets: thinner at the edges, full plastic back, solid metal buttons, and so on. Rebuild any bulleted feature list this way.

## The demo with silences

A demo runs as a head-to-head proof with the speaker silent while the screen does the work:

| Said | Slide or stage |
|---|---|
| Why do you want 3G? The browser and e-mail attachments. | [two icons] |
| Same place, same site, EDGE and 3G. Let's see how we do. | [two phones loading the same rich web page; silence while they load] |
| Twenty-one seconds on 3G. | [waits silently about thirty more seconds, arms crossed, smiling, until the EDGE phone finishes] |
| Fifty-nine seconds on EDGE. 3G is 2.8 times faster. It's amazingly zippy! | |

It passes Kawasaki's five S's. It is short (under two minutes), simple (two sites loading), sweet (a head-to-head against the old technology), swift (it keeps moving, but goes silent at the critical points to build drama) and substantial (waiting for rich sites is a real problem). Dramatize the verdict by letting the audience watch the slow side lose.

## The reveal build

Raise anticipation sentence by sentence and hold the product back until the build peaks. The iPhone's three-minute introduction was the most memorable part of the keynote. Jobs took a swig of water and walked to center stage in twelve seconds of silence before starting:

| Said | Slide |
|---|---|
| I've been looking forward to this day for two and a half years. Every once in a while a revolutionary product comes along that changes everything. | [Apple logo] |
| In 1984 Macintosh changed the computer industry. In 2001 the first iPod changed the music industry. | [Mac photo, "1984"] / [iPod photo, "2001"] |
| Today we're introducing three revolutionary products of this class. | [Apple logo] |
| A widescreen iPod with touch controls. A revolutionary mobile phone. A breakthrough Internet communications device. | [one image and a short label per product] |
| An iPod, a phone, an Internet communicator. Are you getting it? | [three images, then rotating] |
| This is one device, and we are calling it iPhone. | iPhone |
| Today Apple is going to reinvent the phone! | Apple reinvents the phone |
| And here it is. | [gag image: an iPod with a rotary dial; laughter] |

The structure: history of past revolutions, a promise of three, each of the three named, the three recapped, the twist that they are one, the name, the headline, and then a laugh before the real product. The flat version, "The next product we would like to introduce is called iPhone," gives it all away in the first sentence.

## Gestures that literalize the words

Mark the key word in each sentence and give it a matching gesture. Jobs introducing the seventeen-inch PowerBook:

| Said (*key word*) | Gesture |
|---|---|
| The number *one lust object*. | raises an index finger |
| Nobody has caught up with it in *two years*. | two fingers |
| No one is even *close*. | chops the air |
| We do that with *this*. | [pause] |
| It's *stunning*. | [pause] |
| It's only *one inch* thick. | a thin gesture between thumb and finger |
| I happen to have one right here. | walks to stage right, holding eye contact |
| Look at how *thin* it is. | shuts it and holds it up |

The pattern: a number becomes fingers, a size claim becomes a size gesture, a broad claim gets expansive open palms, and a pause follows each reveal line and each one-word verdict.

## Pauses on the ordinal, volume climbing to the headline

Put the pause inside the sentence, after the ordinal and before the item, so the suspense sits mid-sentence, and let volume and rate climb steadily to a peak on the headline. In the iPhone reveal:

> Three revolutionary products. The *first* one [pause] is a widescreen iPod with touch controls. The *second* [pause] is a *revolutionary mobile phone* [louder]. And the *third* [pause] is a *breakthrough* Internet communications device. [pause] An iPod, a phone, and an Internet communicator [louder]. An iPod, a phone, are you getting it? [faster, louder] This is *one* device [louder still], and we are calling it *iPhone* [even louder]. Today Apple is going to *reinvent* the *phone*! [loudest of the presentation]

Earlier lines in the same build each end on a pause: after "two and a half years," after "changes everything," after each industry it changed.

## Voice the objection, then land the key word softest

Anticipate the audience's objection, say it in their words, and answer it with the key word delivered slowest and quietest. For the iPod:

> You might be saying, "This is cool, but I've got a hard disk in my portable computer." [slows] "So, what's so special about iPod here?" [pauses, lowers volume] It's ultraportable. *Ultra*portable. Let me show you what I mean. [speeds up] It's the size of a deck of cards, and it weighs 6.5 ounces, lighter than most of the cell phones in your pockets. [slows, lowers] It is ultraportable. [almost a whisper]

## A label per slide; some comparisons left unspoken

When the slides double as the prompter, each carries a two-to-four-word label naming its one feature, and the speaker supplies the benefit sentence. From the MacBook Air:

| Said | Slide |
|---|---|
| It's the world's thinnest notebook. | World's thinnest notebook |
| A magnetic latch, no hooks to catch on your clothes. | [photo] Magnetic latch |
| A full-size keyboard; it's phenomenal. | Full size keyboard |
| How did we fit a Mac in here? I'm still stunned. | [side photo] How did we fit a Mac in here? |
| This is how big the board is. | [the motherboard beside a pencil, smaller than the pencil] |
| We didn't compromise on performance. | [processor photo] |

Jobs never mentions the pencil. The visual comparison speaks for itself, and saying it would weaken it.

## The later launches

"The products might change, but the storytelling techniques remain the same." Four later launches followed the same template, so it works as a checklist for any launch script:

- **The update run.** Open with a category photo, then one number per slide. The iPad keynote's first three minutes held fourteen words, five photographs and five numbers: a photo of iPods and "250,000,000"; a store photo, "284 Apple Retail Stores" and "50,000,000 visitors last quarter"; the App Store logo, "140,000 applications in the App Store" and "3 billion applications downloaded."
- **The history of milestones.** Place the new product as the next step in a line of past ones. The iPad2 keynote's first five minutes held thirty-three words. After a slide for iBooks, a check for two billion dollars and "100 million books downloaded," the run was "Apple's third post-PC blockbuster" over a photo of the iPod (2001), the iPhone (2007) and the iPad (2010).
- **Story before headline.** The iPhone 4 keynote walked the line's history first (2007 reinvented the phone, 2008 brought 3G and the App Store, 2009 doubled the speed) before landing "the biggest leap since the original iPhone."
- **Stack the per-unit translation.** Two million iPads in fifty-nine days became one iPad sold every three seconds. Thirty-five million app downloads became seventeen per iPad, and five million books in sixty-five days two and a half per iPad. Nine millimeters became 24 percent thinner, a quarter thinner, the thinnest smartphone on the planet. Fifteen million iPads in nine months was more than every tablet PC ever sold.
- **No words at all.** The iCloud keynote's first ten slides carried no text, only device images and subtle animation. The first words, on slide eleven, were the seventy-two-character headline, "iCloud stores your content and wirelessly pushes it to all your devices," which ran unchanged on the website, in the press release and in the stores. The slides *complement* the story; they are not the story.
- **A three-part headline the press repeats.** "First, it's dramatically faster. Second, it's dramatically thinner. And it's lighter as well" became "thinner, lighter, and faster," and newspapers and magazines ran the phrase. The same words went into the e-mail subject line and onto the website. If you can't describe your product in 140 characters or less, go back to the drawing board. Make sure everyone on your team is speaking from the same playbook.
- **A category villain.** For the iPad, Jobs asked whether there was room for a third category between the laptop and the smartphone, said it would have to be far better at key tasks or have no reason for being, then dismissed netbooks as "just cheap laptops." For the iPad2 the villain was rivals treating tablets as the next PC and competing on speeds and feeds.
- **The ten-minute clock.** At the iPhone 4 launch, a four-minute video of iPad coverage, then exactly ten minutes later the Netflix CEO onstage. At the iPad2 launch, nine minutes in, a video called "The Year of the iPad."
- **A close in plain, warm words.** The iPad close slowed down for the headline restated and ended "We hope you love the iPad as much as we do." Have the courage to say "love" and "fun."
