# Act 2: Deliver the Experience

Scenes 8 to 13 and Intermission 2. "Steve Jobs does not deliver a presentation. He offers an experience." Think of a Broadway play: multiple characters, elaborate props, stunning visual backdrops, and one glorious moment that makes the ticket worth it. Slide-by-slide examples of each technique are distilled in [script-and-slide-patterns.md](script-and-slide-patterns.md).

## Scene 8: Channel Their Inner Zen

"Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication" (Jobs, quoting Leonardo da Vinci). Apple makes sophisticated technology "comprehensible to mere mortals" by eliminating features and clutter, and Jobs designs slides the same way: where most presenters add words, he "removes and removes and removes." A Jobs presentation is "strikingly simple, visual, and devoid of bullet points . . . no bullet points. Ever." The Zen principle behind it is **kanso**, simplicity: great beauty and powerful messages through simplification. Hans Hofmann: "The ability to simplify means to eliminate the unnecessary so that the necessary may speak." "The task of leaders is to simplify. You should be able to explain where you have to go in two minutes."

**Why bullets fail.** "The brain is fundamentally a lazy piece of meat" (Gregory Berns). It conserves energy, and the standard template (title, bullet, sub-bullet, sub-sub-bullet, "really in the weeds") overloads it. Garr Reynolds calls slides that try to be documents **slideuments**: the kill-two-birds approach kills only effective communication. Putting the words you are saying on the slide as text usually hurts the message. Seth Godin: bullets tell the audience "Write this down, but don't really pay attention to it now"; nobody takes notes at the opera. Nancy Duarte: "It's laziness on the presenter's part to put everything on one slide." Slides like Jobs's stand out simply because so few people make them.

**Count the words.** The average PowerPoint slide has forty words (Medina). Jobs's first four Macworld 2008 slides held seven words, three numbers, one date and no bullet points. **The Ten-Forty Rule** (Gallo's): your first ten slides should contain forty words or less. Jobs went further at the iCloud launch: no words on the first ten slides, only device images and subtle animation, with the first text, the headline, on slide eleven.

**Slide words match spoken words.** When Jobs said "we're going to talk about music," the only word on the screen was "Music." Words that don't match what you are saying split the audience's attention between you and the screen. Simple slides keep the focus on the speaker.

**Mayer's principles of multimedia learning** (Richard Mayer, UC Santa Barbara, studying multimedia learning since 1991). Jobs's slides follow each one:

- **Multimedia representation:** "It is better to present an explanation in words and pictures than solely in words." Groups taught with text plus pictures, animation and video recalled more, in some cases twenty years later.
- **Contiguity:** present corresponding words and pictures together, not apart. Captioned illustrations beside the text produced 65 percent better performance than plain text, because the brain builds a verbal and a visual model and links them.
- **Split attention:** present words as spoken narration rather than on-screen text. Spoken words have more impact; too many words to read overload the brain.
- **Coherence:** use few rather than many extraneous words and pictures. Redundant or irrelevant information impedes learning.
- **Signaling:** the ideal slide is an image plus a simple line drawing that directs the eye to what you want seen (an arrow, an animated circle), so the audience spends no effort finding its place.

**White space.** Reynolds sees "restraint, simplicity, and powerful yet subtle use of empty space" in Jobs's slides. The biggest mistake is filling every centimeter. Duarte: "clutter is a failure of design." Empty space implies elegance, quality and clarity. One slide showed the iPhone 3G edge-on, so slim it barely appeared: the empty space carried the idea.

**The picture superiority effect (PSE).** Visual and verbal information travel different channels in the brain, and ideas are far more likely to be remembered as pictures than as words. Medina: information presented orally is about 10 percent remembered seventy-two hours later, and 65 percent if you add a picture. To the cortex, words are just many tiny pictures. To present the iPhone 3G, Jobs used eleven slides, only one of them with words. A mediocre presenter would have put the same seven features on one bulleted slide. For the MacBook Air he showed one photo of the computer lying on a manila envelope, no text. Technical detail went to the website. *USA Today*'s "snapshots," which pair statistics with images, are models worth studying.

**Confidence.** Photos instead of words remove the crutch of reading, so you must have the message down cold.

**Simplify the words too.** Replace the accurate, wordy description with a line that fits in a Twitter post. Energy Star compliance, no brominated flame retardants, PVC-free cables and mercury-free displays became "They are the industry's greenest notebooks." Time Capsule became "plug it in, click a few buttons, and voilà—all the Macs in your house are backed up automatically." Einstein: "If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough." The UK's **Plain English Campaign** defines plain English as writing the intended audience can read, understand and act on the first time they read (or hear) it. Its before-and-afters turn a sentence offering to furnish further particulars by telephone into "If you have any questions, please call." Nearly everything in a memo, e-mail or presentation can be edited this way. Paul Arden: people come to see you, not to read your words, so paint them a picture.

**Director's Notes.** Avoid bullet points: always, or almost always. They are fine on pages meant to be read (books, documents, e-mails), but not on slides. Pictures are superior. Focus on one theme per slide and complement it with a photograph or image. Learn to make visually aesthetic slides; you do not have to be an artist.

## Scene 9: Dress Up Your Numbers

"Rarely do numbers resonate with people until those numbers are placed in a context that people can understand." The way to make them understood is to tie them to something the audience already knows. Average presenters spew numbers without context and assume the audience shares their excitement. Jobs makes his numbers **specific, relevant, and contextual**:

- **Specific:** 7,500 songs, not "thousands" of songs.
- **Relevant:** tied to what this audience does. A 30 GB iPod became 7,500 songs, 25,000 photos or up to 75 hours of video, for people who want their media on the go.
- **Contextual:** framed against what this audience cares about. For investors, iPhone share was set against the competitors.

**Keep translating until it lands.** The first iPod's 5 GB meant nothing. A thousand songs was better but still not compelling against cheaper, bigger rivals. 6.5 ounces, small enough to "fit in your pocket," and Jobs pulling one from his own pocket, clicked. Keep stacking context too: four million iPhones sold, which is twenty thousand a day on average, nearly 20 percent of the market, then "What does this mean in terms of the overall market?" and a slide showing the iPhone equal to the remaining three competitors combined after ninety days. Apple's 5 percent market share sounded tiny until Jobs noted it was greater than BMW's or Mercedes's in the car industry. A speed claim (2.8 times faster than EDGE) became the same web page loading in twenty-one seconds versus fifty-nine, and then "twice as fast at half the price."

**Dress up numbers with analogies.** The more complex the idea, the more you need rhetorical devices. SanDisk's 12 GB phone card had fifty billion transistors: think of each as an ant, and end to end they would circle the globe twice. Then "What does this mean to you?": six hours of movies, or music all the way to the moon and back. Intel's Core i7 packed 730 million transistors where a chip from twenty-seven years earlier had 29,000. That is the population of Ithaca, New York, versus Europe, with Europe shrunk to fit in Ithaca.

**Number smiths.** IBM defined a petaflop (a thousand trillion calculations per second) as the power of 100,000 laptops, a stack of laptops 1.5 miles high, the whole world's population on calculators for 46 years to match one day's work, and gas mileage of 200,000 miles per gallon had cars improved at the same rate. The media used the comparisons word for word. "The bigger the number, the more important it is to place the number into a context." A reporter made the $700 billion bailout concrete for Silicon Valley readers: twenty-five times the Google founders' combined wealth, 350 billion venti lattes, $2,300 for every American, or free college for twenty-three million students. Climate groups break 13 trillion pounds of U.S. CO2 down to 44,000 pounds per American against a world average of 9,600, then state the consequences. A doctor turned 220 over 140 into a comparison with 120 over 80 as normal, then into heart attack, kidney disease and stroke, and the patient changed at once. The pattern is number, benchmark, what it means to you, consequence. "Numbers out of context are simply unimpressive."

**Director's Notes.** Use data to support the key theme, choose the figures carefully, and don't overwhelm the audience with too many numbers. Make the data specific, relevant and contextual to the lives of your listeners. Use analogies to dress the numbers up.

## Scene 10: Use "Amazingly Zippy" Words

The iPhone 3G's 3 Mbps against 144 Kbps became "It's amazingly zippy." Of the OS X interface: "We made the buttons on the screen look so good, you'll want to lick them." Jobs's words are simple, clear and direct, and fun, tangible and uncommon in business presentations.

**The plain English test.** A reporter ran Jobs's Macworld keynotes and Gates's CES keynotes through UsingEnglish.com's analyzer, which scores four things (lower is better on each):

1. **Average words per sentence.**
2. **Lexical density:** how hard a text is to read.
3. **Hard words:** the percentage of words with more than three syllables.
4. **Fog index:** years of education needed to understand the text. The *New York Times* scores 11 or 12; some academic documents 18.

| | Jobs 2007 | Gates 2007 | Jobs 2008 | Gates 2008 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Words per sentence | 10.5 | 21.6 | 13.79 | 18.23 |
| Lexical density | 16.5% | 21.0% | 15.76% | 24.52% |
| Hard words | 2.9% | 5.11% | 3.18% | 5.2% |
| Fog index | 5.5 | 10.7 | 6.79 | 9.37 |

Side by side, Jobs gives one fact per sentence and turns totals into rates (five million songs a day is fifty-eight every second), while Gates stacks beta counts, test-years and phrases like "the discoverability of the richness." "Where Gates is obtuse, Jobs is clear. Where Gates is abstract, Jobs is tangible. Where Gates is complex, Jobs is simple." Gates could get away with it because he invented Windows. "You, however, did not." Strive for understanding. Avoid lexical density.

**Three characteristics** of the words Jobs uses to announce a product:

- **Simple:** free of jargon, few syllables.
- **Concrete:** very specific phrases; short, tangible descriptions instead of long, abstract discussion.
- **Emotional:** descriptive adjectives.

The MacBook Air introduction runs all three in a few lines: you can get a feel for how thin it is (concrete); full-size keyboard and display (simple); isn't it amazing? (emotional); a gorgeous 13.3-inch widescreen display (emotional and concrete); I'm stunned our engineering team could pull this off (emotional). Jobs's repertoire includes "Insanely great," "The number one lust object," "the funnest iPod we've ever created" (a word he made up), "A giant leap beyond PC notebooks. Miraculously engineered," and, on a seventeen-inch notebook, "I asked you to buckle up. Now I want you to put on your shoulder harness."

**Watercooler words, said authentically.** His favorites, *amazing*, *incredible*, *gorgeous*, are everyday conversation, and he speaks the same way onstage as off. Choose words that authentically represent your product. A financial adviser who calls a new fund revolutionary sounds insincere. Calling mutual funds amazing products that grow your money while lowering risk, and saying you're especially excited about a new one, is simple and emotional and still professional. "If you genuinely find a product 'amazing,' go ahead and say so." Hype is hype only if there's no "there" there; Jobs is less a hype-master than "the master of the catchphrase."

**Avoid jargon creep.** **Jargon** is language specific to a particular industry, and it blocks the free exchange of ideas, even between divisions of the same company. Mission statements are the worst culprits: committee-made paragraphs of "synergy," "principle-centered" and "best of breed." Apple's runs on emotive verbs instead (ignited, reinvented, lead, spearheading). Suze Orman: too many people want to impress others with what they know; making the message as simple as possible is respectful, like giving the simplest directions to your house. Complexity comes from "our fear of not being important." Jack Welch: "Insecure managers create complexity." He would ask presenters to take him through the basics as if in high school. Speaking over people's heads can cost you a job. David Pogue wants a pitch that says exactly what the product is and does, not "integrated," "B2B" or "consumer-centric." The Bad Pitch blog's mantra: "A good pitch disappears and turns into the story; a bad pitch becomes the story."

**It's like this . . .** Compare a new product to something widely understood, commonly used and well known: "Apple TV is like a DVD player for the twenty-first century"; iPod Shuffle is "smaller and lighter than a pack of gum"; iPod is "the size of a deck of cards." When an analogy works, stick with it. The more you repeat it, the more people remember it, and the press repeats it too. Listeners need a mental bucket for a new concept, so create it for them. The brain takes shortcuts whenever it can (Berns), and "analogies are shortcuts."

**Zip carries past the stage.** Cranium sold a million games with no advertising on the principle "Never forget that your customers are your sales force." Its fans are "Craniacs," and its employees choose their own job titles (the founder's is Grand Poo-Bah). Fun, memorable language makes people want to repeat it.

**Director's Notes.** Unclutter your copy: eliminate redundant language, buzzwords and jargon. Edit, edit, and edit some more. Run your paragraphs through the UsingEnglish tool to see how dense they are. Have fun with words: superlatives and descriptive adjectives are fine for expressing enthusiasm. Wanting to "lick" the buttons is confidence.

## Scene 11: Share the Stage

A Jobs presentation is rarely a one-man play. Supporting characters perform key roles in the narrative. The Intel CEO walked out in a clean-room bunny suit to hand Jobs a wafer and report "Intel is ready." Jobs then revealed that the first Intel Mac was shipping not in June but today. Great actors are "giving": "Everyone needs to shine for the good of the show."

- **The brain craves variety.** Speeches for Kennedy, Reagan and Obama were scripted to run no longer than twenty minutes. Jobs's keynotes run about 1.5 hours and hold attention with demos, video and guest speakers.
- **Share credit, even with a rival.** At Macworld Boston 1997, Jobs brought Bill Gates in by satellite to announce Microsoft's investment in Apple. Over the boos he told the audience to let go of the notion that for Apple to win, Microsoft has to lose.
- **Know what you don't know.** Jobs handed the unibody manufacturing story to designer Jony Ive for six minutes, then retook the stage, thanked him and restated the segment's headline, "A new way to build notebooks." Other actors add credibility and excitement.
- **Your best sales tool** is a customer's endorsement. Facing skepticism about movie rentals, Jobs brought out Twentieth Century Fox's chairman to say why the studio signed first. "A reference is good. A customer or partner physically sharing the stage is even better."
- **The number one reason people buy** is word of mouth. Prospects don't want to act as a beta group, and they want to minimize risk. Your product must save customers money, make them money, or help them use their money more efficiently, especially in tough times, and testimonials prove that it does. Launch with customers who tested the product and partners who will endorse it publicly, so you have instant references. Develop a list of reference customers and give them a *reason* to vouch: deeper access to you and your staff, input into new designs, visibility. A customer who gives a testimonial is worth more than one who doesn't. A cheap camcorder testimonial posted online carries as much weight as a slick production.
- **The media.** Jobs read favorable reviews aloud over a slide of the quotes. The *PC* magazine line praising a Mac operating system drew laughs. Top-tier endorsements give buyers confidence.
- **Give credit where credit's due.** At Macworld 2007's close, Jobs asked everyone who worked on the products to stand for applause and thanked their families for the missed dinners. He opened Macworld 2008 by thanking customers. Public credit shows integrity and inspires employees to work harder.
- **Poke fun at yourself.** Jobs brought the actor who played him in a TV movie onstage and coached his impression.

**Director's Notes.** For a new product or service, have customers who tested it and can back your claims, plus reviews from reputable publications or popular blogs. Put testimonials in the presentation: videotape the customer, edit to no more than two minutes, and insert it. Publicly thank employees, partners and customers, and do it often.

## Scene 12: Stage Your Presentation with Props

Kahney: Jobs's keynotes are "marketing theater, staged for the world's press." Jobs uses props in every presentation, usually in demos. When he introduced the unibody MacBook, Apple staff at the row ends passed aluminum frames through the audience ("We need them back"). He stayed silent for sixty seconds to let the product speak, added a line of color commentary, went silent for thirty more, then closed: "You're the first to get your hands on one." It turned an explanation into a multisensory experience.

**The Kawasaki method.** Guy Kawasaki: the right demo costs little and can counteract competitors' marketing. It informs, communicates the benefits and inspires action. A good demo is:

- **Short:** it doesn't suck the wind out of the audience.
- **Simple:** no more than one or two key messages, enough to tantalize but not bewilder.
- **Sweet:** it shows the hottest features and differentiates you. Show real functionality: imagine someone shouting "So what?" at every feature.
- **Swift:** fast paced. "Never do anything in a demo that lasts more than fifteen seconds."
- **Substantial:** it shows how the product solves a real-world problem the audience has.

Jobs's iPhone 3G demo at WWDC 2008 meets all five (it is laid out in the patterns file). It lasted under two minutes, showed nothing but two phones loading the same page, put 3G head to head against EDGE, kept moving but went silent at the critical moments, and solved a real problem: waiting for rich sites to load.

**Focus on the one thing.** Each product has many features; Jobs often demos just one, like a movie trailer that shows only the best parts. He showed Safari for Windows' speed alone, side by side with Internet Explorer (6.64 seconds against 13.56), in under three minutes. "I'm going to focus on *one thing* to demo today" introduced GarageBand's podcast studio. His demos, like his slides, carry no extraneous messages.

**Stagecraft.** Jobs walks to stage right (the audience's left) and sits facing the audience so it has a clear view of the screen. He scripts calls and gags into demos: a live call to Jony Ive, a conference call with Phil Schiller, a mock "Supersecret Apple Rumors" podcast, Photo Booth's teenage effects on his own face, and a "four thousand lattes" crank call to Starbucks that was written into his notes beforehand. "It is precisely because he has fun that people enjoy watching him." When the audience is not like you, bring in someone who speaks directly to it. John Mayer played for the GarageBand demo, and one entrepreneur handed his demo to two teenage users of his teen-market site. Cisco's John Chambers has a scripted straight man, Jim Grubb, whose title is Chief Demonstration Officer, and Grubb's team rehearses for countless hours in the lab so the demo is simple and actually works. Surprise lives here too: the system Jobs had been demoing all along turned out to be running on Intel.

**Three types of learners.** Demos reach all three:

- **Visual** (about 40 percent): few words, plenty of pictures. People can't connect with what they haven't internalized.
- **Auditory** (about 20 to 30 percent): verbal and rhetorical techniques, personal stories, vivid examples.
- **Kinesthetic:** they learn by doing, moving and touching, and get bored listening. Pass objects around, run writing exercises, bring them into the demo.

Marco Montemagno, an Italian TV host, uses three rules: give the audience something to do (sketch the neighbor, pass papers along); ask someone to share the stage (volunteers race to fold a T-shirt); make use of your own skills onstage (a table-tennis rally). Online, use polls, draw on the screen, and hand mouse control to the prospect. A prop is anything that takes attention away from the deck, such as reading a letter, writing on a flip chart or a physical product, and it gives the audience a break from the slides.

**Director's Notes.** Build a product demo into the planning phase: short, sweet and substantial. Bring in another team member if you can. Commit to the demo; comedians say a joke works only if you commit to it, so have fun with it. Provide something for every type of learner.

## Scene 13: Reveal a "Holy Shit" Moment

"This is the MacBook Air, so thin it even fits inside one of those envelopes you see floating around the office." Jobs pulled it from a manila envelope and held it up, and that photo became the image of the event. The moment was planned all along: the press releases, website images and ads showing a hand pulling the notebook from an envelope were ready before he walked on. The presentation is theater, scripted to provoke an emotional response.

**One theme.** The secret to a memorable moment is to identify the one thing, the one theme, the audience should remember after leaving the room, with no notes or slides needed to recall it. "They will forget many of the details, but they will remember 100 percent of what they *feel*." The moment brings the headline to life; "1,000 songs in your pocket" stayed a tagline until Jobs pulled the iPod from his pocket.

**The mental Post-it note.** "The brain doesn't pay attention to boring things" (Medina). It does attend to an emotionally charged event. The amygdala releases dopamine, which aids memory, so it is as if a Post-it note on the idea reads "Remember this!"

**Build the drama.** Like a playwright, never give away the big moment out of the gate. Add layers until you hit the big note. The iPod went: it holds a thousand songs; the coolest thing is your whole library fits in your pocket; it is the size of a deck of cards (over a photo of a card deck); 2.4 by 4 inches, under three-quarters of an inch thick, 6.5 ounces, lighter than your phone; photographs of it; and finally "I happen to have one right here in my pocket!" The 1984 Macintosh came out of a canvas bag and spoke for itself in a digitized voice ("Never trust a computer you can't lift"). Even dropping "interim" from his CEO title became a moment: at the end of a two-hour keynote, "one more thing," first applause for the team and thanks to the agencies, then the news, then credit back to everyone at Apple.

**It need not be a product.** A personal story can be the moment. An organic grower's statistics were forgotten, but a farmer's story was remembered: on the conventional farm he had to shower and disinfect his clothes before his children could hug him, and now he walks off the lettuce field into their arms.

**The flat version versus the built version.** The iPhone could have been introduced as "The next product we would like to introduce is called iPhone." Instead three minutes of build (laid out in the patterns file) made every sentence raise the listener's anticipation. Every Jobs presentation, major or minor, is scripted to have one moment that leaves everyone talking, the way a Spielberg film has one scene that sticks for years.

**Director's Notes.**
- **Plan a "holy shit" moment.** It needn't be a breakthrough: a personal story, new and unexpected information, or a demo can do it. Look for emotions that uplift people, make them laugh or make them think. The more unexpected, the better.
- **Script the moment.** Build up before laying it on, the way a novel doesn't give away the plot on page one (*The Sixth Sense* saves its twist for the end). Create at least one moment that will have them talking well after you finish.
- **Rehearse the big moment.** It must come off crisp, polished and effortless. Make sure the demos work and the slides appear when they should.

## Intermission 2: Schiller Learns from the Best

When Phil Schiller replaced Jobs at Macworld 2009 he used seven of Jobs's techniques, which make a compact checklist:

1. **A Twitter-like headline** to set the theme up front: "Today is all about the Mac."
2. **A road map:** "I have three new things to tell you about today," on a slide reading "3 New Things," with verbal reminders along the way.
3. **Dressed-up numbers:** 3.4 million weekly Apple Store visitors is "one hundred Macworlds each and every week."
4. **Props and demos,** done seated at an onstage computer.
5. **A shared stage:** the engineer who built iMovie demoed it, and a video of three employees explained the eight-hour battery.
6. **Visual slides:** the first had no words, only photos of new stores, and feature lists used the fewest words possible paired with images.
7. **A holy shit moment** saved for last: "just one more thing," three iTunes changes, ending with DRM-free songs, the day's biggest headline.
