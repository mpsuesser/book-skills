# The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs

Carmine Gallo, a communications coach and former journalist, breaks down how Steve Jobs crafted and delivered his keynotes, and argues the techniques work for anyone: a CEO launching a product, an entrepreneur pitching investors, a salesperson closing a deal, an educator inspiring a class. Most professionals present to deliver information. A Jobs presentation creates an experience, "a reality distortion field," that leaves the audience "awed, inspired, and wildly excited." It informs, educates and entertains. It is a theatrical event with heroes, villains, a supporting cast and stunning backdrops, in which "each presentation tells a story, and every slide reveals a scene."

None of it is innate. "Jobs is not a natural. He works at it." Adopt even some of the techniques and a presentation stands out from the legions of mediocre ones. Use them at the book's breadth: products, services, companies, causes, initiatives and ideas, in any industry and in any presentation software. The software is not the main character; the speaker is. "Slides don't tell stories; you do."

Two convictions run under every scene:

- **What are you really selling?** Your widget doesn't inspire. Cisco's John Chambers sells human connections, not routers. Howard Schultz sells a "third place" between work and home, not coffee. Jobs sells tools to unleash human potential, not computers. Show how the widget improves the listener's life, entertain them while you do it, and you create an evangelist. "Sell dreams, not products."
- **Passion.** Be passionate about the topic and you are "80 percent closer" to Jobs's magnetism. No technique can make up for a lack of passion for your service, product, company or cause.

## The play in three acts

The book is built like a three-act play, the metaphor Jobs used when he split a launch into acts "like every classic story." Each scene below gives its technique and what its Director's Notes require. Depth, examples and numbers for each act are in the act files. The slide-by-slide script patterns distilled from Jobs's keynotes (what the slide shows beside what is said, where the emphasis and pauses fall) are in [script-and-slide-patterns.md](script-and-slide-patterns.md).

### Act 1: Create the Story

Most people fail to think through their story, and that separates mediocre communicators from extraordinary ones. Depth for Scenes 1 to 7 and Intermission 1: [act-1-create-the-story.md](act-1-create-the-story.md).

1. **Plan in Analog.** Storyboard the plot on paper or a whiteboard before opening the presentation software. Script first, then sketch, then produce. Cover the nine elements of great presentations: headline, passion statement, three key messages, metaphors and analogies, demonstrations, partners, customer evidence and third-party endorsements, video clips, and flip charts, props and show-and-tell. Shape the argument on Aristotle's five-point outline. Pass the **Napkin Test**: the idea is simple enough to sketch on a napkin.
2. **Answer the One Question That Matters Most.** Every listener is asking "Why should I care?" Answer it right out of the gate, then early, often and clearly. Name the one thing you want the listener to take away, repeat it at least twice, strip buzzwords and jargon, and keep it the same across every piece of collateral.
3. **Develop a Messianic Sense of Purpose.** Find what you are truly passionate about. Usually it is not the widget but how the widget improves customers' lives. Write a one-sentence **passion statement** and share it with gusto. "Great leaders rally people to a better future."
4. **Create Twitter-Like Headlines.** Write a one-sentence headline of 140 characters or less that is concise, specific and offers a personal benefit ("iPod. One thousand songs in your pocket."). Write it in the planning stage, build the presentation around it, deliver it before the details, repeat it after them, and use the same line in every channel.
5. **Draw a Road Map.** Give a verbal preview of coming attractions in groups of three: three acts, three features, three parts to a demo. List every key point, categorize until three remain, and hang stories, facts, examples, analogies and endorsements under each. "Three is the magic number."
6. **Introduce the Antagonist.** Establish the problem before revealing the solution. Ask "Why do we need this?", make the customer's pain tangible, and build the pain. Write an elevator pitch that answers four questions in one sentence each: What do you do? What problem do you solve? How are you different? Why should I care?
7. **Reveal the Conquering Hero.** Describe the state of the industry as it stands, then your vision of where it could be, then show in plain English how your product or idea is the cure. The hero's mission is not necessarily to slay the bad guy but to make our lives better.

**Intermission 1: Obey the Ten-Minute Rule.** "Your audience checks out after ten minutes." Change the stimulus at least every ten minutes with a video, a demo, another speaker or a story.

### Act 2: Deliver the Experience

Depth for Scenes 8 to 13 and Intermission 2: [act-2-deliver-the-experience.md](act-2-deliver-the-experience.md).

8. **Channel Their Inner Zen.** Slides are simple, visual and free of bullet points. One theme per slide, complemented by a photograph or image. Use white space and few words, and match the slide's words to the spoken words. Pictures beat text (the **picture superiority effect**). Simplify the spoken words the same way: plain English, sentences short enough for a Twitter post.
9. **Dress Up Your Numbers.** Data is meaningless without context. Choose few figures, make each **specific, relevant, and contextual**, and tie it to something the audience already knows, often through an analogy.
10. **Use "Amazingly Zippy" Words.** Simple, concrete, emotional language: no jargon, short sentences, descriptive adjectives you genuinely mean. Edit, edit, and edit some more.
11. **Share the Stage.** Bring in employees, partners, customers and media reviews: in person if possible, by video or quotes if not. Publicly thank employees, partners and customers, and do it often.
12. **Stage Your Presentation with Props.** Build a demo into the plan and make it short, simple, sweet, swift and substantial (Kawasaki). Commit to it and have fun with it. Give something to visual, auditory and kinesthetic learners alike.
13. **Reveal a "Holy Shit" Moment.** Plan, script and rehearse one emotionally charged moment that brings the one theme to life and leaves everyone talking. It can be a product reveal, a personal story, new and unexpected information, or a demo. Build up to it; the more unexpected, the better.

### Act 3: Refine and Rehearse

How you say something is as important as what you say, if not more so. Depth for Scenes 14 to 18 and the Encore: [act-3-refine-and-rehearse.md](act-3-refine-and-rehearse.md).

14. **Master Stage Presence.** Eye contact, open posture and frequent hand gestures. A voice varied in inflection, volume and rate, with well-placed pauses. Record yourself and watch.
15. **Make It Look Effortless.** Hours of grueling practice over many days. Know exactly what you will say, when you will say it and how you will say it. Review the video for eye contact, body language, filler words, vocal delivery and energy. Prepare for tough questions with the **bucket method**.
16. **Wear the Appropriate Costume.** Dress a little better than everyone else, but appropriate for the culture: like the leader you want to become.
17. **Toss the Script.** Talk to the audience, not the slides. Rehearse with the five-step method until the slides alone prompt you: one theme per slide. Use notes only for step-by-step passages such as demos, as three or four large-font points per card.
18. **Have Fun.** Treat the presentation as "infotainment." Never apologize. When something goes differently from the plan, acknowledge it, smile and move on. It didn't "go wrong" unless you let it derail the rest.

**Encore: One More Thing.** Close with a surprise sometimes, not always, because "a surprise would fail to surprise if everyone knows it's coming."

## Working a request

**Building a presentation, pitch or talk.** Walk the scenes in order and produce each artifact before moving to the next: the one thing and the answer to "Why should I care?"; a passion statement; the headline; three key messages, each with its supporting devices; the antagonist and the hero; a script in two columns (what is said, what the slide shows), one theme per slide, drafted with the patterns file; the numbers dressed up; a cut of jargon; a demo, guests or video placed at least every ten minutes; the holy shit moment, scripted; and a rehearsal plan with the expected question buckets. Done when every artifact exists and the script passes the review below.

**Reviewing or fixing a draft** (a deck, a script, a recording, a pitch email). Read it against each scene and name every failure in the book's terms. The common ones:

- No clear answer to "Why should I care?"; the benefit is buried behind the technology ("what that means to you" is never said).
- No headline, or one that is long, abstract, or about the company rather than the listener; different wording in each channel.
- Four, seven or twelve points where three would do; an agenda slide instead of a verbal road map.
- A solution without a problem (the pot of coffee with no cup to pour it into); no villain.
- **Slideuments**: bullets, sub-bullets, slides that repeat the speaker's sentences, forty-word slides, clashing fonts, every centimeter filled.
- Naked numbers with no context; too many of them.
- Jargon and buzzwords ("best-of-breed, leading-edge, agile solutions"); high lexical density; long sentences.
- A one-man show for ninety minutes; no demo, prop, guest or video; nothing that changes within ten minutes.
- A flat reveal that gives away the big news in the first sentence.
- Delivery: reading from notes ("Who's Mr. Note Card?"), hands in pockets or at the sides, behind a lectern, monotone, rushing, filler words, low energy, apologizing for glitches.

For each failure, give the fix from its scene. Done when every scene has been checked and every failure has a concrete rewrite: the new headline, the restructured three, the rebuilt slide column, the dressed-up number.

**A single piece** (a headline, an elevator pitch, a slide, a number, a demo, Q&A preparation, delivery coaching). Go straight to its scene in the act file, use that scene's criteria and examples, and check the result against its Director's Notes.
