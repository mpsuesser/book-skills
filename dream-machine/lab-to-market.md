# From the lab to the market

How Xerox invented personal distributed computing at PARC and let others commercialize it, read through the book's diagnoses. Each side blamed the other, and the book holds both accountable. It also covers the incumbents (IBM, AT&T, DEC, Intel) whose mental models kept them from seeing what was coming, and what actually moved new technology into use.

## Diagnose the parent organization

- **Numbers guys.** Xerox was taken over by IBM-style marketing and Ford-style finance: "numbers guys ... thought that nothing was real until they could reduce it to entries on a spreadsheet". To them everything "could be predicted, analyzed, controlled, and *managed*." Faced with Moore's law, one asked "Nothing could follow that kind of law!" Demanding quarterly results suits "a slightly better widget". Jack Goldman's analogy: cost-benefit analysis applied to polio would have produced "the best iron lungs you ever saw."
- **Analysis as obsession.** Goldman warned of what happens "when careful analysis went from being a tool to being an obsession": you are "so afraid of uncertainty and risk that you didn't even dare experiment." Stewart Brand's phrase for this was "subservience to Plan that amounts to panic", in which "we routinely reject the princely gifts of spontaneous generation."
- **Zero for the unquantifiable.** Xerox put its office-products operation in Dallas, far from PARC. The analysis behind that decision effectively assigned "a value of zero to unquantifiable factors such as propinquity, synergy, and the paradigm-shifting power of new technology." The result was rivalry instead of synergy, and Dallas built an obsolete electromechanical word processor. Ask what the user's analysis values at zero.
- **Missing the point.** Engineering chief Jim O'Neill rejected five laser printers for Livermore over a potential $150,000 loss, but "making a profit on these particular printers wasn't the *point*."
- **Warring camps.** The copier business (90 to 95 per cent of revenue) fought the electronics people, and the electronics people split between systems and stand-alone machines. When the heartland is threatened, the instinct is to pour resources into it. When Xerox brought in Steve Lukasik to sort out the electronics people, his brief was "Lead. Unify." In the end the failure was one "of imagination ... at the highest level of management. Xerox never got organized for this."
- **Distance from politics.** Pake ran corporate research from California, and learned that "the problem of being out here is that you don't know what the corporate politics are back east until it's too late."

## Diagnose the lab

- **Arrogance.** Pake: PARC "wanted to believe that they were annoying the rest of the corporation"; much of the oppression was "imagined and hoped for." He added: "if we could have kept that arrogant attitude under control and been more considerate of visitors, we could have avoided a lot of our troubles." When Bob Potter of the office-products division came to a Dealer meeting, the lab staged a firestorm "instead of listening to what he had to say", and "squandered any chance of influencing" the person who needed a product next year.
- **"They finally had a chance, and they blew it."** This is the PARC veterans' own verdict on the Star. Lampson: headquarters "had the vision for PARC in the beginning" and let the engineers shape the product. "They knew they didn't understand computers, but they knew they had a great research lab". Meanwhile "the wizards out on Coyote Hill Road understood computers but not marketing." Their "unfettered exuberance" produced **three critical errors**. Check every lab product for each:
  1. **Feature-itis.** "They loaded up the Star software with every neat thing they could think of", about a million lines of code at the ragged edge of the hardware. The Star was "painfully, maddeningly *sloooow*."
  2. **A closed system.** All hardware and software came from Xerox, so Xerox "had to guess right about what those customers wanted; it had no way to tap into the creativity of the wider community." It guessed wrong. There was no spreadsheet because no one in a research lab needed one, "precisely the inverse of priorities in the business world." Closure also removed any "inexpensive, low-risk entry path". A minimal installation cost at least $100,000, so for customers "it was far safer to wait and see."
  3. **Passing up the simpler thing.** "We could have shipped something much like the IBM PC, and a short time later shipped something very much like the original Alto." A 68000-based alternative was built and rejected, "because all the software would have had to be rewritten, and we didn't have the stomach for it"; it would have meant scaling "back too much."
- **Excess of vision.** Lampson: "We just didn't pay attention to a business model of how to do it ... the problem wasn't a shortage of vision at headquarters. If anything, it was an excess of vision at PARC."
- **Different levels of aspiration.** The PARC people dismissed microcomputers as toys. Kay: "It had never occurred to us that people would buy crap"; customers were "perfectly happy with the McDonald's hamburger approach." Larry Tesler went to the Homebrew Computer Club and saw it differently: "The world was full of bright people who didn't work for PARC", and they were learning fast. Price decided the outcome. Chuck Thacker on seeing the IBM PC: "I knew we'd lost because the PC's price was right."

## Diagnose the incumbent's mental model

The book's repeated finding is that "just because a step seems 'logical' in hindsight doesn't mean it automatically gets taken." The block is a mental model of the product and the customer. Name the user's version:
- **IBM** treated machine design as "a closed question of how to build machines. They did not recognize that it was an open question." It saw time-sharing as academic self-indulgence. It thought of MIT "as an *account* rather than as a coparticipant in a new kind of product", and took "the view that a system is a static thing which is only created once and never modified." Its commitment to System/360 made it "a locomotive speeding down the tracks ... They had too much at stake."
- **AT&T**: "Son, this is how a telephone works." It saw packet switching as a threat, and as late as 1989 or 1990 still told Wolff, "we just can't see us making any money." The diehards: "channelized thinkers ... could not get their minds into a new focus."
- **DEC's Ken Olsen** asked "Why would anyone want a computer on his desktop?" He meant a feeble micro compared with a time-sharing terminal, and he had a point. But treating personal computers as toys cost DEC the market, even though it had the culture to lead.
- **Intel** had a model of an "*industrial* customer" who embedded its chip in a controller. It was hardware people selling to hardware people, and nobody made the connection to a general-purpose computer.
- **Engineers who stand by their designs**: "assuming very rigid positions, that they are not emotionally free of abandoning".
- **Market surveys of the old users.** Surveying demand for time-sharing among batch users was "analogous to trying to establish the need for steam shovels by [conducting] market surveys among ditch diggers." Or: "Go away, little boy, there's no revenue there."
- **The new users' blind spot.** Steve Jobs's team missed the Alto's Ethernet. To them, networking meant giving up *your* autonomy.

## What moved technology into use

- **Let people experience it.** Xerox's Advanced Systems Division put Altos at customer sites as beta tests. It proved customers would pay, got feedback from non-technical users that greatly improved the system, and "by letting people directly experience what PARC had wrought" enlisted allies inside Xerox. At Boca Raton, the executives' wives (former secretaries) understood the editor immediately, while their husbands asked, "It does that?" Don Massaro stopped a demonstration after thirty minutes: "I'm already convinced." Once Roberts put the skeptical ARPA official Steve Lukasik on electronic mail, Lukasik made it the way to reach him, and every office he oversaw got on the net.
- **Really *show* them.** Taylor wanted Engelbart's 1968 demonstration to "really *show* them, not just talk about it". Kahn wanted the 1972 public Arpanet demonstration to "give researchers an incentive to get their applications up and running", and to be "so compelling that no one, not even the phone company, could ignore it." Kahn: "Up until that point you couldn't *see* it anywhere." Olsen's founding lesson: "It turns out that it takes more than ideas. You've got to *sell* your idea."
- **A different customer means a different product.** For consumers, the kit had to become an appliance: "a finished system that would work as soon as you plugged it in." The killer application sells the machine, as VisiCalc sold Apple IIs. The price has to be right.
- **The slaughter of the whitecoats.** Gatekeepers who control a central resource try to outlaw the cheap alternative and lose. Minicomputer users "stormed the bastions and slaughtered the whitecoats" of the computer centers, and later managers hid microcomputers in petty-cash budgets past their IT departments. The IT departments' grievance was legitimate (flaky, incompatible software). They ended by networking the machines they could not keep out.
- **Join the engineers.** Lick's answer to pressure for relevance was to make common cause with the engineers, giving research a route to customers and users (the Easter message in research-leadership.md).
- **Listen to the users.** Andreessen "haunted the Internet newsgroups incessantly, trying to figure out what people wanted in a browser and then incorporating new features practically overnight."
- **Ideas leave with people.** PARC's ideas reached the market through those who walked out to Apple, Microsoft, 3Com and Adobe, and through a lab that "kept its door open". Its vision reached the mass market "only in pieces. And slowly." Charles Simonyi on moving from Xerox to Microsoft: "It was like going into the graveyard or retirement home before going into the maternity ward."
- **Know when to concede.** Xerox's admission that it had lost was the decision to make its next workstation run IBM PC software and carry a VisiCalc clone.
- **Expect everyone to stumble.** When computation goes from scarce to abundant, "all anyone could do was try things out, see what worked and what didn't, and continue to explore the unknown."
