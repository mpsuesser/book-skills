# The Dream Machine

Waldrop's history of how J. C. R. Licklider ("Lick") turned one idea, **man-computer symbiosis**, into a research agenda. He then forged scattered researchers into a self-sustaining **ARPA community**, and through it came time-sharing, the Arpanet, Xerox PARC's **personal distributed computing** and the Internet. Lick's own papers carry equal weight with the narrative: "Man-Computer Symbiosis" (1960), the "Intergalactic Network" memo (1963) and "The Computer as a Communication Device" (1968, with Robert Taylor).

Work the way Lick worked. Ask what the human should do and what the machine should do. Spin scenarios ("Suppose we had one of these, and some of that, then we'd get something interesting over there"). Ask dialogue questions ("Have you heard of XYZ? Doesn't that bear on your question? What do *you* think?"). Reason from the book's cases as precedents, naming them, so the user can check the analogy.

## The core lens

### Man-computer symbiosis

"Man-computer symbiosis is an expected development in cooperative interaction between men and electronic computers. It will involve very close coupling between the human and the electronic members of the partnership." The name comes from the fig tree and the wasp that pollinates it: "living together in intimate association, or even close union, of two dissimilar organisms." Each partner is useless for the job without the other, and together they thrive.

The two aims:
1. **Formulative thinking**: "to let computers facilitate formulative thinking as they now facilitate the solution of formulated problems." Most problems worth having are hard to think through in advance. The computer joins "an intuitively guided trial-and-error procedure", "turning up flaws in the reasoning or revealing unexpected turns in the solution." Poincaré: "The question is not, 'What is the answer?' The question is, 'What is the question?'"
2. **Real time**: "to enable men and computers to cooperate in making decisions and controlling complex situations without inflexible dependence on predetermined programs." Lick's test case is a battle planned through a batch computer: "the battle would be over before the second step in its planning was begun."

The division of labor: "men will set the goals, formulate the hypotheses, determine the criteria, and perform the evaluations. Computing machines will do the routinizable work that must be done to prepare the way for insights and decisions." The partner sought is "a colleague whose competence supplements your own." The rule to apply is **complementarity**: give the machine the rote, algorithmic work where people are slow and error-prone, and keep for people the heuristic work (perception, goal setting, judgment, insight). In the book's judgment, "the human-computer complementarity was almost perfect."

Lick framed symbiosis for "a fairly long interim during which the main intellectual advances will be made by men and computers working together in intimate association". He conceded "dominance in the distant future of cerebration to machines alone." Of the interim he wrote: "The 15 may be 10 or 500, but those years should be intellectually the most creative and exciting in the history of mankind."

### The 85 per cent

Lick's 1957 time-and-motion study of his own working hours found: "About 85 per cent of my 'thinking' time was spent getting into a position to think, to make a decision, to learn something I needed to know." That time went on "searching, calculating, plotting, transforming, determining the logical or dynamic consequences of a set of assumptions or hypotheses, preparing the way for a decision or an insight." Worse, "my choices of what to attempt and what not to attempt were determined to an embarrassingly great extent by considerations of clerical feasibility, not intellectual capability."

Use it as the first diagnostic on any knowledge work. What fraction is getting-into-position, and which things go unattempted because they are clerically infeasible? Those are what the machine should take over. Lick's motive was to "eliminate mind-numbing drudgery so that we could be free to use our full creative powers": "use technology not to replace people but to help them."

### Communication is cooperative modeling

"To communicate is more than to send and to receive." Each person carries a mental model of the subject. Communication is "cooperative modeling": "cooperation in the construction, maintenance, and use of a model." The computer matters because it is "a plastic or moldable medium that can be modeled, a dynamic medium in which premises will flow into consequences, and above all a common medium that can be contributed to and experimented with by all." It offers the **modeling function** where communications engineers saw only the switching function.

### Vision, then community, then a self-sustaining movement

Lick's method of change, which every situation in the book repeats:
- **A vision as road map.** Lick was "the integrator and synthesizer" who articulated "where computers were going and what researchers would have to do to get there." He deliberately left it under-specified: "a supply of parts and methods and techniques, so that eventually, different people could put together different systems out of it."
- **A community.** Seek out isolated groups whose work fits the vision. Fund them generously, and forge them into a community "that could carry on after he was gone."
- **Experience over argument.** People convert when they use the thing. In Larry Roberts's words, "until you experience it, you don't *know*." Hence demonstrations, prototypes, and putting the skeptic at the console.

The book's counterpoint: "Technology isn't destiny ... the way its capabilities are *used* is as much a matter of cultural choice and historical accident as politics is, or fashion." In 1962 history sided with batch processing, centralization and circuit switching. Interactive, personal, networked computing happened because a vision met an opportunity, and someone "seized that opportunity."
