# Funding, leading and building a research community

How Licklider and his successors at ARPA's Information Processing Techniques Office (IPTO), and then Bob Taylor at Xerox PARC, produced an outsized share of modern computing, and where each of them failed. The book presents the tensions at the end of this file without resolving them. Present them the same way.

## The ARPA style

Jack Ruina's charter for ARPA: fund the highest-quality research whether or not the services think they need it, through program managers who know the science thoroughly. "Instead of being subjected to endless review and micromanagement, those managers had to be given autonomy, authority, and the freedom to act." The recipe: "Assault the technological frontiers everywhere you can ... Look for people with ideas that push the envelope. Give them development money. Be generous. Take risks. Cut through the red tape. Do whatever you have to. But do it." Decisions took days, not months. From above, Ruina gave Lick "benign neglect". ARPA's purpose under Taylor was to "invest in research that could change the status quo by an order of magnitude."

Corbató on the effect: NSF handed out money "with eye droppers—and then only after excruciating peer review." Lick gave umbrella grants "which allowed us to fund the whole program. So there was this tremendous pump priming, which freed us from having to think small." Speed counted too. Charlie Herzfeld gave Taylor $1 million for the network in twenty minutes, and Project MAC went from idea to approval in a week.

Two more rules of the office. The universities should build what industry will not. Lick in 1961: "It will not suffice to wait until the computer industry develops the [symbiotic] computer the university needs", and preparing the programs to make it "go critical" "is a task that universities can and should handle, for it is itself an intellectual enterprise of high order." And **rotate the managers**. Taylor left IPTO once its goal was on line: "I don't think it's good for someone to stay in that office for very long ... There's sort of a czar mentality that creeps in."

## Lick's community-building playbook

1. **Reframe the mission so it carries the vision.** Lick took over command and control and recast it: "the problems of command and control were essentially problems of man-computer interaction." He also told the brass: "I wasn't doing battle planning. I was doing the technical substrate that would one day support battle planning ... what the military needs, is what the businessman needs, is what the scientist needs." He kept the work open and unclassified.
2. **Seek out the isolated groups** whose work fits the vision, and back their leaders with money for whole teams. Lick started modestly: "I felt I could settle for it if I just had ten."
3. **Make them want it.** At SDC, Lick could force batch-processing work into time-sharing, and he admitted "this was cheating ... these people had every right to have their own vision." But he needed enthusiasm, not "sullen acquiescence." So he brought in the best time-sharing people for a show-and-tell, and SDC "capitulated totally."
4. **Work around rules that block the vision.** Lick was barred from soliciting proposals, so he asked groups, "Do you want one of these things, and what would you do with it if you had it?" The discussions turned into proposals.
5. **Turn natural rivals into a network.** Lick treated the service agencies as agents and friends, and held a monthly meeting of counterparts from NASA, NSF, NIH and AEC. Sutherland: "The group had no charter, no responsibilities, no budget, no purpose—but it was a great thing ... Precisely because the group had no charter, it was a wonderful way of getting information flow between the agencies."
6. **Bring the people together.** Principal-investigator meetings, where they stayed up late and drank a little. A summer study to turn a field into a community: "A whole community got together, became friends, exchanged ideas, went back home—and the whole movement went on." A separate meeting for the graduate students (Warnock's suggestion: "Why not have a meeting for the grad students the way you do for PIs?"). Kay remembered it as "the most civilized, the most ecumenical, the most incredibly supportive atmosphere ... People would cheer you on even if they didn't agree with you, just because they loved the fact that you were good." The graduate students then built the Arpanet's protocols themselves.
7. **Make the community self-sustaining.** Lick held the whole vision "wrapped up in *me*", so the risk was the man hit by a bus. The cure was a community that kept going without him. Funding university groups paid students and co-opted a rising generation, "winning the hearts and minds of a generation of young scientists." Lick's own summary: "It was a thing that organized itself into a community, so that there was some competition and some cooperation, and it resulted in the emergence of a field." Taylor's coda: before ARPA no U.S. university granted a computer-science Ph.D., and "a university graduate program requires a research base, and that in turn requires a long-term commitment of dollars."
8. **Choose the successor.** Lick: "I think maybe the best thing I did was to pick my successor." He picked Ivan Sutherland, aged 26, a true believer who was better at the work.

The test of the result is the book's: IPTO directors "were not dictators so much as protectors, intermediaries," exercising authority "with taste, tact, and restraint." As Kleinrock put it, "They wanted progress, not progress reports."

## How Lick judged and mentored people

- **Hire for brilliance.** At Lincoln he visited the ten best graduate schools for new Ph.D.s: "My bias was toward very bright people." At BBN: "Hire the most brilliant people you can find, and stand back."
- **Rigorous laissez-faire.** He expected very hard work and mastery of the tools, with contempt for laziness and sloppy thinking. But he "almost never told his students what to do in the lab, figuring that it was far better to let them make their own mistakes and find their own way." He challenged understanding with "problems or puzzles that would require insight into the theory to solve." The point was to have fun.
- **Mentor, not micromanager.** "As long as people made reasonable progress in the right direction, he would let them find their own way." He gave ideas away: "If you wrote a paper using an idea he'd come up with, he'd give it to you!"
- **Fund the right words.** On Engelbart, whom no one else would fund: "he's way out there ... But he's using the right words, so we're sort of honor-bound to fund him." Back people whose framing fits the vision even when the work is unproven. Taylor said the same of Engelbart: "might or might not be able to make any of his ideas work ... but he was definitely headed in the right direction."
- **Center of Excellence.** At Carnegie Tech the grant was open-ended: "All he wanted from them was excellence. Train some great students. Explore some big ideas. Break the mold." As Newell recalled: "If you guys are doing some excellent science, it [doesn't] matter what [it is]." Lick defined computer science broadly, as the study of "the phenomena surrounding computers."
- **Demand performance.** He was "impatient as ever with dithering, sloppiness, or mediocre quality" and "pulled the plug" on projects after their first year. When SRI management pushed Engelbart toward safe work: "Either you put Doug back in and let him do what he wants, or we're through."
- **Intellectual honesty.** When a team proved his doubt wrong, he said so ("You were right ... It works."), and earned their loyalty.
- **Learn by letting people try.** He bought the undergraduates a bare disk drive and let them install it, because "the money was better spent in training us to use the equipment, and to gain confidence, than in bringing in an outside expert." He told a lonely new director, "Mike, you're doing a great job."

## Where Lick failed

- **Administration.** "At ARPA ... Lick had succeeded because he never *had* to be a manager". He "again and again took jobs that required him to be an administrator—and then was almost contemptuous of the skills it took to do those jobs right. It was perhaps his greatest failing." As Project MAC's director he ran his own research group, and Fano's verdict was that "Lick didn't understand what a director should *not* do". A director allocates resources, so heading a group is a conflict of interest. A leader who is a visionary needs a deputy who handles the paperwork. On Lick's second ARPA tour, Col. David Russell did that while Lick set "tone and direction."
- **Transitions.** When Larry Roberts left IPTO there was no machinery for transition and no groomed deputy.

## Creating the environment

- **Fano's party.** An explosive intellectual environment "had to have an indefinable spark, an unpredictable, near-magical mix of just the right time, just the right place, just the right people, and just the right challenge ... No one had a surefire formula." Someone still has to step forward: "OK, if nobody else is going to do it, *I* am going to do it." They also have to do the homework, so that when asked "*Where* are you going to do this?" they have the answer.
- **Lick's checklist for a big proposal** (Project MAC): the right research mix, good people, a summer study to shape the field, help from the sponsor, and time off to think about how the program would go. Lick also asked to hear that "Computers are as much for communication as they are for calculation."
- **Jack Goldman's prescription** (founding PARC): "hire the smartest people you can find and give them their head—but let them know who's paying the bills." Smart individuals were not enough: "You had to build a community, a culture, an environment of innovation. You had to give your people the kind of challenge that would light a fire in their eyes ... You had to provide them with lavish resources ... keep the bottom-line guys at bay so *your* guys could have the freedom to explore and make mistakes ... But in ten years you might just change the world." The lab is not "the corporate fire department".
- **Location.** Top people will not cut themselves off from their profession, so put the lab near a strong university and cutting-edge firms. Design the building for "chance encounters, impromptu bull sessions, and the kind of serendipitous exchange of ideas that so often strikes sparks."
- **The freedom to make mistakes** (Whirlwind and SAGE). Mistakes were "admitted and fixed rather than evaded or denied," with little finger-pointing. Managers decided without being second-guessed: "As long as [our decisions] were plausible and could be explained, we could carry other people with us." Herbert Simon's corollary: "trying to avoid every conceivable error is a recipe for paralysis: having the freedom to make mistakes is necessary for achieving any progress at all."

## Taylor's PARC method

- **"Never hire 'good' people, because ten good people together can't do what a single great one can do."** Taylor hired whole teams of great people, the BCC group among them.
- **Hiring by consensus.** A candidate talked, the lab discussed them, and everybody had to vote yes, the boss included. Kay: "if you made it through ... You were a colleague from the word 'Go.' So the arguments could go deeper without hurt feelings."
- **An integrated vision, not a topic.** The goal was "a *system* of information technology, a whole new way for human beings to work together." Taylor refused to start a graphics group: "To focus entirely on graphics would be like trying to build the Arpanet by focusing entirely on the technology of telephone lines."
- **Shape the context and let the system excite itself.** Lampson said Taylor "subtly, but profoundly, shaped the whole context of their work". He seeded PARC with people from the ARPA culture, kept exposing them to provocative ideas (pressing Licklider's papers on them), then let them "function as a kind of self-exciting system." Stuart Card described the resonance this produced. The line from Bush to Licklider to Engelbart to Kay was "like the Holy Grail ... whenever we could phrase an idea so that it fell on this path ... suddenly everybody's eyes would light up, and you'd hit this resonance frequency."
- **Manage personalities, not technical decisions.** Kay: "Bob didn't want people who had to be managed ... [he] did not meddle with people's technical decisions ... there ... to manage the personalities, to keep people from killing each other." When the team rejected his view (on building the MAXC computer), he said "if you think this is the right thing to do, I'll support it. But consider the alternative," and bided his time.
- **The fundamental dilemma.** "Get all these maverick geniuses moving in the same direction, without forcing everyone to move in lockstep ... sense of purpose and group cohesion, without crushing spontaneity and individual initiative." Set things up so people "would freely follow their own instincts—and end up organizing *themselves.*" The book calls this "*the* fundamental dilemma of modern management" and the fundamental political challenge of democracy. Taylor learned what was happening by talking to the youngest people in each group.
- **Dealer Meetings.** A weekly mandatory meeting where one person talks for about an hour and the rest "have at him." The speaker sets the rules, like the dealer in a card game.
- **Class one / class two disagreement.** In a class one disagreement people just yell at each other. In class two, "each side could explain the other side's position to the other side's satisfaction. You don't have to *believe* the other guy ... You just have to give a fair account of what he's saying." Push every dispute to class two. It is effective at "clarifying unspoken assumptions and ferreting out facts that one person knew and another didn't ... you all know the same set of things, and you end up concluding the same thing."
- **Kay's Learning Research Group.** He named it "to be as vague as possible about its charter." He hired "only ... people who got stars in their eyes when they heard about the notebook computer idea." When anyone asked what to do and he had no strong idea: "I would point to the notebook model and say, 'Advance that.'" This is a **clear romantic vision** used as the group's compass.

## Tensions the book holds open

- **Shared vision vs freedom.** George Pake saw Taylor's computer-science lab as "a cult" with a siege mentality, and feared his vision had become "a stultifying orthodoxy, crushing any creative impulse that didn't fit in with it." Pake: "creativity is a function of freedom"; "I believed in diversity. I didn't think we should have one point of view." Kahn called Taylor black-and-white: support "all the way" for those going his direction, the "flame-thrower" for those against. The book credits Taylor's vision for PARC's achievement, and records that it ended in his forced exit and a devastated lab.
- **Directed vs exploratory funding.** George Heilmeier's **catechism** asked of every proposal: what is the research trying to accomplish, why do the researchers think they can succeed, and what would the military get out of it? Lick: "You have no *right* to do that!" Heilmeier: "Lick, I have a *responsibility* to do that." Lick's creed: "give good people the money and they go off and do good things." Kahn judged them both right. Directed management fit engineering jobs like packet radio and the Internet. Exploratory work like AI needed the Lewis and Clark model, since "this was just not an engineering job." Kahn also credited Heilmeier's "wire brushing" with doing good, while "relevance" nearly destroyed what made ARPA special. Fit the management style to the kind of work.
- **Lick's resolution, the Easter message.** Lick chose to "stay and join" rather than "leave and fight." His proposal: let's grow up, quit treating applications as at odds with pure research, and make common cause with the engineers. The new way "will not, over a long period, be a zero-sum game", because "it takes advantage of every demonstrated success to increase both the basic research and application budget." Applications "give the university research groups an engineering arm, a marketplace, customers, users ... more feedback from real tests of the new ideas and because every star needs an audience and performs best before a big and enthusiastic one." Lick and his colleagues meant to give Heilmeier enough of what he wanted to get most of what they wanted.
- **Abundance vs scarcity.** Cerf: "In the early days, ARPA could build a community because ARPA could afford to support everybody. But when there is scarcity, you don't have community; all you have is survival." Community-building on Lick's model presumes enough money to include everyone.
- **Whose money.** The Vietnam-era question "Is it right to take this money?" got an answer from the community: IPTO was its peer and extension. Metcalfe, looking back: "That was the lie we told ourselves."
