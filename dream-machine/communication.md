# Communication as cooperative modeling

Licklider and Taylor's theory of communication ("The Computer as a Communication Device", 1968), applied to meetings, collaboration, shared knowledge and on-line communities. It opens with the claim: "In a few years, men will be able to communicate more effectively through a machine than face to face."

## The model of communication

- **More than transfer.** "A communications engineer thinks of communicating as transferring information from one point to another". But two tape recorders playing to each other do not communicate: "communicators have to do something nontrivial with the information they send and receive." The aim is "the jointly constructive, the mutually reinforcing aspect of communication—the part that transcends 'now we both know a fact that only one of us knew before.' When minds interact, new ideas emerge."
- **Modeling is central.** "Any communication between people about the same thing is a common revelatory experience about informational models of that thing." If the two people's concepts "are very different", then "there is no common model and no communication."
- **The mental model's strengths and shortcomings.** "In richness, plasticity, facility, and economy, the mental model has no peer, but, in other respects, it has shortcomings. It will not stand still for careful study. It cannot be made to repeat a run. No one knows just how it works. It serves its owner's hopes more faithfully than it serves reason. It has access only to the information stored in one man's head. It can be observed and manipulated only by one person."
- **Cooperative modeling.** "Society rightly distrusts the modeling done by a single mind." That is why communication must be cooperative modeling (defined in SKILL.md). The check is: "How can we be sure that we are modeling cooperatively, that we are communicating, unless we can compare models?"

## Diagnostics for a group that is failing to communicate

1. **Is there an externalized model?** "When communicators have no such common framework, they merely make speeches at each other; but when they have a manipulable model before them, they utter a few words, point, sketch, nod, or object." Even "a flow diagram or an outline" changes the conversation. Lick and Taylor's hypothesis about remote communication: "Perhaps the reason present-day two-way telecommunication falls so far short of face-to-face communication is simply that it fails to provide facilities for externalizing models." The fix is a shared, manipulable model in view of everyone, local or remote.
2. **What kind of convergence is needed?** If two models are "similar in structure but different simply in the values of certain parameters", communication brings "convergence toward a common pattern". This is "the easiest and most frequent kind." If the models are "structurally dissimilar", success shows as "structural changes in one of the models or in both of them". It takes insights, new hypotheses, or both sides restructuring. Diagnose which kind the group faces. Arguing about values when the structures differ goes nowhere.
3. **Is each person's data getting into the others' models?** Participants bring data "in undigested and uncorrelated form," shaped "by insight, subjective feelings, and educated guesses." "Getting his colleagues to incorporate his data into their models is the essence of the communications task."
4. **Is this creative informational activity or informational housekeeping?** Housekeeping means payroll, balances, orbits and repetitive control. Creative activity means making "coherent pictures of not well understood situations." Tools built for housekeeping fail at creative work. When many minds interact well, "the interplay may produce, not just a solution to a problem, but a new set of rules for solving problems. That, of course, is the essence of creative interaction."
5. **Did the group simplify too early?** "It is frightening to realize how early and drastically one does simplify, how prematurely one does conclude, even when the stakes are high". "Deep modeling to communicate—to understand—requires a huge investment." Against an uncooperative opponent, decision makers in crises explored only a few alternatives a few moves deep, because "modeling takes time, and the pressure of events forces simplification even when it is dangerous." The remedy in both cases is the ability "to model faster and to greater depth."
6. **Is the dialogue in short spurts?** "Interactive communication consists of short spurts of dialog ... filibustering destroys communication."
7. **Does the system arouse interests?** "A communication system should make a positive contribution to the discovery and arousal of interests."

## The project meeting held through a computer

Engelbart's progress-review meeting is the worked example. Its subject was so broad that "no one of the attendees, not even the host, could know all the information pertaining to this particular project." It accomplished in two days "what normally might have taken a week." Its mechanics are a pattern to copy:
- Shared screens, with pointers that any participant could move for all to see.
- Each speaker's topical outline on the screens as they talked, "providing a broad view of his own model."
- Outline items naming reference files that could be called up in detail. This worked because "from the beginning of the project, its participants had put their work into the computer system's files."
- Detail on demand "when facts had to be pinpointed", and global views "to answer questions of relevance and interrelationship."
- The next step: each participant can "thumb through the speaker's files as the speaker talks" and "check out incidental questions without interrupting the presentation for substantiation."

## The modeling function, not the switching function

Communications engineers wanted computers for **switching** (connecting lines, or "store and forward" of messages). Lick and Taylor stressed the **modeling function**: "an interactive, cooperative modeling facility." Transmission and processing had been "separately institutionalized", and melding them brings "strong intellectual and social benefits" against "powerful legal and administrative obstacles." Their caveat about the technology: "Emphatically we do not say: 'Buy a computer and your communication problems will be solved.'"

## Critical mass and distributed intellectual resources

For any hard problem, "you find only a few people who can contribute effectively to its solution." The dilemma:
- Bring them together "physically in one place to form a team, and you have trouble, for the most creative people are often not the best team players, and there are not enough top positions in a single organization to keep them all happy."
- Let them go their separate ways, and "each creates his own empire, large or small, and devotes more time to the role of emperor than to the role of problem solver." Between meetings "the correlations among mental models degenerate ... so that it may take a year to do a week's communicating."
- The resolution is "some way of facilitating communication among people without bringing them together in one place."

What to share is more than data. Programs "transcend mere 'data'—they include procedures and processes for structuring and manipulating data". The whole includes "raw data, digested data, data about the location of data—and documents—and most especially models." Without a network, reusing another site's program costs about as much as writing it afresh.

The half-dozen early on-line communities were "socio-technical pioneers" for four reasons, which serve as a checklist for seeding any community:
1. Some members understand both man-computer interaction and the technology.
2. Others are creative people in other fields who sense the impact on their own work.
3. The community has adequate machines and has learned to use them.
4. "Their efforts are regenerative": tool-building and substantive use feed each other, producing "large and growing resources of programs, data, and know-how."

These systems differ from look-alikes "by having a greater degree of open-endedness, by rendering more services, and above all by providing facilities that foster a working sense of community among their users."

## On-line interactive communities

- **The surprise use wins.** E-mail began as an "ad hoc add-on by BBN". Kleinrock: "E-mail was the biggest surprise about the ARPA network ... it just blossomed. And that sucked a lot of people in." A network the community had accepted grudgingly became wildly popular. Watch for the use users invent, and build it out, as Larry Roberts did when Lukasik's mail piled up: he wrote the rd mail reader, with folders and selective retrieval, overnight.
- They will be "communities not of common location, but of common interest", together forming "a labile network of networks—ever-changing in both content and configuration."
- Instead of letters, "you will simply identify the people whose files should be linked to yours and the parts to which they should be linked—and perhaps specify a coefficient of urgency."
- The **OLIVER** (after Oliver Selfridge) is an "on-line interactive vicarious expediter and responder". It is a complex of programs and data in the network that "acts on behalf of its principal, taking care of many minor matters that do not require his personal attention and buffering him from the demanding world." It knows "who your friends are", "your value structure, who is prestigious in your eyes, for whom you will do what with what priority, and who can have access to which of your personal files", along with organizational and security rules. Part of it is shared with other people's OLIVERs, part is custom, and part is learned in service. "Secretaries will have OLIVERS."
- Services come in two groups: those subscribed to regularly, and those called for when needed, "most important—communication, display, and modeling programs."
- **Benefits for the individual.** (1) "Life will be happier for the on-line individual because the people with whom one interacts most strongly will be selected more by commonality of interests and goals than by accidents of proximity." (2) Communication becomes more effective and so more enjoyable. (3) Programs and models will be "(a) highly responsive, (b) supplementary to one's own capabilities, rather than competitive, and (c) capable of representing progressively more complex ideas without necessarily displaying all the levels of their structure at the same time." (4) Everyone can "find his calling."
- **The question for society** is: "Will 'to be on line' be a privilege or a right?" If only a favored segment gets "intelligence amplification", the network "may exaggerate the discontinuity in the spectrum of intellectual opportunity."

## Who can afford it? The cost argument

Lick and Taylor's method for judging whether a communication system is economically feasible:
1. Estimate the hourly cost per communicator by component. Their figures were "$1 for a console, $5 for one man's share of the services of a processor, 70 cents for storage, $3 for transmission ... and $1 for software support", or just under $11. Adjusted for realistic console use, the figure was $16 per hour.
2. Compare it with the alternatives. It came out below a long-distance call. A day trip from Boston to Washington cost $128 for eight working hours, which is also $16 per hour.
3. Apply the effectiveness test: "If computer-aided communication doubled the effectiveness of a man paid $16 per hour then ... it would be worth what it cost."
4. Project along the cost curves. Processing and storage costs halve every two years or less, so "computer facilitation will be affordable before many people are ready to take advantage of it."
5. Find the components that are not falling. For consoles the problem was "the chicken and the egg—in the factory and in the market." Transmission suffered from too little competition and threatened to become "the dominant cost."
