# Supercommunicators

Duhigg's thesis: "the most important goal of any conversation is to *connect*," and connecting is "a set of skills... There's nothing magical about it." When people click, their brains become *neurally entrained*: pulses, emotions and neural activity synchronize, and the "extent of speaker-listener neural coupling predicts the success of the communication."

**Supercommunicators** (researchers' term: *high centrality participants*) are the people whose presence synchronizes a group. In Sievers's experiment they spoke less than dominant leaders, asked ten to twenty times as many questions, repeated others' ideas, admitted confusion, laughed at others' jokes, and "were constantly adjusting *how* they communicated, in order to match their companions." Groups run by a dominant leader had the *least* synchrony. The supercommunicator's opinion usually became the consensus, and almost no one noticed their influence.

## The three kinds of conversation

Many discussions are three different conversations, and "Miscommunication occurs when people are having different *kinds* of conversations": different cognitive languages.

| Conversation | Mindset | What it's about | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***What's This Really About?*** | *decision-making mindset* | practical matters, plans, choices; what we'll discuss and how we'll decide together | "What are we going to do about Sam's grades?" "Can we talk about the upcoming meeting?" |
| ***How Do We Feel?*** | *emotional mindset* | feelings, beliefs, memories; a friend complaining about their boss who wants empathy, not advice | a sigh; "There's some stuff going on I might need to deal with" |
| ***Who Are We?*** | *social mindset* | relationships, how we see ourselves and are seen, social identities; gossip, people in common, religion or family background | "You went to Berkeley? Do you know Troy?" "As a Black woman, I see this differently than you" |

The three intertwine and shift mid-dialogue: a friend asks for help with a work problem (practical), admits being stressed (emotional), then worries how others will react (social). Seventy percent of conversations are social in nature.

The canonical mismatch: "Jim is driving me crazy!" / "What if you just invited him to lunch?" / "I'm not asking you to solve this! I just want some empathy." Duhigg did the same to a colleague who said "We need to do a better job of respecting each other": he answered a request for empathy with org charts.

## The matching principle

"Effective communication requires recognizing what *kind* of conversation is occurring, and then *matching* each other." If someone is emotional, allow yourself to become emotional; if they are intent on decision making, match that focus; if they are preoccupied by social implications, reflect that back.

**Matching isn't mimicry.** Mirroring gestures, moods or words "doesn't forge a real connection," and giving in to someone's preoccupations doesn't either: both produce "dueling monologues." Matching is "understanding someone's *mindset*—what kind of logic they find persuasive, what tone and approach makes sense to them—and then speaking their language," and then explaining how *you* are thinking so they can match you in return. What must show is the *desire* to align. Gottman: happy couples "communicate agreement not with the speaker's point of view or content, but with the speaker's affect."

The CIA case officer Jim Lawler nearly lost a recruit by pitching her, then won her over by matching her disappointment with his own: "if you listen for someone's truth, and you put your truth next to it, you might reach them." "When we match someone's mindset, a permission is granted": to see the world through their eyes, and to be heard in return.

## The learning conversation

The aim for meaningful discussions is a *learning conversation*: "learn how the people around us see the world and help them understand our perspectives in turn." It runs on four rules:

1. **Pay attention to what *kind* of conversation is occurring.** (The matching principle.)
2. **Share your goals, and ask what others are seeking.** Depth: [`what-is-this-really-about.md`](what-is-this-really-about.md).
3. **Ask about others' feelings, and share your own.** This brings emotions to the surface. Depth: [`how-do-we-feel.md`](how-do-we-feel.md); when the parties are fighting or online, [`connecting-amid-conflict.md`](connecting-amid-conflict.md).
4. **Explore if identities are important to this discussion.** Depth: [`who-are-we.md`](who-are-we.md), which also holds the before / beginning / as-it-unfolds plan for any hard conversation that can threaten someone's sense of self (performance feedback, a pay complaint, asking a spouse to change).

## Applying the book to a conversation

Use these steps whether the user is preparing, is in the middle of one, or is debriefing one that went wrong.

1. **Ask "Why am I opening my mouth?"** Have the user write one sentence naming both an aim and a mood, the way investment bankers who did this before every meeting cut their arguments significantly: "air our complaints" (aim) + "hear each other out" (mindset). Example: "My goal is to ask Maria if she wants to vacation together, but in a way that makes it easy for her to say no." Done when the user can state the aim, the mood they want, and a guess at what the other person wants.
2. **Diagnose which conversation each party is in right now.** Read the clues:
   - Plans, decisions, options, finances, facts laid out analytically → *What's This Really About?*, persuaded by the *logic of costs and benefits*.
   - A story or a joke, a personal problem told as a story, values, sighs → *How Do We Feel?*, persuaded by the *logic of similarities*: they want compassion, not a solution.
   - Keeps bringing up other people, groups, affiliations, status, "people like us" → *Who Are We?*
   - Notice whether people have said their goal for this conversation, and whether you have. When unsure, ask others: "What do you want to talk about?" "Do you want to talk about our emotions? Or do we need to make a decision together? Or is this about something else?" "Do you want me to suggest some solutions, or do you just need to vent?" Teachers ask upset students "Do you want to be helped, hugged, or heard?": *helped* is a practical *What's This Really About?* conversation, *hugged* an emotional *How Do We Feel?* conversation, *heard* a more social *Who Are We?* conversation.

   Done when you can name the conversation each party is in and which logic each finds persuasive.
3. **Match, then invite matching.** Speak in the mindset and logic the other person is using, and make your own mindset visible. In a debrief, name the mismatch: that is usually why it failed.
4. **Apply the toolkit for the conversation in play**: open the file listed for its rule above. Done when the user has the specific questions, moves or plan that conversation calls for, worded for their situation.
5. **Re-diagnose when the conversation shifts.** A practical talk turns emotional the moment someone discloses something. Answer the new conversation, not the old one.

## Diagnosing a conversation that went wrong

| Symptom | What happened | Where the fix is |
|---|---|---|
| "You're not listening" after you offered fixes | practical reply to an emotional conversation | matching principle, above |
| More data made them dig in | costs-and-benefits logic aimed at someone in the logic of similarities, or their identity was threatened | `what-is-this-really-about.md`, `who-are-we.md` |
| Talked past each other about the wrong topic | assumed you knew what they wanted; skipped the *quiet negotiation* | `what-is-this-really-about.md` |
| Polite but flat; nobody opened up | shallow, fact questions; disclosures glided past | `how-do-we-feel.md` |
| Your laugh or sympathy felt patronizing | matched the surface but not their mood and energy | `how-do-we-feel.md` |
| The same fight keeps returning | the emotional conflict under the surface one was never discussed; someone tried to control the other person | `connecting-amid-conflict.md` |
| Sudden defensiveness or anger | *identity threat*: someone was lumped into a group, or denied one | `who-are-we.md` |

What matters most is *wanting* to connect, and showing it. The effectiveness of particular tactics "waxes and wanes based on our surroundings, the types of discussion we're having, the kind of relationships we hope to achieve."
