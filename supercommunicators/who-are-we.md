# The *Who Are We?* conversation

***Social identities*** are "that part of our self-concept that comes from our membership in social groups, the value we place on this membership, and what it means to us emotionally": how we see ourselves, and believe others see us, as members of tribes (family, schools, workplaces, faith, politics, race, gender, teams). Everyone also has a personal identity apart from society. Identities intersect ("I'm a gay Hindu computer engineer from the South who votes libertarian"). They push us to see our *in-group* as more virtuous and intelligent and the *out-group* as suspicious and threatening, and they make us "exaggerate the differences between groups."

*Who Are We?* is happening whenever we gossip ("I hear everyone in accounting is going to get laid off"), signal an affiliation ("We're Knicks fans in this family"), map social links ("You went to Berkeley? Do you know Troy?"), or name a difference ("As a Black woman, I see this differently than you"). Discovering a shared identity builds trust. Identities are more or less ***salient*** depending on the setting: a pro-Obama T-shirt means nothing at a neighborhood barbecue where everyone voted for Obama, and makes instant kin of two wearers at an NRA rally.

## When identity is why facts fail

Vaccine refusal unites organic-food liberals, anti-tyranny conservatives and microchip-fearing libertarians, who are "reading from the same hymnal." For many, being anti-vaccine is a social identity that pays "increased self-esteem and a sense of community," and casts medical experts as a threatening out-group. The pediatrician Jay Rosenbloom's handouts and studies backfired: "The more information I provided, the more they'd dig in their heels." You can't change someone's behavior "if, as a prerequisite, you force them to say: Everything I've believed until now is wrong."

The expert's identity is part of the problem. A senior physician advised "just tell them: *I'm the doctor and I know better than you*." "You put on this white coat, and you start to think of yourself as the team with all the answers." Latching onto a single identity (*I am your parent*, *I am the teacher*, *I am the boss*) hobbles us, because we start seeing everything through that one lens. A *Who Are We?* conversation needs two things: address the stereotypes in your own head, and make the other person feel respected and part of a common tribe.

## We all contain multitudes

***Stereotype threat*** (Claude Steele): simply knowing a negative stereotype about your group exists can undermine your performance, even when no one around you is prejudiced. Strong women math students second-guessed themselves on math tests, and equally prepared Black students scored lower on the verbal GRE. The gap closed when the test was framed as not measuring ability, which made the stereotype less salient. In Dana Gresky's version, women primed with the stereotype who drew a detailed self-concept map with *many* nodes (clubs, hobbies, roles) performed as well as men; a sparse map didn't help. "We can make the bad voices in our head less powerful by remembering all the other voices in there, too."

Apply it to yourself as well as to them. Rosenbloom began spending a few minutes with every family finding an identity they shared (family, neighborhood): "It reminded me I was more than a doctor." When a parent called vaccines a plot, he felt a link instead of annoyance, "because I know what it feels like to get pushed around by experts."

The process: **invite** people to talk about their backgrounds, allegiances, and how their communities shaped them ("Where are you from? Oh, really? What was it like growing up there?"); **reciprocate** with how you see yourself ("You know, as a southerner, I think that…"); **evoke their many identities** as the talk unfolds ("I hear you saying that, as a lawyer, you support the police, but as a parent, do you worry about cops pulling over your kid?").

## Building a shared identity: the Qaraqosh soccer league

In Qaraqosh, Iraq, after ISIS, Christians felt betrayed by their Muslim neighbors. Salma Mousa tested the *contact hypothesis*, which holds that bringing groups with clashing identities together under specific conditions can overcome old hatreds. She added three Muslim players to half the teams in a Christian league, with equal playing time for everyone. Teams switched to Arabic, Christians paid for taxis so their Muslim teammates could get through checkpoints, and a Muslim was voted player of the year. Six months later, players on mixed teams were 49 percentage points more likely to train with Muslims. "It's how we structured everything that made the difference." Three design choices carry over to any *Who Are We?* conversation:

1. **Draw out multiple identities.** Players became goalkeeper, captain, the one who brings the drinks: identities "related to winning" that outranked religion. In conversation, remind everyone that none of us is one-dimensional; this disrupts the stereotypes in our heads.
2. **Put everyone on equal footing.** On the field there were no power differentials. In conversation: offer no unsolicited advice and don't trumpet wealth or connections; pick topics where everyone has some experience, or everyone is a novice; encourage the quiet to speak and the talkative to listen.
3. **Look for social similarities that already exist, and make them salient.** Mousa didn't ask anyone to redefine themselves; she made an identity they already carried, *teammates*, louder. A link becomes powerful when it is rooted in something meaningful. Both knowing Jim matters only once you talk about what his friendship means to each of you. Being Lakers fans matters when you share what it felt like to go to games with your parents and watch Magic score.

## Motivational interviewing

In *motivational interviewing*, "counselors rarely attempt to convince or persuade. Instead, the counselor subtly guides the client to think about and verbally express their own reasons for and against change." An elderly, devout retired police officer told Dr. Rima Chamie that God would provide, so he didn't need a COVID shot. She skipped the data: "It's wonderful your faith gives you so much strength." Later, as an aside: "I imagine your grandchildren's health is probably very important to you." At the end: "I'm so thankful that God gave us these brains, and these laboratories, and the ability to make vaccines. Maybe He gave us vaccines to keep us safe?" Thirty minutes later he asked for the vaccine. Another of her moves: "On a scale of one to ten, how do you feel about this vaccine?" If they say three: "Why not two? Why not four?" The new in-group is *we are all people who want to do the right thing for our families*. Rosenbloom won over a family whose previous doctor had dismissed them: he asked about their neighborhood, schools and weekends, swapped favorite parks, asked about their vaccine fears and their other worries, and never pushed. "It worked because they felt listened to."

## Identity threat

***Identity threat*** is the one behavior that consistently made people uncomfortable and upset in hard conversations: lumping a listener into a group they reject ("You're rich, so you know most rich people are snobs"), or denying them one they value ("You didn't go to law school, so you don't understand how the law actually works"). It is often indirect ("You're one of the good Republicans, but most of them only care about themselves") or unintended ("Since you don't have kids, you might not understand how a parent feels"). It triggers stress hormones and the urge to flee or fight back. People averaged 11.38 identity threats in the past week; only 1 percent had none. Advantage is no protection.

Identity threats come *from generalizing* ("Lawyers are all dishonest," "Everyone who voted for that guy is a racist"). **Speak instead about your own experiences and emotions, and listen for the other person's specific pain.** When someone describes something painful, acknowledge and validate it. Three temptations make it worse: minimizing their struggle, trying to solve it because their discomfort is hard to watch, and implying that because you haven't suffered it, it isn't real. A Black student told his white friend how a shop owner followed him around a store. The friend didn't question the details or offer fixes: "Anyone in our friend group seems shadier than you." / "I appreciate that." Support sounds like "That must have hurt," "I'm sorry that happened to you."

## Hard conversations: the fourth rule in practice

Many hard conversations threaten someone's sense of self even when they have nothing to do with race or ancestry. Blunt performance feedback can sound like an attack on someone's intelligence, a raise request like calling the boss uncaring, asking a spouse to change like an attack on who they are, concern about an uncle's drinking like a judgment of how he lives. Plan for three periods.

***Before*** (*ask yourself*). Thinking just a few minutes ahead measurably helps: Black-and-white pairs of close friends who first acknowledged the talk might be awkward and wrote down its benefits, its obstacles and how to overcome them went deeper than friends who didn't prepare, one pair of whom fled after three minutes.
- **What do you hope to accomplish?** What do you most want to say and learn, and what do *others* hope to say and learn?
- **How will this conversation start?** How will everyone get a voice? Who speaks first? "Studies suggest the person with the least power should begin."
- **What obstacles might emerge?** Anger, withdrawal, hesitancy to say the controversial thing. How can you make it safer?
- **When those obstacles appear, what's the plan?** Anticipating what makes you anxious lowers its impact. How will you calm yourself and others, or draw out someone who has gone quiet?
- **What are the benefits, and are they worth the risks?** (Usually yes.) How will you remind everyone why this matters when it gets hard?

***At the beginning:***
- **Establish guidelines.** No one is allowed to **blame, shame, or attack others**. The goal is to **share our feelings, not litigate who is at fault**. Define whether **asking questions is okay** (in good faith) and which very personal inquiries need forethought ("check your motivations"). People may decline to answer. **Everyone is encouraged to speak**, perhaps with a moderator. **Speak about your own experiences and describe your own stories.** Don't generalize, and don't solve or diminish others' problems unless they ask for help. When someone describes something painful, **listen**, say you're sorry it happened, and acknowledge what they felt.
- **Draw out everyone's goals.** Share yours, then ask theirs: *emotional goals* ("I want to make sure we stay friends," "I need to get something off my chest"), *practical goals* ("I'd like to walk away from this with a plan"), *group goals* ("It's important to me that we all show compassion for each other").
- **Acknowledge, and keep acknowledging, that discomfort is natural—and useful.** People will misspeak and ask naïve questions; treat those moments as chances to learn. The aim is not to "neutralize the discomfort, but rather give people a framework for persevering through it."

***As the discussion unfolds:*** draw out multiple identities and share your own; keep everyone on equal footing ("We're all experts in what we've seen and felt"); acknowledge people's experiences and look for *genuine* similarities, then push them further ("You went to Valley High? So did I!" → "High school was tough for me. What was it like for you?"); **manage your environment** (move from a group to a one-on-one, talk away from the workplace, start a meeting with the weekend).

**Leading a group through it (Netflix).** After an executive's racial slur split the company, Vernā Myers's sessions followed the guidelines above. Moderators used lines like "I want to bring our attention to some things that were just said" and "Some people are very emotional about this issue; maybe we can all take a breath." Myers opened with her own biases and mistakes (her "I hope she can drive!" when she heard a woman pilot during turbulence). Then she asked people when they had felt excluded, then when they had excluded *others*. Her colleague Wade Davis asked hiring managers "What is the Netflix bar? And how do you know someone meets it?" and one designer heard himself: "I define the bar as myself." Lead with belonging rather than accusation: "If the first lesson you hear is that you're biased and inherently prejudiced... It feels threatening." Perfection is not the goal: "if you're trying to say the perfect thing, nothing authentic is going to happen. The goal is staying in the conversation, finding space for messy learning and supporting each other." Brief, one-off trainings that make stereotypes salient often fail. What worked was prolonged sessions under clear guidelines that drew everyone in. Later, when a controversial comedy special divided staff, town halls ran on the same rules: everyone may talk; no shaming, blaming or attacks; think before you speak; contribute rather than just criticize. The model reply that came out of them: "I don't agree with you on everything, but thank you, I understand you're hurting and I'm committed to having this conversation." Compromise isn't always possible; "Often the best we can hope for is understanding."
