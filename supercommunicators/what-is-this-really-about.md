# The *What's This Really About?* conversation

The practical, decision-making conversation. It has two goals: first, determine what everyone *wants* from the dialogue; second, figure out *how* it will unfold: the unspoken rules and norms, and "how we will make decisions together." It usually happens at the start of a conversation, and again whenever people turn to choices, plans, or costs and benefits. When it is skipped: "We kept talking about completely different things" or "All we did was monologue at each other."

## The quiet negotiation

Every conversation opens with a ***quiet negotiation***: "A subtle give-and-take over which topics we'll dive into and which we'll skirt around; the rules for how we'll speak and listen." It runs on *invitations*, which a partner *accepts* or answers with *counter-invitations*, carried by tone, posture, asides, sighs and laughs. Its prize "is not winning, but rather determining what everyone wants."

Surface topics hide deeper ones. "We're here to discuss this quarter's projections" turns out to mean "whether there're going to be layoffs." "Should we submit an offer for the house?" sits on top of *Can we afford to pay that much?* "Do you want me to pick up the groceries or get the kids?" sits on top of *Is it fair for me to pick up the kids when I have so much work to do?* Lines such as "That memo was crazy, right?" or "I'm not sure he can get the job done" are invitations into this conversation: they signal there is something deeper to discuss.

## Part one: find out what everyone wants

**Assume nothing about what the other side wants.** Dr. Behfar Ehdaie, a prostate-cancer surgeon at Memorial Sloan Kettering, assumed his patients wanted objective data, showed them that active surveillance was as safe as surgery for 97 percent of men, and watched them fixate on the 3 percent and choose surgery anyway. The negotiation scholar Deepak Malhotra named his "biggest mistake": assuming, at the start, that he knew what the patient wanted. "You don't want to begin a negotiation assuming you know what the other side wants."

**Ask open-ended questions about values, beyond asking "What do you want?"** That direct question fails when people don't know what they want, are embarrassed to say, can't express it, or fear revealing too much. Ehdaie switched to "What does this cancer diagnosis mean to you?" and "What did your wife say when you told her about your diagnosis?" One patient talked about his father dying young: what he really wanted was the treatment that would make his family worry least. Others wanted emotional reassurance, control, social proof of what others had chosen, or the newest treatment. Ehdaie often had to ask "the same basic questions, again and again, in different ways" until something revealed what mattered. Within six months his patients choosing surgery fell by 30 percent. "It's an invitation for people to tell you who they are."

Open-ended questions come easily if you ask about:
- **beliefs or values**: "How'd you decide to become a teacher?"
- **a judgment**: "Are you glad you went to law school?"
- **experiences**: "What was it like to visit Europe?"

**Keep a ledger of what each person wants.** On the Leroy Reed jury (a felon with serious mental disabilities, on trial for a gun he kept boxed in his closet), the literature professor John Boly opened with "I'm really not at all sure what I think or what I feel on this case... I want to listen to other people and I want to talk about and figure this out together." He asked questions as casual asides (*What do you think of handguns? Do you own a gun? Can we talk about what "possession" means? What is justice?*) and tracked the answers in his head: Henry wants guidance, Barbara wants compassion, Karl wants to go by the book.

## Part two: decide how we'll make choices together

The rules for a dialogue range from the trivial (*Will we interrupt each other?*) to the crucial ("Should we send this man to jail?"). The central question is **which kind of logic everyone finds persuasive**:

- ***Logic of costs and benefits***: practical, analytical reasoning that contrasts potential costs with hoped-for benefits. Persuaded by evidence, studies and data.
- ***Logic of similarities***: decisions made by drawing on experience and asking "What does someone like me usually do in a situation like this?" It tells us when to empathize. Persuaded by compassion and stories. "Stories bypass the brain's instinct to look for reasons to be suspicious" (Emily Falk).

The two often contradict each other, so "we can only make decisions together if we all agree on which *kind* of logic is most persuasive." Tells: someone who lays out facts or asks for data is in costs and benefits; someone who tells stories, talks about values, right and wrong, or their past is in similarities. Ehdaie gave data to the first group. To the second he said he would advise his own father, a surgeon's father, to avoid the operation, and told them what other patients had done.

Mismatches: Duhigg answered his cousin's conspiracy theory ("Mattress stores are money-laundering fronts!") with public financial filings, and was told *he* was brainwashed; the cousin was reasoning from stories of elites lying before. In reverse, a customer-service rep in costs-and-benefits mode wants the transaction date, not the story of how your son ordered a thousand dollars of Legos.

## Experiments and adding items to the table

The rules get found by **experimenting**: introduce a new topic, interrupt and then don't, make a joke, ask a probing question, get suddenly serious or silly, make an unexpected concession, and watch whether others play along. "Great negotiators are artists. They take conversations in unexpected directions" (Michele Gelfand).

This is the move of ***interest-based bargaining*** (*Getting to Yes*): expand the pie, and treat negotiation as "an act of creativity," not a battle. First ask open-ended questions and listen ("If you want the other side to appreciate your interests, begin by demonstrating that you appreciate theirs"). Then **add items to the table**: "We've been focused on wages, but what if, instead of increasing paychecks, we give everyone more sick days? What if we let them work from home?" At home: open with "Am I doing enough to help?", add "What if I do grocery pickup and wash the dishes, and you get the kids and fold the laundry?", and arrive at "I want to respect your time, and work is important, so what if I get takeout and ask Uncle Arvind to get the kids, so we can both come home late?" "The challenge is not to eliminate conflict, but to transform it."

Boly worked both logics on the jury:
- **For the empathetic jurors** he added a wisp of story in a light tone: the gun "looks like a toy," and if he bought one he'd wear it ("I'm packing a rod!"), whereas Reed "handles it almost like it was this sacramental thing" and locked it in a closet. Jurors began building their own story ("he didn't take it out of the box"), and the judge's question *Did he know he had acquired a gun?* changed meaning.
- **For Karl**, a by-the-checklist firefighter who told no personal stories, the jury first used open questions to reveal his deepest desire ("Share more of that with us"; "Do you think there's ever a case where exceptions can be made?"). It was public safety. Boly then added an item *in Karl's logic*: acquitting Reed sends the DA a message to chase real criminals, so acquittal *helps* public safety. He paid down the ego cost of changing one's mind with respect: "I have an enormous amount of respect for your sense of the importance of the law." The vote went from 3 guilty, 2 leaning acquittal and 7 undecided to 12 not guilty.

## Before the conversation: prep

Jotting topics down for thirty seconds made conversations go better (fewer awkward pauses, less anxiety, more engagement) even when the topics never came up.
- Basic: **What are two topics you might discuss?** (general is fine: *last night's game*). **What is one thing you hope to say? What is one question you will ask?**
- Once that is habit: **two topics *you most want* to discuss; one thing you hope to say *that shows what you want to talk about*; one question *that reveals what others want*.**

## During the conversation: read the clues

People fill their speech with clues about what they want, and partners fail to pick up on many of them. After you extend an invitation:
- **Accepting**: they lean in, make eye contact, smile, *backchannel* ("Yeah," "Uh-huh," "Interesting"), laugh after saying something even if it wasn't funny, ask follow-ups ("What do you mean?"), or interrupt. Interruptions usually mean they want to add something.
- **Declining**: they go quiet, their expression passive, their eyes elsewhere, overly contemplative, taking in your comments without adding any. This is often mistaken for listening. Keep searching and experimenting.
- **Their own experiments**: non sequiturs, asides, abrupt new subjects, or asking the same question in different ways mean they want to add something to the table. Let them proceed.
