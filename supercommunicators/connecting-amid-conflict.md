# Connecting amid conflict, and online

During a fight it is even more important to have the *How Do We Feel?* conversation. Asking and listening are often not enough here: everyone is wondering *Are they listening, or just preparing their rebuttal?* So you must **prove** you are listening, and **share control** of the conversation.

## Every fight is at least two conflicts

The real goal of discussing a conflict is not victory but figuring out *why* the conflict exists. Within each fight there is the surface issue and the ***emotional conflict*** underneath (Sheila Heen). A couple fighting about having another child: *you want another child, and I don't* on top; underneath, *I'm angry because you're prioritizing a kid over my career*, *I'm scared another child will bankrupt us*, *I'm frustrated because you don't seem to care what I want.*

**The tell that an emotional conflict is driving things:** "No matter how many sensible things they say, they never seem to get closer to a resolution." People "love to pretend that they can become analytical robots," but the emotions leak out in other ways. Conflicts persist "not because of a lack of solutions or because people are unwilling to compromise, but because combatants don't understand *why* they are fighting." Talking about emotions won't make two people agree on a baby, "But if you don't at least talk about your emotions, you'll just keep having the same argument over and over." Everyone carries a different story in their head about why the fight is happening, and the work is hearing each other's stories.

## Looping for understanding

Eye contact and nodding rarely register: speakers are too absorbed to notice them. Proof of listening has to come *after* the speaker stops. ***Looping for understanding*** is "the single most effective technique for proving to someone that we want to hear them":
1. **Ask questions**, to make sure you understand what someone has said.
2. **Repeat back, in your own words**, what you heard. Distill it rather than parrot it.
3. **Ask if you got it right.**
4. **Continue until everyone agrees we understand.**

Looping does two things: it makes sure you hear, and it shows you *want* to hear. That second effect creates psychological safety, which makes people willing to reveal vulnerabilities. Looping "at the beginning of a conversation forestalls conflict escalation at the end," and people who loop are seen as "better teammates, advisors."

At a Washington gathering of gun-control and gun-rights activists, a gun-rights advocate named David Preston told his group about his mother's suicide when he was eleven months old. Listeners asked how he felt about her now and what he had carried away. Then a liberal activist summarized: "What I hear you saying is that you've felt a lot of pain for most of your life, and it's been hard for you to express that pain, and that has made you push people away." / "Exactly." / "And now you want to let that pain go." / "Thank you for hearing me." To a woman who kept a handgun after a relative was assaulted: "You see your gun as a symbol that you won't let anyone hurt you. Did I get that right?" / "It's proof that I deserve to feel safe."

**Structuring a dialogue across a divide** (the organizers' guidelines): each person shares a personal story about why the issue matters to them; stories come from their own experience, not things heard or seen online; they offer memories, feelings and perceptions, not lessons or takeaways. Listeners ask only open-ended, curious questions: "No rebuttals disguised as inquiries; no asking a question when you think you already know the answer." A good warm-up prompt: describe "a time when they took on a challenge where they were not sure they could succeed, but eventually tried and overcame the challenge and were proud of themselves," then loop it.

*When we are in conflict with someone…*
- **First, acknowledge understanding**: loop, and use lines like "Let me make sure I understand."
- **Second, find specific points of agreement**: "I agree with you," "I think you're right that…" These remind everyone you *want* to be aligned.
- **Finally, temper your claims.** Replace sweeping statements ("Everyone knows that's not true," "Your side always gets this wrong") with *somewhat* and "It might be…", and talk about specifics ("why you left dishes in the sink last night") rather than generalities ("how you never do your part around the house").

You can still advocate, argue and challenge, "as long as your goal is to understand, and be understood, rather than to win." Expect several rounds: "It's hard to metabolize another person's perspective in just one conversation."

## Control the right things

The marriage researchers nicknamed "the Love Shrinks" found that happy and unhappy couples fought about the same things, and that the happy ones were no better at resolving them. The difference was **control**. Unhappy couples tried to control *each other*: "You need to stop talking, right now!" "Don't use that tone with me!" "Don't even go there." "If you roll your eyes one more time, I'm leaving." (That couple divorced nine months later.) Happy couples controlled three other things. *Focus on controlling:*
- **Yourself**: taking breaks when angry, deep breathing, writing feelings down instead of shouting them, practiced habits ("I statements," a list of what they love about each other, happy memories), speaking slowly enough to stop mid-sentence, defusing with a joke or a change of subject. "Happy couples slow down the fight."
- **Your environment**: postponing the 2 a.m. fight, with the baby screaming, until a rested morning.
- **The conflict's boundaries**: "make the fight as small as possible, not let it bleed into other fights." The destructive opposite is ***kitchen-sinking***: "Are we spending the holidays with my family or yours?" becomes "You're so selfish, you never do the laundry, this is why we don't have enough money."

Controlling these three puts both sides "on the same side of the table." Trying to force someone to listen or see your side "almost never works... only inflames the battle." This is also why looping and matching work: proving you listen hands the other person some control, and following their emotional or practical lead shares control over how the dialogue flows. "There're lots of conflicts that don't have solutions. But when everyone feels in control, the conflict sometimes just fades away."

Control attempts are often invisible to the person making them: telling someone how they may feel ("There's no way you should feel that way"); answering a concern with an instant solution or a long monologue; downplaying it ("I wouldn't have seen the big deal"); restricting who gets to speak. Moderators steered people back to shared control. For self-control: "When you feel triggered or angry, take a breath. If you find yourself feeling defensive, step back." For environment, less polarizing language: *rule of law* for *police state*, *gun-rights advocate* for *freedom warriors*, *tactical rifles* for *assault weapons*. For boundaries: one topic at a time, "not a debate with a goal of scoring points." A former cop who supported gun rights replied first to a gun-control post on red-flag laws, saying he had wished he could take guns from dangerous people and asking for experiences on that one narrow issue, while she looped the replies. Dozens of people then told nuanced stories instead of fighting. "Listening means letting someone else tell their story and then, even if you don't agree with them, trying to understand why they feel that way."

## Online

Online conversation lacks voice and body and is fast and unedited. *When talking online, remember to…*
- **Overemphasize politeness.** Tensions drop if at least one person stays consistently polite; adding *thanks* and *please* alone reduced them.
- **Underemphasize sarcasm.** You hear the wry tone in your head; readers don't.
- **Express more gratitude, deference, greetings, apologies, and hedges**: "That comment taught me a lot," "I would love to hear your thoughts," "Hey!", "I hope you don't mind…", "I think…"
- **Avoid criticism in public forums.** Public negative feedback online backfires and makes bad behavior a digital norm.
