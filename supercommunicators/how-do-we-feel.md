# The *How Do We Feel?* conversation

"Every conversation is, in some respect, a discussion about *How Do We Feel?*" Emotions are already shaping the dialogue through a sigh, a flash of pride or a passing mention of a family emergency. At that moment you choose: acknowledge the feeling and respond emotionally yourself, or glide past it into "the shallows of small talk." "Most of the time, that's the wrong choice": acknowledging someone's vulnerability, and becoming vulnerable in return, builds trust. When Duhigg's father died, almost no one asked him anything; "What was your dad like?" would have been treasured.

**The framework for emotional connection:** "If you want to connect with someone, ask them what they are feeling, and then reveal your own emotions." *How Do We Feel?* "functions by inviting others to reveal their vulnerabilities, and then being vulnerable in return."

## Perspective getting, not perspective taking

*Perspective taking* means imagining yourself in someone's shoes and showing empathy. When Nicholas Epley was a teenager caught drunk driving, his parents did exactly that, and it only showed him how much they *didn't* understand, because they had never asked. His counselor only asked: "Why were you drinking?" "How would you have reacted if your car had hit someone?" "Is this who you want to be?" The questions never mentioned emotions but drew them out, and he changed. Epley's alternative is ***perspective getting***: ask people to describe their inner lives, values, beliefs and feelings, then listen. "When you describe how you feel, you're giving someone a map of the things you care about."

## Deep questions

Researchers found a reliable test for which questions spark emotion. Questions about everyday experiences or uncontroversial opinions ("How did you celebrate last Halloween?" "What is the best gift you ever received?") get unemotional answers. Questions that push people to describe **beliefs, values, or meaningful experiences** get emotional answers even when they don't sound emotional ("What do you value most in a friendship?"). "The difference between a shallow question and one that sparks an opportunity for emotional connection is vulnerability."

A ***deep question*** can be light ("What would be your perfect day?") or heavy ("What do you regret most?"), and can look innocuous ("Tell me about your family," "Why do you look so happy today?"). Nearly any question can be remade into one using three characteristics:
1. **It asks about values, beliefs, judgments, or experiences, rather than just facts.** Swap "Where do you work?" for "What's the best part of your job?" Shortcut: before speaking, imagine you're talking to a close friend. What would you ask?
2. **It asks people to talk about how they feel**: "How do you feel about…?" "Did it make you happy when…?" "Why do you think he got angry?" "How would you feel if that happened to you?"
3. **Asking it should feel like sharing**, as if you are revealing something about yourself. People are nearly always happy to have been asked.

Fact questions ("Where do you live?" "What college did you attend?") are conversational dead-ends. Recast them: "What do you like about where you live?" "What was your favorite part of college?" Epley on a train: "What do you do for a living?" → "Do you love that job?" or "Do you have something else you dream of doing?" "Two questions in, and you've gotten to somebody's dreams." In speed-dating studies, the successful conversations drew out "needs, goals, beliefs [and] emotions"; the failed ones were people talking about themselves or asking shallow questions.

**Follow-up questions** are particularly powerful: they signal you're listening and make reciprocity easy ("Your favorite part of college was ultimate frisbee? Me too!"). "They allow self-disclosure without it seeming like self-obsession." Deep questions also allow vulnerable replies without demanding them. That helps in offices, and it helps people (such as women) who are penalized for showing emotion: asking "What's the best part of working here?" makes the listener think, and can loosen their stereotypes.

**Start deep.** People consistently expect deep conversations with strangers "to be more awkward, and to lead to weaker connections and less happiness, than they actually did," with no meaningful gender differences. Epley paired hedge-fund strangers on three questions ("If a crystal ball could tell you the future, would you want to know?" "For what do you feel most grateful?" "Can you describe a time you cried in front of another person?"); they ignored his calls to stop until twenty minutes had passed. People who ask lots of questions, especially ones inviting vulnerable answers, are more popular and more often seen as leaders.

## Emotional contagion and reciprocity

***Emotional contagion***: humans "synchronize their own emotions with the emotions expressed by those around them," often automatically. One of its most reliable triggers is vulnerability: revealing deeply held beliefs and values or meaningful experiences, or anything that opens us to others' judgment. "To get deep, we have to make an offering of our vulnerability." The cycle: deep questions → vulnerability → emotional contagion → connection.

The Arons' *Fast Friends Procedure* (36 escalating questions that made strangers close, and in one case married) worked only when partners took turns. When one person answered all the questions and then the other did, it was awkward and nobody felt close. "Reciprocity is critical."

Reciprocity is nuanced. If someone reveals a scary diagnosis, reciprocating with your own aches and pains grabs the spotlight. Emotional reciprocity is "empathetic support": "I know how scary that is. Tell me what you're feeling." *We reciprocate vulnerability by…*
- **Looping for understanding** until you understand what someone is feeling (steps in [`connecting-amid-conflict.md`](connecting-amid-conflict.md)).
- **Looking for what someone needs**: comfort? empathy? advice? tough love? If you don't know, loop more.
- **Asking permission**: "Would it be okay if I told you how your words affect me?" "Would you mind if I shared something from my own life?" "Can I share how I've seen others handle this?"
- **Giving something in return**: "It makes me sad to hear you're in pain," "I'm so happy for you," "I'm proud to be your friend."

"Reciprocity isn't about matching vulnerability to vulnerability, or sorrow to sorrow. Rather, it is being emotionally available."

## Hearing emotions no one says aloud

People rarely announce emotions; they perform them ("I cooked you dinner and you're welcome!"). These are ***nonlinguistic emotional expressions***: tone, cadence, gesture, posture, expression, sighs, laughs. Adults learn to trust words ("It's nothing. I feel fine.") over crossed arms, a creased brow and downcast eyes.

Specific emotions "are really, really hard to read with any accuracy." Read two dimensions instead:
- **Mood** (*valence*): positive or negative? upbeat or glum? laughing or shouting?
- **Energy** (*arousal*): high or low? talkative and expressive, or quiet and withdrawn?

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| **High energy** | upbeat, enthusiastic, joyful, excited | angry, indignant, insulted, outraged |
| **Low energy** | blissful, content, grateful, satisfied | frustrated, annoyed, grumpy, discouraged |

"We exhibit emotional intelligence by showing people that we've heard their emotions—and the way we do that is by noticing, and then matching, their mood and energy." Sometimes you match exactly: laugh as joyfully as they laugh. When matching would only feed tension (anger met with anger), acknowledge instead: "You seem sad... What will cheer you up?" or "You seem upset. What's wrong?" Either way the message is *I hear your feelings*. When you express emotion yourself, watch whether others try to align with your mood and energy.

**Laughter is the clearest case.** Less than 20 percent of conversational laughter answers anything like a joke; we laugh to show we want to connect. A belly laugh met with a chuckle reads as rejection or pandering; matched intensity reads as connection. Listeners can tell friends from strangers in one second of recorded laughter.

**NASA's test.** The psychiatrist Terence McGuire screened astronaut candidates by expressing emotion himself: he spilled his papers and joked that his son's balloon tie made him "look like a clown," and he told them about his sister's death with his voice wavering. Then he watched whether they matched. One candidate gave a "perfect answer" about his father's death, then said only "He was very kind" and asked nothing about the sister; he was not selected. Another laughed, helped gather the papers, and asked "Were you close? How did it impact your mom? Do you think about her, even now?"; he was selected. "The specific emotions a candidate displayed were less important than *how* they expressed them": did they make clear they were *trying* to align?

**The Big Bang Theory's fix.** The first pilot failed because the audience couldn't tell how to feel. In the reshoot, Leonard, Sheldon and Penny trade the same word, "Hi," at the same volume and speed with different attitudes, and the audience understood at once that everyone was clumsily trying to connect. "If one of them had said 'hello' instead of 'hi,' or if one of them had been loud and then Penny got soft, the whole scene would've fallen apart." Deliberately mismatched mood and energy (Sheldon loud and fast about "his" seat, Penny slow and quiet) signals a failure to connect.
