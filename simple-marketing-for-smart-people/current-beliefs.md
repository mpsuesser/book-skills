# Step 1: Identify your prospects' current beliefs

Your starting point is where the prospect is now. Step into the customer's shoes and see the world through their current knowledge and beliefs. In published marketing you can't see heads shaking at a term they don't know, so you have to research the gap.

## How hard is a belief to change?

"You can't change someone's beliefs" really means it can be difficult, and the difficulty depends on how deep-seated the belief is: lightly held or deeply ingrained. Marketing beliefs are rarely religion or core values. Getting someone to clean their carpets (carpets are dirtier than a toilet seat) is far easier than getting them to change religions. Where you can, **preach to the choir**: speak to people who already hold most of the required beliefs. They move to a decision faster, and you spend less time on people who may never align.

## The chain of beliefs

Picture the beliefs someone needs to buy as a **chain**, each belief linked to the next, running from where the prospect is now all the way to your product. Rich Schefren's term; it is also called the *sequence of beliefs* or the *chain of agreements*. Link together every belief a prospect needs to go "from unaware to fully informed," fix the broken links, and the prospect proceeds to the last belief: "Yes, this product is worth my money. I will buy it."

### Concentric rings

You broadcast from the center. The rings, from the center outward, are **Customer, Hot prospect, Warm prospect, Cold prospect.**

| | Shorter chain of beliefs (inner rings, close to a purchase decision) | Longer chain of beliefs (outer rings, beginning of the customer journey) |
|---|---|---|
| What they lack | Don't know if your offer is a good value, if now is a good time to buy, if your product will be easy to use, or if they should trust you | Don't recognize they have a problem, don't know about potential solutions, don't know your topic, don't know you |
| Next beliefs to instill | "I trust this company" • "This fits my timeline" • "This is worth the money" | "I have a problem" • "This could get worse" • "Other people have been through this" |
| Content centered on | Your company and your offering | The problem they're dealing with |
| Copywriting rule | Warm prospects respond to price, the guarantee, and the company's trustworthiness | Cold prospects aren't ready for direct offers: address the problem first, then introduce the solutions, then present yours |

Inner-ring prospects already know their problem and the landscape of solutions, know you, and hold no blocking false beliefs. Outer-ring prospects may need an hour just to get up to speed. Educating them is a job for marketing content, not one-on-one sales: one piece reaching 10,000 people beats 10,000 conversations, so marketing content lets you "multiply yourself." Using the same messaging for both groups causes confusion and hurts sales.

### Whom to target first

- **Normandy Strategy**: start with the inner rings. Convert the low-hanging fruit, build cash reserves, then advance outward, "taking one beach at a time" so the easy sales fund the expansion.
- The pool of true believers runs out quickly. The outer rings hold millions more people, and reaching them costs time, money and effort. Weigh going after colder prospects against the "gold to mine" left in your current audience.
- **Buyers tend to be repeat buyers.** People who bought products similar to yours are among the most likely to buy yours, and your best prospects are often your existing customers, because they already hold the required beliefs. Competitors' customers hold them too. With them, your task is beliefs about how your product is different; they already know their problem. So start with people who bought something similar, then experiment with broader audiences and gauge the response.
- Expanding into foundational beliefs often uncovers key beliefs you'd been taking for granted. When content hits one, the audience starts buzzing (clicks, shares, purchases): you've "struck a nerve."

Case: a self-improvement education company forty years old, with a loyal audience, a huge email list and multiple Facebook posts a day, had no on-ramp into its topic. It threw terms around casually, linked to no foundational ideas, and dropped new subscribers in the deep end. Its coupons had done nothing, and it assumed its prices were too high. Billy built an extremely basic introductory course; the client objected, "It's too simple... Our audience is more advanced." It became the company's largest revenue event in forty years, and sales of the advanced products rose too, a **domino effect** that is common when Belief Building works. The cost was real (team time, paused projects, consulting), so outer-ring expansion is a deliberate balancing act.

## Three methods to identify current beliefs

### Method 1: Listen for the (bad) questions your customer asks

The most revealing questions are the "wrong," "bad" or even "dumb" ones (keep that word to yourself). A beginner asking whether to use American or Belgian yeast is fine for now. A beginner asking about northern versus southern Belgian strains is asking a bad question: it is too nuanced, and answering it implicitly says "That's a good question to ask." Their real goal is good-tasting beer, and the leading cause of bad flavor is poor cleaning and sanitizing.

- Redirect gently: "I hear you, and I understand why you want to know that, but let me share something I wish I knew when I first got started..." Then ask about their current practices, steps and results.
- **Give your prospects better questions to ask.** Rejecting the wrong question is a kindness, because it shows them how to get what they want. Handle it delicately; it takes courage for a beginner to question an expert.
- Ask yourself: What does my audience really want? Which of their questions aren't the best ones for reaching that goal? How can I redirect them? Their questions reveal their current beliefs and what they think is important. The Ladder of Importance in [`required-beliefs.md`](required-beliefs.md) is the tool for the redirect.

### Method 2: Ask them questions

Create a space where the customer feels neither judged nor pressured. Use **what/how questions**, not "The Five Whys." Joe Hudson, the authors' mentor, points out that "why" questions sound judgmental and make people defensive, while "what" and "how" questions are softer and draw people out.

- "Why did you do that?" becomes "What were you trying to accomplish?" or "How did you go about making that decision?"
- What led you to purchase X product or hire X service provider?
- Think about the last time this problem occurred. How did you deal with it?
- What makes it important for you to succeed with X? (For a CEO: "What makes it important for you to be seen as a strong leader?")

### Method 3: Peek into the cycles of your prospect's life

Demographic "ideal customer" profiles (Sophie, 35, drives a black 4Runner, makes $80K) do little to uncover beliefs. Use the **Cycles-Based Approach** instead. People live in cycles (rituals, routines, patterns), and a cycle's pattern of behavior exposes the beliefs behind it.

- **Physical therapist.** Injury unfolds as before, during and after. Ask, "Can you walk me through what happened when you got injured?" A soccer player played through a twisted ankle, rested, iced it, took painkillers and saw a doctor a week later. The beliefs: **Pride** (don't look weak in front of peers), **Self-Reliance** (home remedies first, perhaps thinking it wasn't serious or to save money), and **Influence of Past Experiences** (self-care worked before, or a professional didn't). Tailor the approach and communication to those beliefs.
- **Leadership coach** (Clarke Ching, "The Bottleneck Guy"). The cycle: a hit product, soaring demand, mass hiring, and a senior leader doing the job of two or three people, too busy managing to lead. The leader becomes the bottleneck without seeing it. Predictable behaviors: reading time-management books, taking productivity courses, working late and "being the hero," updating the task software last thing at night and first thing in the morning, micromanaging. Beliefs behind them: "The problem is my time management." "I'm not the bottleneck, I'm just very busy." "If I take a break, everything will break." "It's easier if I do it myself." Jump straight to the solution and there is no buy-in. The coach first meets the client where they are, so the client feels seen and heard, then shows the true cause of the problem, and only then introduces the solution.

### Whichever method you use

- Study prospects for patterns, commonalities and prevalent beliefs.
- Look for insights that challenge your assumptions. Did anything surprise you?
- Find what your marketing material assumes that isn't true. Have you taken a belief for granted? Do you need to back up a step to a belief further up the chain?

This exposes the curse of knowledge: the places you assumed the prospect knew or believed something they didn't.
