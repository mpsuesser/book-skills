# The marketing landscape: channels, tactics, commodity vs fancy eggs, practice

## Midstream: marketing channels

Channels are the river's branches, the distribution channels and platforms that carry your core message to prospects. Each one is a whole ecosystem where messages are exchanged, relationships are built and goods are sold. People equate marketing with channels because the most-used phone apps are also the biggest channels. But "90% of their hard work is done before their ad appears on their customer's social media feed." Gurus who insist you must be on one platform are channel experts, not marketing experts, and their survival depends on FOMO.

**20 marketing channels:** YouTube, podcasts, email newsletters, Facebook, Instagram, X (formerly Twitter), Pinterest, LinkedIn, TikTok, SEO, pay-per-click advertising, text messaging, Amazon, TV commercials, direct mail, billboards, newspapers, bus benches, trade shows, conferences.

**The smart marketer's mindset toward channels:**
- Put most of the effort into a compelling core message.
- Don't put too much hope in any single channel. Test several.
- When a channel works, double down until it fails to deliver an acceptable return on investment.
- Channels come and go, so never think success rides on one of them.

**Spotting channel advice.** Headlines like "5 Tips to Go Viral on Instagram," "This Year's Most Important Updates to Facebook Ads," "How to Optimize Your YouTube Thumbnails for Maximum Clicks," "13 SEO Tips for Higher Rankings" or "Podcast Promotion Strategies" should trigger the thought "I'm getting advice about marketing channels." You can ignore 99% of them until your core message is set; after that they start to make a difference.

## Downstream: marketing tactics

Tactics are the specific actions you take to steer, like choosing a paddle stroke or getting around a fallen tree. They only help if you know your destination. Without a core message they are "like paddling aggressively without knowing which way the river flows." They are "shiny objects," candy for the mind.

**20 marketing tactics:** viral giveaways, online contests, website quizzes, website pop-ups, push notifications, guest blogging, upsells, downsells, chatbots, video marketing, two-step opt-ins, retargeting ads, referral programs, limited-time offers, themed social media days, online communities, mobile optimization, voice search, hashtags, cart abandonment emails.

**Spotting tactic advice.** "Unlock the Power of Viral Giveaways," "Five Creative Ways to Use Website Pop-ups," "Proven Templates for Cart Abandonment Emails," "Chatbots: Uplevel Your Customer Service Game," "Stay Top of Mind with Push Notifications." Resist the dopamine hit and put your attention upstream on the core message.

**Prioritizing a limited week.** For a smart person with, say, ninety minutes a week for marketing, tasks are not equal. Use the river to categorize what's in front of you and spend the time upstream on the core message. With it in place, you get the most out of whatever channels and tactics you use. Marketing courses you've already bought (Facebook ads, funnels, YouTube) reach their full potential once the core message is upstream of them. You have permission to stop doing and spend time thinking.

## Commodity eggs vs fancy eggs

Anything for sale shows the Belief Building pattern. Read a list of household items (milk, dish soap, socks, laundry detergent, sponges, shampoo, bath towels, trash bags, lightbulbs), then "Eight-week executive coaching program for CEOs." The resistance you feel at the last item is "you bumping into the world of Belief Building."

- **Commodity eggs** are easy to understand, so nobody reads a long sales page for a $1.99 carton and companies compete on price. Customers are **fully aware**, a copywriting term for customers with no (or a very small) gap between their current beliefs and the beliefs needed to buy.
- **Fancy eggs** (organic, free-range, pasture-raised, omega-fortified) made eggs complicated. Cartons came with booklets answering "What does organic mean? Why is organic better for you?" to justify paying $5 instead of the usual $1.50 a dozen. De-commoditizing a product to raise its price requires Belief Building.
- **Happy Egg Company** puts its claim in its name: the chickens are happy, which market research showed customers care about. The proof covers all three appeals. **Logos**: data on outdoor hours (8+ a day) and acreage (8–10 acres). **Pathos**: the story of chickens playing all day and sleeping in the barn, safe from predators. **Ethos**: free range and USDA-certified organic, borrowing a government agency's credibility.

**The pattern:** the better a customer understands a product, the less copy it takes to educate them, and the worse they understand it, the more. Long sales pages are long because they may have thirty beliefs to instill. That is why tests show long pages beating short ones, provided they're well written; many pages are long only because their authors think they should be. Billy's common critique: "Nope, I'm not convinced. You need to write more."

**Ask: am I selling commodity eggs or fancy eggs?** Ninety-nine percent of readers sell fancy eggs: something that needs explaining. Too many smart people market them like commodity eggs, as if selling hand soap. Merchandising tactics (coupons, buy-one-get-one, loyalty points, favorable financing) don't transfer to a fancy egg product.

**What sets how much Belief Building a product needs:**
- **Price.** The lower the price, the less Belief Building.
- **Length of the sales cycle.** A gallon of milk to a family sells faster than accounting software to the government.
- **Complexity.** A coaching program is harder to use than eggs.
- **Novelty.** Henry Ford had to educate horse-and-buggy owners about the Model T.

| Less Belief Building needed | More Belief Building needed |
|---|---|
| Grocery store eggs | Organic eggs |
| Plain white T-shirt | T-shirt with new cotton blend |
| Notebook | Online course on productivity |
| Pocket calculator | Business accounting software |
| Plastic cutlery set | Ergonomic, steel cutlery |
| Standard gym membership | Personalized fitness and wellness program |

**Exercises:**
1. **Same Product, Two Audiences.** Explain one product to an audience already familiar with it and to one with no prior knowledge or beliefs about it. How do the explanation and selling points differ?
2. **From Basic to Premium.** Take a household commodity such as dishwashing detergent, imagine a premium version, and write messages that build beliefs about its unique qualities.

When a fully aware customer sees no difference between you and your competitors, price is their only basis for choosing, which is a race to the bottom. A real difference, taught well, lets you charge more.

## Marketing with integrity: what the authors skip and what they practice

Marketing done this way is product development, user research, content creation, world-building, customer service and deep empathy, "so good, selling becomes unnecessary because people sell themselves on it." You can skip "probably 90% of what most people think of when they hear 'online marketing.'"

**Tiago won't use:** unethical high-pressure sales tactics; complex funnels with endless sub-branches; countdown timers with fake urgency; artificial social proof from "anonymous" testimonials; "limited time" offers that aren't limited; relentless self-promotion on social media; "live" webinars you can watch any hour of the day.

**Tiago practices:** clear and persuasive copywriting; classic, heartfelt storytelling about people's everyday problems; a strong, well-communicated differentiator; an email list he controls and regularly provides value to; informative, insightful content that delivers value upfront while moving people toward a sale; long-term relationships; authentic testimonials that help people see themselves in the offer.

Done this way, people look forward to your promotions. An educated prospect is a better prospect, while dishonest marketers prefer to keep prospects in the dark. All you need is knowledge of your prospect, knowledge of your product, and the Belief Building approach.

## Next steps and other uses

1. **Revisit your current audience.** Ask: What does my customer already know? How long is their chain of beliefs? Is this who I want to target going forward? Some owners find they've been selling a too-advanced product to beginners, or an introductory one to advanced buyers. Either way, realign the messaging, channels and testimonials to the real target.
2. **Complete your core messaging document** by running the Belief Building exercises. Use it to guide all content, presentations, and any sales page written with claim/proof.
3. **Create new marketing material.** The best moment is just before a push: a launch, website redesign, rebrand or newsletter rollout.
4. **Use Belief Building beyond selling the product.**
   - A service provider can build a free online course that prepares prospects for high-end services, especially if they keep repeating the same basics.
   - Before launching a new product to existing customers, educate them on what they need to know to buy it.
   - Use it while delivering the product: a teacher in a classroom, a physical therapist raising compliance with a treatment plan, an executive coach getting a CEO to follow advice. Whenever you have someone's best interest at heart and need them to act, it is the tool.
5. **Get full value from trainings you already bought.** The core message upstream makes the channel and tactic courses pay off.

To start, pick the one action you're most excited about, then write down when you'll give it at least thirty minutes, where, who's involved, and what you'll need.
