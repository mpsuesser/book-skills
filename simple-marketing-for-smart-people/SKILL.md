# Simple Marketing for Smart People

Marketing is teaching: "You are teaching people how to value your product." The whole system hangs on one **focusing question**:

> **What does my customer need to believe in order to buy?**

It is "the only question you need to ask yourself to develop your core message." Answering it and turning the answers into marketing material is **Belief Building**: "the process of bringing a prospect to the point where they fully understand and value our offer. Buying from us becomes their only logical choice." Think of it as a sophisticated form of objection handling. Sales training handles objections at the point of sale; Belief Building handles them earlier in the customer's journey and actively educates the prospect on the offer's value and relevance. Belief Building first gets you buy-in, and buy-in leads to buyers. Peter Drucker: "The aim of marketing is to make selling superfluous." Your role shifts from seller to educator and guide, and the sale becomes the natural end of the journey. Customers sold this way are pre-sold by the checkout page: they stay loyal, buy again, and become advocates because they believe in the product, not because they got a deal. "The best prospects to pitch to are the ones who have the beliefs needed to buy. You can—and should—be the one to instill those beliefs."

The origin cases: Joe, a broke carpet cleaner whose coupons failed, realized his town didn't "get" the value of clean carpets (4,000 times dirtier than a toilet seat, a possible harbor for mold, one of the leading causes of asthma). He wrote articles on why to clean carpets, how often, and how to tell they were cleaned correctly. "This educational material became his marketing material," and it grew into a million-dollar business. Eugene Schwartz ("Belief is the goal") could not sell a 1950s TV repair manual until he first instilled the belief that the prospect *could* repair a TV; without it, even a 50% discount would not have mattered. "Once you realize that your prospects don't hold the beliefs required to accept your offer, your task switches from selling to Belief Building."

The question works because it is **convergent**. Smart people's expertise has split one topic into hundreds of subtopics, and marketing itself diverges into endless channels and tactics. "If divergence is the problem, convergence is the solution." Convergence is not dumbing down. The depth stays; you just don't lead with the details.

## The river: where marketing effort goes

Marketing is a river with three sections. Sort every task, tip, tool and piece of advice into one of them, then work upstream first.

| Upstream | Midstream | Downstream |
|---|---|---|
| **Core message**: "the central argument for why someone should choose your product or service." Answer the focusing question here. | **Channels**: the places you distribute Belief Building content, such as your website, email, YouTube, podcasts, SEO and ads. | **Tactics**: actions that amplify the message, such as pop-ups, giveaways, hashtags, retargeting, chatbots, share buttons and countdown timers. |

- Upstream decisions lock in direction. Good upstream decisions can often compensate for downstream errors. Skilled moves in a wrong direction have limited impact.
- The core message is the underground spring. Customers never see it, but it gives every piece of marketing its direction and coherence. It needs high quality, not "good enough." Keep it in a **core messaging document**, which is not a brand manual: a brand manual sets visuals (logo, fonts, colors), while the core messaging document lists "the beliefs your prospects need to hold for your product to be an obvious purchase." Refer to it whenever you create new marketing material. Your raw ingredients are the components of your core message, and the upfront thinking spent on them beats obsessing over the website, plugins and content volume. Tiago's is a Google Doc of 100+ beliefs, and it replaces a list of 100 random content ideas ("throwing spaghetti at the wall").
- Tactics are not the big needle movers; they suit incremental gains after you have traction. When the core message is dialed in, "all of those shiny objects work better." Case: a $10,000 Facebook funnel with retargeting, upsells and downsells made zero sales because no thought went into its messaging. A simple, well-thought-out email campaign then brought in $40,000 in a couple of weeks, and the new messaging lifted the website, social accounts and ads too.
- Outsource marketing *activities* (ads, design, posting, the website) freely. Create the core message yourself: an agency or intern lacks your topic's language, your empathy for the customer, and the judgment to spot a bad fit.

## The Belief Building process

Run the three steps in order. The output of steps 1 and 2 is the core messaging document; step 3 turns it into content.

1. **Identify your prospects' current beliefs.** Use the three research methods: listen for the (bad) questions customers ask, ask them what/how questions, and peek into the cycles of their lives. Place prospects on the **chain of beliefs**, the linked beliefs running from where the prospect is now to "Yes, this product is worth my money. I will buy it." Long chains (cold prospects) need problem-centered content; short chains (hot prospects) need content about you and your offer. Decide whom to target first. Done when you have recorded the prevalent beliefs, the false beliefs, and the beliefs you have been taking for granted. Methods, rings, targeting and cases: [`current-beliefs.md`](current-beliefs.md).
2. **Identify the beliefs required for the purchase to be obvious.** Start from a shared reality the prospect already accepts (the apple on the table). Anchor the beliefs to the customer journey's **Awareness, Consideration and Decision** stages and run the nine stage questions. Build your **Ladder of Importance**, the success factors of your field ranked from most to least important. Bring in other people to expose your blind spots. Organize and prioritize the beliefs into the core messaging document, and keep it revised as competitors, products and technology change. Done when every stage has its beliefs, each objection to your option has been recognized, and the ladder's top rung is clear. Stage questions, ladder, objections and examples: [`required-beliefs.md`](required-beliefs.md).
3. **Create the marketing content that builds those beliefs.** Treat each piece as a rhetorical argument: **pick the belief, make a claim, prove it.** Back each claim with mixed proof (ethos, pathos, logos), and stack evidence the way a lawyer presents Exhibit A, B and C. Fill in the input card (belief, claim, proof, format, channel, call to action) before you write. Ask of every proposed piece, "Which belief is this going to instill or strengthen?" Drop it if the answer is none, or if that belief already has plenty of content. Done when each piece of content maps to one required belief and every claim in it carries proof. Claim/proof model, the two misconceptions about proof, Aristotle's appeals, worked examples, free content and testing: [`building-belief.md`](building-belief.md).

## Diagnostics

- **Missing one belief can derail the whole sale.** A prospect who holds 99.9% of the beliefs but thinks online courses don't work will not buy. A false belief (the academics' charge that Tiago's course "promotes plagiarism") sabotages the sale just as surely. Bridge the gap: face the belief head-on and flip it into a strength.
- **Price isn't the issue yet.** If prospects don't understand the offer, lowering the price fixes nothing. A coupon does little for someone who doesn't believe in the value. Discounting cuts margins, leaves the customer unconvinced, and brings worse customers: "Discounting may get us better sales figures, but it doesn't get us better customers." The customers who pay the least tend to need the most attention. The book's advice: stop printing coupons, giving deals and making concessions.
- **Commodity eggs or fancy eggs?** "The better a customer understands a product, the less copy is needed to educate them. The worse a customer understands a product, the more copy is needed." A *fully aware* customer has little or no gap between current and required beliefs, so they buy on price. Most experts sell fancy eggs but market them like commodity eggs; slashing a $3,000 workshop to $99 is the classic case. Price, length of sales cycle, complexity and novelty set how much Belief Building a product needs. "Unless you're Walmart, you always want to be in a position where you need to build belief." See [`landscape.md`](landscape.md).
- **Content that feels basic is working.** Building beliefs feels like "dumbing down." Call it distilling, finding the essence, finding the signal in the noise. Diving straight into the full complexity of your topic "displays a lack of empathy for your customer." Ask: What stage is my reader at? What's their readiness for the next step? What is that next step or milestone? How can I meet them where they are?
- **Superfans are almost a false positive.** Loyal fans buy at launch and then the "well of true believers" runs dry. Refill it by educating people in the outer rings.

## Smart-person failure modes

The curse of knowledge is the Achilles' heel: a sharp, detail-loving mind overcomplicates. The book's image for this is the NASA brewery, a gorgeous rig with too many moving parts that kept breaking and nearly set the house on fire. Watch for:

- **Learning instead of implementing.** Reading every marketing article, worshiping complexity for its own sake, being "suffocated by the weight of the fancy systems they've built." Aim at the critical parts, not exhaustive understanding.
- **Thinking the product will sell itself.** "It's harder to sell a product than make one." People pay only when you speak "in their language, on their terms, to their needs."
- **Incorrect assumptions about success.** Expensive production (Tiago's $5,000 trailer video) has little correlation with results. Validate first, for example by teaching the content to one person. "Production values don't matter until later. Way later."
- **Seeing marketing as inherently bad.** "Nothing about sales or marketing is inherently bad. It's how they're often done that's bad." The best marketing goes unnoticed as marketing, so learn timeless principles rather than copying examples found in the wild. Follow the golden rule of marketing: "market to others as you want to be marketed to."
- **Taking a stand too timidly.** Prospects want an expert who says "Here's what I recommend, and here's why." Without claims, marketing gets diluted and prospects go to competitors who state their stances boldly.

Closing stance: people can be trusted to make good decisions for themselves when they are presented with the truth. Be emotionally unattached to the sale. Clear up misconceptions, lay all possible paths on the table, argue for your recommended path, and "let the chips fall where they fall." Show why you created your solution, how it works, who it is perfect for and who it isn't designed for. Entertain as you educate, and be "smarter, not louder."
