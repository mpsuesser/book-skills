# Step 3: Create the content that builds the required beliefs

## You're making an argument

"Don't think about what you're doing as marketing—think about it as making an argument." You are arguing for your way of doing things and for why some factors matter more than others. This is a *rhetorical argument*, not an emotional confrontation. Marketing comes from rhetoric, "the art of persuasive communication," which Aristotle developed in the fourth century BC. A student told that composition is the most important thing in photography has no reason to believe it until you argue the case.

### Topic-based vs argument-based content

The common trap is the **topic-based approach**: publishing content broadly about your topic, then its subtopics. Billy wore himself out this way and got likes and followers but few sales. The **argument-based approach** asserts claims and backs them with evidence. When he switched, sales rose and responses changed from polite thank-yous to deeper, heartfelt replies. Audiences want "guidance from someone willing to take a stand," and an argument creates clarity and conviction. Research agrees: a technical subject taught with slides of assertions backed by evidence produced significantly better understanding and retention than conventional bullet-point slides that took no stand (the assertion-evidence approach).

The two biggest mistakes smart people make are failing to think through the customer's beliefs and failing to prove their claims. The focusing question avoids both. Identify the beliefs, then argue for each one: "Here is my argument for why you should believe X and then Y and now Z." Gary Bencivenga: "I have always advised my clients to build their products squarely on their strongest proof elements."

## The claim/proof model

A **claim** is the statement you need the audience to accept. What must follow a claim is **proof**. Claim/proof is "the source code of persuasive human communication," and it works in any area of life.

- **Courtroom.** You defend Jimmy, a shoplifter, and you need the judge to believe "Jimmy is turning his life around." Said alone, it earns "I don't believe you." Lawyers present Exhibit A, then B, then C, and it's often the cumulative evidence that sways the court. Billy's proofs for Jimmy: an exam paper from his community college marked "A," praise from the leader of the shelter where he volunteers, and the story of his harsh upbringing, which builds empathy. "Be that lawyer in the courtroom": add evidence upon evidence until the argument is airtight.
- **Brand advertising.** A car climbs a mountain and forges a river. The claim, implied rather than stated: this car is tough. The proof: the car driving in extreme environments.
- **Stand-up comedy.** The claim is a relatable truth ("My kids are obsessed with technology"), and the proof is the examples that land the laugh (suggest a sunrise and they Google it). Chris Rock notes that new comedians mistake the setup (the claim) for the punchline (the proof). A claim without proof leaves the audience unsatisfied, and the same critique applies to most marketing.
- **Movies.** Disney's eternal claim is that the hero is brave. Moana proves it three ways: she leaves an island no one has left, on a small shaky raft over giant waves, to intense, ominous music. Proof can be multi-sensory. Ask, "How can you dramatize your proof points?"

**Weaving proof in:** first nail down what you want to say (the claim), then decide how you'll say it (the proof). Connect each claim to a belief required to buy. "Flood your content with proof. Throw in colors, sounds, feelings—the whole shebang. Hit your point from every angle." Marketing is a "choreography between assertion and creativity": the claims are plain statements, and proving them is where the creativity comes in.

**Take a stand.** Without claims, marketing gets diluted and prospects leave for competitors who state their stances boldly. Aisha Williams, an estate planning attorney whose family was torn apart by her grandfather's hole-filled simple will, claims "A will alone is not enough." Prospects want an expert who says "Here's what I've seen, and here's what I recommend." So say "Here's what I recommend, and here's why"; the "here's why" is the proof. Backing up claims is also how you stay honest.

## Two misconceptions about proof

1. **Proof isn't only proof that the product works.** "Works" assumes a measure of success that customers don't share (one spouse loves a couch for its comfort, the other for its looks). Most objections come long before the credit card. The earliest is often not realizing there's a problem at all ("I don't need an estate plan"). Price objections only arise if you get to a discussion of price, and most prospects never get that far. A retired man with a will but no estate plan has a long chain of beliefs ahead of him. Proving that the attorney is fast and reasonably priced means nothing to him yet. She has to make claims about the problem and prove those. If he doesn't accept the early claim, every claim about her services is irrelevant.
2. **Proof isn't always scientific proof.** People make most decisions without lab results and follow a trusted friend over a study. If your brother says the award-winning laundromat is awful, you go elsewhere. Proofs for "A will alone is not enough":
   - **A personal story**: her family's ordeal.
   - **A scientific study or data**: for example, an analysis of California estates that had a will but no estate plan showing they took double the time on average to release to heirs.
   - **An analogy**: having only a will is like locking the front door but leaving the windows wide open.

Tailor proof to the person: a research study for an engineer, a visual analogy for an artist. Learn which proof types resonate with your audience. To be safe, sprinkle in a variety, and double down on whichever type lands. "Proof does the job of getting the prospect to accept our claim—that's it." Treat people as humans, not robots: weave emotion into one proof point and share a data chart in the next.

## Argue like Aristotle: ethos, pathos, logos

- **Ethos** (memory hook: "ethic," as in "Am I an ethical person?") is the character of the person making the argument. It establishes trust and shows that you or your sources are reliable and authoritative. It comes through expert opinions or authoritative figures, testimonials from people your product has helped, data from research studies supporting the claim, and third-party reviews.
- **Pathos** (hook: "pathology") is the appeal to emotion, positive and negative. Analytical minds undervalue it and even resent it, a common mistake among smart people. But emotion drives decisions and motivation: excitement about a goal is what makes you pursue it. Use stories that evoke empathy, joy or other emotions, imagery that symbolizes deeper sentiments or values, and vivid words that stoke the prospect's desires. Using pathos respects the prospect as a human.
- **Logos** (hook: "logic") succeeds when the prospect nods and says "That makes sense." Explain the mechanics of how your product or service works, map a clear path from problem to problem solved, and demonstrate clearly that the value exceeds the cost. Logos matters most when you must win over a team, as in B2B, though the decision to write the check often comes down to one person whose emotions still count.

The three work together in one blended message that is memorable, persuasive and empowering.

## The content recipe

1. **Nail down what to say (the claim).** It should instill a predetermined belief required for the purchase.
2. **Support the claim with proof.** Mix and match ethos, pathos and logos.
3. **Create the content.** Flood it with claims, proof, sights, sounds and feelings to make a compelling case for your recommended way of doing things.

Fill in the inputs before writing: **Belief, Claim, Proof (and its appeal), Format, Marketing Channel, Call-to-action.** "The thinking behind the post does 90% of the heavy lifting"; once the inputs are set, the writing is much easier and consistency follows. You are not your prospect: your topic is on your mind all day, while they have a thousand other problems. Don't take even a belief they half-hold for granted.

### Worked examples (Building a Second Brain)

| | LinkedIn post | Email | Instagram post |
|---|---|---|---|
| Belief | Information overwhelm is holding me back. (a problem belief) | A Second Brain can benefit my career. (a solution belief) | A notebook is a secret weapon of top creatives. |
| Claim | Information overwhelm is bad and only getting worse. | When you have a tight deadline, a Second Brain can save you. | Top thinkers and creatives relied on notebooks throughout history. |
| Proof | Nielsen data: Americans spend over twelve hours a day consuming media (logos) | Tiago's personal story (pathos) | Leonardo da Vinci, with 13,000+ pages of notebooks (ethos) |
| Format / channel | Text + image / LinkedIn (professionals, B2B, targeting by profession) | Text / email | Text + public-domain da Vinci image / Instagram (visuals-first) |
| Call to action | Leave a comment about how you deal with information overload | Enroll in the course | Enter your email for a free PDF of da Vinci's note-taking techniques |

How each one works:
- **LinkedIn post.** It opens with the striking statistic, then makes the reader audit their own return on all that consumption: what do they have to show for the last book or course? It asks for exactly one step, admitting the problem ("The very first step in making any change is realizing you have a problem").
- **Email.** The story: Toyota asked Tiago to run a full-day workshop for fourteen executives on three days' notice, just as his course launch was wearing him out. With no time for new research, he spent thirty minutes searching his Second Brain, found years of examples, research, case studies, diagrams and slides, and said yes with confidence. The close asks "When the next opportunity comes along, will you be prepared?" Read your own drafts on two tracks: as a subscriber, and as a student of Belief Building checking that the proof backs the claim.
- **Instagram post.** Downstream tactics come after the message: popular hashtags, turning the post into a reel, a comment-to-win prize. They "pour fuel on my fire."

## Free content: hard teaching vs Belief Building

There are three ways to gain customers: (1) drag them in kicking and screaming with heavy-handed persuasion and manipulation, (2) crank out endless random content and hope generosity makes them buy, or (3) give the customer agency by making a strong case for your recommended path and letting them make the best decision for them. The book's approach is the third.

- **Implementation details** (Tiago's term) are downstream specifics, like which email template to use or whether to build the site in Wix or WordPress. Don't burden prospects with them. "Customers first need the education to even find the classroom." They need to know whether they're even on the right path.
- **Hard teaching** (Billy's term) is the mirror of hard selling: "overwhelming their followers with so much 'value' they don't have time to even consider the product on offer." It looks like endless tutorials, ultimate guides, walk-throughs, PDF reports and live workshops, all the best content given away in the hope that people buy. What prospects actually need is your product: the structure, the accountability and the skin in the game, not another 5,000-word blog post. Hard teaching delays the sale and attracts freebie-seekers and tire-kickers. Mountains of free content can actually hurt you.
- Good free content makes prospects more empowered buyers. It meets them at their current beliefs and takes them to the next step, which eventually leads to an offer. When they ask the questions they think are right, push back and get them asking the right ones.
- Belief Building content often challenges basic assumptions. When it hits a missing or incorrect belief, the audience feels an unmistakable jolt, and it shows up as comments, messages and sales.

## Testing beliefs with content

Treat every post or email as "a ping, a little test" of a belief. What do people think about this? What are the existing beliefs around it? What conversation is happening around this belief? Crickets on a post you expected to take off means people don't care about that belief; a surprise hit means you struck a nerve. You can test a new belief every day on any channel, which accelerates your learning. This is studying your buyer's psychology "like an anthropologist" and building a system for speaking to it.

## Sell every click

Tell people the "why," not just the "what." "Hi there, I created a new module" gives no one a reason to click. Sell every click, even to existing customers, or they won't click and will eventually forget you. Most offers urgently need answers to three questions:
- Why should your customers care?
- How are you different?
- Why should they trust you?
