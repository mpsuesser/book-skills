# Step 2: Identify the beliefs required for the purchase to be obvious

## Shared reality: the apple on the table

"Meet your prospect where they are" means establishing a **shared reality**. Put one apple on the table between you and your prospect; you both agree it is there. That undeniable truth is the starting point. If one person insists there are two apples, the conversation has nowhere to go. Just as you wouldn't open a logical argument with a premise your audience disputes, don't open your marketing from disagreement. "Start where agreement is easy, then build your case, introducing new beliefs and reinforcing them until the value of your offering becomes self-evident." Lead prospects from simple, undeniable agreements to the conclusion that your product is "not just a good choice—but the inevitable one," reached through their own logic and reasoning.

## Anchor beliefs to the customer journey

Listing beliefs off the top of your head (this one, and this, and this) hits a wall. The road map is the customer journey. Like a romantic relationship, it passes through considerations and milestones, and rushing from first awareness to the final decision rarely works. Most offers are not impulse buys, so plan a journey that unfolds over a week or a year.

- **Awareness.** The customer realizes there is a problem and is unhappy with the status quo. They want acknowledgment that you understand it, so aim for "Wow, this business really gets me." They can't fully articulate the problem yet. Give them the words to describe it and you also earn "Maybe this person can help me."
- **Consideration.** The customer is exploring solutions, some direct and some outside the box. Your beliefs position your product as a viable solution ("We can help you, too") and invalidate the other options: "We want there to be just one option: you." The Ladder of Importance is most useful here.
- **Decision.** Cash changes hands and marketing turns into selling. If the marketing worked, you just open the door and the prospect steps through. Smart people often excel at marketing and then stumble at the sale: "Don't falter at the finish line!" Reinforce belief in your trustworthiness and assure them they're making the right decision.

Splitting the focusing question across three stages adds constraints, which turns marketing into a puzzle your analytical mind can solve.

### The stage questions

Ask the focusing question once for each stage ("What does my customer need to believe in the Awareness stage?"), then three ways within it, each of the form "What do they need to believe about …?". That makes nine questions:

| Stage | What they need to believe about |
|---|---|
| **Awareness** | their problem; the prevalence of their problem; what happens if the problem doesn't get solved |
| **Consideration** | their different options; "my option"; the risks associated with inaction |
| **Decision** | me; my product; what happens after purchase |

Set aside time for these. Bring in business partners, employees, colleagues and collaborators; the more perspectives, the better. Dig for the beliefs beneath the surface.

### Objections and blind spots

Whenever you recommend your option there will be objections, even from excited prospects: "Will this work for me?" "What if I'm a beginner?" "What if I don't live in the United States?" For each one: (1) **recognize the objection**, then (2) **overcome the objection**. Closeness to your product creates blind spots, which is why other people belong in this exercise. Tiago's academics, who thought his note-taking system promoted plagiarism, held a Consideration-stage belief about "my option." Behind the objection were valid beliefs (academic integrity matters, citing sources is important, honesty is essential to creating knowledge). He agreed with them, added content supporting them to his marketing and course, and showed that a Second Brain fights plagiarism by keeping track of sources. The academics became loyal advocates. Most business owners ignore an objection and hope it goes away; face it head-on and flip it.

### Example: required beliefs for Building a Second Brain

- My brain is for having ideas, not holding them.
- I am already creative and do creative work.
- My thoughts and ideas are valuable and worth saving and revisiting.
- I am already doing most of the work required.
- I can get and stay organized quickly and easily.
- If I don't make an intentional effort to preserve my thinking, it will be lost.
- (Decision and delivery) Even if I miss a live call, I can still get value from the program.

If any one is missing, the offer doesn't make sense to the prospect, or it stays a "nice-to-have." The live-call belief matters because without it some people skip the course over a scheduling conflict, tripping "over a tiny pebble" just short of the starting line. The customer journey begins long before the purchase and runs through delivery, and it needs beliefs all along the way.

## The Ladder of Importance

**Success factors** are the actions, attitudes and behaviors that actually made a difference to success in your field. Rank them from most to least important and you have a ladder, the **Ladder of Importance**. Your job is to make sure the prospect's ladder matches yours, and you do it through marketing content. Often that means dethroning a belief from the top rung.

**Photography** (the audience believed camera quality was the top factor):
1. An eye for composition
2. Working with light
3. Storytelling through images
4. Post-processing techniques
5. Quality of the camera

A beginner who puts the camera on the top rung won't be receptive to a course called *How to Develop an Eye for Composition*. A photography teacher should teach what's important before teaching implementation details.

**Brewing beer** (beginners asked about yeast strains but weren't sanitizing):
1. Cleaning and sanitation
2. Fresh ingredients
3. Temperature control
4. The recipe
5. The yeast's region of origin

Phrase content as an argument for the top rung: "Cleaning and sanitizing is more important." The ladder combats the curse of knowledge by surfacing what you take for granted. "Too many smart people are out there teaching detailed tutorials on extremely niche questions instead of teaching what's fundamentally important." An answer to "What's important?" is what customers really want from you; implementation details come later.

## The changing landscape of beliefs

The core messaging document is a living, breathing document. Revisit the required beliefs whenever a new competitor gains traction, a popular new product captures your audience's attention, or a technological breakthrough hits your field. When AI took off in 2023 it became a new Consideration-stage option. Businesses didn't all need to adopt it, but each needed to tell customers how AI affected them, if at all. Ignoring it made them look out of touch while competitors talked about it. Competitors come and go, desires change, trends emerge and your own products change. Keep clearing false beliefs and instilling new ones.
