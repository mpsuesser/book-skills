# Steps A and B: symptom identification, discovery and integration

Discovery is defined by its experience, not its technique: any experiential method (parts work, chair work, Focusing, dream work, bilateral stimulation, inner child work, sentence completion, imaginal interactions and enactments) can follow the links from the manifest symptom to its constructs, and different techniques reach the same material. Discovery must be experiential, never "talking about": a **discovery experience** is the client's conscious encounter with previously implicit meanings and knowings that generate the symptom. Speculation and interpretation are not discovery. The client is guided to "bump into" constructs while speaking *from* and *in* present-moment live experience.

## Step A

- Quote the symptom in the client's own words. Richard's self-talk: "Who am I to think I know what's right?" "This could be wrong." "Watch out—don't go out on a limb."
- Ask when it is most likely: "When are you most likely to lose control of your body?"
- A broad pattern (lack of voice, lack of self) is made coherent and specific, then pursued thematically across contexts.
- An emerging remark can redefine the symptom. "I can't change my life" turned out to mean "I *mustn't* change."
- Step A can include symptoms that surface over sessions, or are identified only by their disappearance ("not being hunted").

## Discovery techniques

- **Symptom deprivation.** The client imagines being in the symptom situation *without* the symptom: confident in the meeting, without the guilt, sitting attentively in class, a drinking day with no bourbon, losing all the weight. Frame it: "This is not a rehearsal for actually doing that at work; it's just an exercise to do here with me so we can find out some important things." It brings up the dilemma or distress the symptom normally avoids. Then have the client let themselves feel the new discomfort and see if any words come. Variants:
  - *Situated in the relationship*: imagine a year of good work and a raise, then telling Dad directly (Ted).
  - *With Focusing*, when plain deprivation yields nothing: ask what the client would feel "in the middle of your body" without the drink, then "if that knot could say something…" what it would say (Cliff).
  - *Actual vs probably*: when the client predicts positive outcomes, ask "is that the *actual* experience you're having… or is that just how good you think it would *probably* be?" The smile drops and the uncomfortable feeling appears (Debbie).
  - *Resistance to the deprivation itself* is a point of contact with the pro-symptom position: focus on the sensation of resistance without trying to overcome it (Emma).
  - Deprive the client of each newly found symptom in turn: the perfectionism, the anger, "I can walk."
- **Sentence completion**, a projective technique adapted as a basic discovery tool. The therapist offers a stem that assumes symptom coherence and the client completes it spontaneously, without pre-thinking, repeating the stem for several endings: "If I let this end—"; "If you think I'm doing well—"; "The way I can make Dad realize what a lousy father he's been is—"; "If I stop snacking and lose all that weight…"; "If I can follow some rules, that's good, because then—"; "If I can't take care of other people…". Keep the client going "even if you don't like the endings or don't even fully understand what they mean." When first endings are conventionally positive ("I wouldn't be to blame"), persist until the emotional truth comes. A stem spoken from *within* the resistance can break through it ("If I let myself really feel the tragedy of what has happened to you, then…").
- **Follow the next question down.** Ask what an emotion would say and to whom ("What would the anger say? Would it be directed toward anybody?"). Ask the completion of a fragment ("we're not prepared to do *what*?"). Ask "Who gives you that status?" and "What do you want him to feel about how he did as your dad?" Of each layer, the therapist asks inwardly: "And what's under *this*?"
- **Probe for earlier origins**: "Is there something *familiar*, in your life, about this jolting experience…? Anything of this kind from much earlier in life…?" and "Does that sound familiar?"
- **The earliest-defining question**: ask for the gut response to "the earliest thing that most defined you," insisting on the first thought even if it seems silly; it is never silly and always connects to the current stuckness (Ross).
- **The "extra quality" question.** When the client moves outward to external threat (tapping the brakes), join them there and find another pathway. Normalize the built-in vulnerability everyone shares, then ask what about it has "some *extra* quality, or intensity, or dauntingness."
- **Seek the client's idiosyncratic meaning of key words.** "Inadequate" meant not 100% in control, unable to protect his daughter whatever the circumstances. Emotion labels matter less than feeling the emotion and going beneath it: shame and guilt can hide under one word.
- **Serial accessing.** Between sets of bilateral stimulation (or any focusing method), ask a question that elicits more awareness; the client holds it during the set, then you explore what came. At the limit, embody what emerged in a new question or statement ("What would it mean if you really felt able to take care of yourself?" / "What would it mean if someone wanted to take care of you?"). Bilateral eye movements can bring unconscious themes into awareness when verbal techniques, Focusing and Gestalt body awareness fail.
- **Dreamwork and imagery.** Gestalt-type re-immersion in a dream with eyes closed; follow an intruding image at once (Brenda's dream led to the car scene at age 8).
- **Inner figures as portals.** Ask "her deeper mind to guide us toward the source"; ask each inner parent figure what was hurting or scaring them. Each figure is the client's own ego state merged with implicit knowledge of that parent.
- **Content of voices.** For hallucinated voices, ask what the voices' messages mean to the client; their attacks parallel the pro-symptom position.
- **Guided visualization at the age of origin**: "What's it like for you, there in your family, when you're scared or hurting over something?" Persist for minutes until the learning is spoken.
- **Revisit instances, and the moment just before.** Raoul's hair-trigger anger shared a theme: someone had departed from a previous agreement.

Whatever the technique, elicit what was suffered *and* what was learned from it: "What's it like for her to be alone?" / "And when she's not, what's her experience?" Surprise, or "which doesn't make logical sense to me," confirms discovery is engaged; slight disorientation marks contact with constructs foreign to the conscious world; "all of this is new to me" confirms nonconscious material. A trancelike gaze calls for "What's coming up?"

## The schema in depth

- **Schemas in words** sound like this: "Making a mistake means I'm worthless and unacceptable and deserve the shaming that will come, so I've got to do everything perfectly"; Mom's distress is my job, or I'm an unlovable failure; it's dangerous if too much goes well, because it attracts a major blow from the universe, so I must avoid being happy.
- **Directly vs indirectly required symptoms.** A directly required symptom is itself the tactic and has a function. An indirectly required one is a by-product of the tactic being carried out or disallowed, and is functionless.
- **Target constructs** may define the problem or the solution. Richard: problem constructs that confidence makes him like Dad and that people will hate him; solution = keep myself quiet (general strategy) by thinking "What do I know?" (concrete tactic). His master construct was black-or-white: *any degree of expressed confidence equals Dad*.
- **Happy-ending solutions.** One class of solutions is the *envisioned ideal, happy-ending outcome*: a fantasy expected to end the vulnerability, heal the injury or restore well-being (Ted: make Dad see his failure by staying a mess). At its core sits a tacit **construct of possibility**, formed in childhood (that the father *could* face his wrongdoing), easy to overlook. Once conscious, happy-ending solutions are readily disconfirmed by the client's existing knowledge. When one dissolves, the *problem* still exists: guide a new solution that meets it directly, often grieving.
- **Context-specific schemas.** "Expressing any anger makes me unlovable" is narrow; "All women are viciously competitive with each other and dangerous" spans every context with women. A schema is part of each relevant context's memory network.
- **Several schemas per symptom** are common. Charlotte's obsessive attachment rested on a merging schema plus a divorce schema; Jane's rule-voice on a connection schema plus a punishment schema.
- **Constructivist understanding** is a defining element: what we construe is what we learn, unaware that we construe, so the model seems the truth of the world. Nullification comes only when constructs meet a disconfirmation the emotional brain itself accepts; then they are recognized *as* constructs. Schemas are then seen as "a self-created mirage."
- **Multi-component whole.** Integration is complete only when every component has been felt, verbalized accurately while felt, and incorporated into everyday awareness, with the symptom recognized as the *solution* to a problem.

## Integration techniques

- **Overt statement.** The client "tries on" a first-person, present-tense assertion of the emotional truth that states explicitly how and why the symptom is necessary, says it to an imagined key person, and checks: "Does that fit or feel true for you, in your body?" Richard's: "Feeling *any* confidence means I'm arrogant, self-centered, and totally insensitive like Dad, and people will hate me for it, so I've got to *never* feel confident, ever." ("My body is *buzzing* with how true it feels.")
  - *Re-word collaboratively* until it is true: propose the closest words, the client repeats and changes them ("The first part fits"; "we don't need the 'shaming' part?" "Not where I'm at now.").
  - *Conscious resistance from values*: say "An important *part* of me wants to be you and doesn't want to give that up." Use an ease-in prefix: "I hate to admit this, but—". De-pathologize the purpose: "it's not at all that you *like* keeping your life a mess… you seem to have this powerful purpose…"
  - *Calibrate* for pace and tolerance: the full form has the client say the words; sometimes it is better for the client to listen to the therapist speak them.
  - The overt statement does double duty: it integrates, and it launches **mismatch detection** (see juxtaposition-and-verification.md).
  - Agreeing with the voices' or symptom's own message is an overt statement too ("Yes, I can walk, I'm just faking").
- **Limbic language** names what is at stake, the vulnerabilities and the measures urgently necessary. Everyday phrasing ("people won't like you if you're confident in what you say to them, so it's good to be careful") intellectualizes, minimizes and depersonalizes, and the accessing may become too weak for transformation. Soften the voice, slow the pace and allow silences; an ordinary voice signals non-attunement.
- **Coherence empathy** reflects back the client's own purpose and agency, making explicit only what has emerged. "How does it feel to really stand behind your own deep purpose for doubting yourself?"
- **Connecting down and connecting up.** After finding the emotional truth (down), take the "return trip back 'up'": the client imagines a recent occurrence of the symptom and adds the overt statement of its purpose ("And I'm telling myself that so that I'll keep quiet and won't… be hated as a domineering know-it-all, like Dad"). A cue to hold the sentence in relation to the surface behavior ("sometimes you hold back from painting because of the risk of hitting that point") links necessity and symptom. Clients are often unaware of the link even between components already conscious.
- **Index cards**, standard practice at the end of every session. Write the overt statement, ideally crafted together in session (the crafting is itself a repetition). Stay faithful to the client's words and add no interpretations. Instructions: "Read this once a day for a minute or two, and also whenever you're noticing any self-doubting, and just let it bring you back in touch with how true this feels… Don't try to analyze or overcome any part of it." Ted's: "Read it each day just to stay in touch… don't try to change anything, for now." Send by text or email for remote work. Cards matter because discoveries made in an altered state are easily forgotten. A card can name several items, such as a list of the client's **terms of attachment**. A card can be shared with a spouse as an intermediary. Clients who decline a card (a child: "This is our secret") can repeat the sentence in mind whenever the symptom occurs. If a client is distressed about between-session contact with the material, hold cards until intensity diminishes.
- **Between-session writing** can serve the same role: free writing on things the client said that relate to the core theme, with the therapist writing down only the client's responses.
- **Checking the card's effect.** Ask the deliberately nonspecific "Were you able to stay focused on what we put on that card?" The client's spontaneous reference to specifics marks integration ("any degree of confidence… does mean that, to me—which hasn't changed now that I know it").

Integration is mindfulness training on the specific pro-symptom schema: persistent awareness plus *unreserved acceptance* of it. It is not a paradoxical intervention; it requires the therapist's discipline to refrain from counteracting. A noncounteractive stance can include the therapist's voicing a speculative pro-symptom statement for the client to try ("…from down here at the bottom, I'm really on top and I rule") and "I certainly wouldn't want you to change something you weren't ready to change." Axiom: a person cannot move away from a position they do not yet knowingly occupy. Minimum depth for change: the client is in touch with their own purpose and agency in maintaining the symptom. A sudden laugh of recognition on revisiting a pro-symptom position often marks the onset of fundamental change. Disorientation after the first conscious immersion is common: the symptom reveals itself as the conscious portion of a core life dilemma, not a personal defect.

## Resistance to discovery and integration

**Resistance** is conscious or nonconscious non-cooperation with the current step, and can occur at any step. Temporarily regard it as the "symptom," with the same empathy, acceptance and assumption of cogency, and discover its emotional truth: the client expects that cooperating would cause a specific negative effect. Then find a reliable way to prevent that effect with resources the client already has, or recognize that the expectation rests on childhood conditions not possible in adulthood. The JE then often happens in seconds.
- *Resistance to discovery*: the client shifts focus, intellectualizes, downplays. A client who twice downplays a vulnerability as merely "possible" is signaling that owning it at full strength is too vulnerable now; respect it.
- *Resistance to integration* often comes from conventional notions of health ("really screwed up to deliberately keep myself so messed up"); reframe the purpose and ease in.
- Parts that protect parents: "This isn't about demonizing your parents at all. It's just about checking out the part that holds the distress."
- In a relational tug-of-war (help vs don't-help), counteracting lets the client "win" from below. Stay neutral, noninterpretive, "non-helping"; disclose the difficulty; find a point of play; be simply present ("Hey, I'm right here").

## Safety and pacing

- Signs of heading toward traumatic memory: the client becomes wooden, glassy-eyed or confused, or shifts focus away. Take **small enough steps**.
- Late in a session: "How will it be for you to be in touch with this after you leave my office today, and you don't have me accompanying you in it?" If difficulty looms: "I understand, and what I want to suggest is for you to forget all about these things between sessions, and only when we are together *during* sessions will we revisit them. How would that be, for you?" Clients offered this permission to re-suppress have all succeeded at it.
- Before a blunt card, check whether it would overwhelm.
- Sessions with suicidal ideation are devoted to safety.

## Reverse resolution

Once the purpose is conscious, the client may choose to *keep* the symptom as an adaptive response of value. A graduate student's "procrastination" was a refusal to follow a parent-chosen path; recognized as self-determination, it led to withdrawal from the program.

## Objective hardship

Real hardship cannot be dispelled, but there is nearly always an added schema that amplifies it (requesting help is shameful weakness, so I'm overburdened and alone), and that schema can be unlearned. "Psychotherapy cannot undo the very real hardships and sufferings inflicted by racism, but it can dispel the amplification of those hardships and sufferings by the individual's particular emotional learnings and schemas."
