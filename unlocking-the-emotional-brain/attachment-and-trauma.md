# Attachment, trauma, children and special populations

## Is it always about attachment?

The book takes a position between two camps. The attachment-centric camp holds that "all psychopathology constitutes primary or secondary disorders of bonding or attachment." The other treats attachment as one determinant among several: Kagan argued that social class predicts outcomes better than maternal sensitivity, and Minuchin that an attachment focus can make social and racial issues disappear. Minuchin and Schnarch warned that in reparenting the therapist can become *too* important, fostering dependency or codependency and failing differentiation. The authors' verdict: attachment is one of several domains; "all sides are correct some of the time."

- **Learning domains**: attachment, social (bullying, racist or ethnic oppression, betrayal by friends or colleagues, layoffs, war zones, gaining and losing popularity or fame), existential (illness or injury, disability, affluence to poverty, accidentally causing death, natural disasters, the sense of mortality), artistic, athletic and spiritual. Securely attached people form problematic schemas too. All the domains deposit schemas into the same emotional implicit memory, like "different rivers converging to one delta," and they intersect.
- **Find the domain from content, never from theory.** Symptom coherence finds the schema without knowing its home domain; the domain is learned from what is retrieved. Make no *a priori* assumptions (gender narratives, insecure attachment); learn from the client as "anthropologist of the individual, couple, or family." Raoul's hair-trigger anger came from a business partner's betrayal five years earlier, not his secure, honest family, so reparative attachment could not have worked.
- **Temperament and genes.** Kagan's high- vs low-reactive infants stay so into adolescence; genetic differential sensitivity shapes which experiences form schemas. Nullifying a schema nullifies that instance, not the predisposition.

## Attachment styles as coherent schemas

An attachment bond is emotionally significant, persistent, proximity-seeking, directed to a specific individual, a secure base, a source of "felt security," and accessible and responsive. The child forms mental models of the caregiver's contingencies and strategies to maintain proximity and prevent negative responses. Each insecure style is a fully adaptive solution in its context, "fully under the control of the person's emotional brain."
- **Insecure-avoidant** (adult: *dismissive*), with a consistently rejecting caregiver. Problem: expect rebuff for expressing distress or need, or for approaching. Solution: don't communicate, *don't feel* the need (dissociation), avoid contact. Adults downplay relational events, have few childhood memories, and give brief answers affirming normality.
- **Insecure-ambivalent** or **insecure-resistant** (adult: *preoccupied*), with an inept or self-preoccupied caregiver responsive only to intensity. Intermittent reinforcement makes this tenacious. The child must *make* responsiveness happen, which yields a controlling, relentless style and anxious hypervigilance toward the caregiver.
- **Insecure-disorganized** (adult: *unresolved*), with an unpredictable, extreme, punitive caregiver: "fear without solution." Disoriented freezing is a coherent but functionless expression of helplessness. The schema: Other does extremely hurtful, terrifying things unpredictably, and self is defenseless. Many such children develop a solution combining extreme codependency (compulsive pleasing, suppressing wants, role reversal) with invisibility (going unnoticed in plain sight); anxiety stays high because the solutions are unreliable.

Schemas with a problem but no solution, in any domain, generate hypervigilant high anxiety or panic, dissociation and numbness, or depression that is really hopelessness and despair.

## Terms of attachment

**Terms of attachment** are the person's learnings of how a primary-bond relationship works: which forms of connection are available, and which behaviors, thoughts and feelings are *required and forbidden* to participate. They are living knowledge of the conditionality of love, the rules for being acceptable and admitted into full connection, imposed by caregivers and/or construed. Examples: I must never like what Mom or Dad doesn't, or be humiliated; I must never think I matter or tell Mom what I feel; I get attention only if clever and funny; don't cry, or be slapped; no energy or strong feelings, or be screamed at; Dad attends only if I talk about what he likes; I'm acceptable only if exactly as Mom wants, and one wrong makes me totally unworthy. Each person's terms are unique, and two parents can produce two different insecure schemas.
- **Primary attachment learnings** are terms of attachment. **Secondary attachment learnings** were learned within the attachment relationship, often by observing the figure, but do not model how connection works (Richard's learning that confidence equals Dad and gets you hated).
- **Technique**: compile the client's terms of attachment as a list of succinct sentences and give it to them to re-read daily. This integrates them and sets up finding contradictory knowledge for each item.

## The decision path for reparative attachment

Reparative attachment assumes, after Bowlby, that only a secure dyadic relationship can revise insecure learnings. The book's contribution: contradictory knowledge for insecure attachment is not limited to secure attachment with the therapist; it follows from the schema's content, which opens an expanded palette. Ask, per schema:
1. Does the schema consist of learnings formed in an attachment relationship? (Probe early family.) No → unsuitable.
2. Does the core problem defined in the schema consist of terms of attachment? No → unsuitable.
3. Are the specific terms of attachment possible to disconfirm through the client's relational experience of the therapist, or any feasible client–therapist interaction? Yes → suitable; No → unsuitable.

- To rule out the therapist, consider each way the client could experience the therapist that could disconfirm the constructs or meet the need. Ted needed accountability from his *father*; the therapist's understanding could not supply it.
- Even when the path says yes (Regina: lateness or spilled coffee could show the therapist's regard unchanged), the therapist is free to use other methods, which may be equally or more effective.
- Secure attachment with the therapist disconfirms *only if* it contradicts the schema *and* occurs in juxtaposition with the insecure expectations. "the client's experience of secure attachment with the therapist does not in itself guarantee that such a juxtaposition experience is occurring." Clients can welcome empathy while blocking the JE, for instance to avoid the distress of contrasting the therapist's empathy with the parents' lack.
- Reparative attachment works most readily for adults living largely in a child identity or ego state, for whom the therapist is a plausible attachment figure. A client with a well-developed adult identity may say: "you're a professional practitioner… it *doesn't* fill those empty slots." Common factors (trust, empathy, alliance, shared goals) can be fully present without the client experiencing the therapist as an attachment figure, and secure attachment is not reparative unless it disconfirms via a JE.
- Other methods for insecure attachment include imaginal relatives, the Ideal Parent Figure methodology, overt statements driving mismatch detection, and the client's own lived experiences.
- The characteristic progression of Coherence Therapy attachment work: awareness of the adaptive function gives agency and choice; then experiments in choosing to matter, first with the therapist, then with selected others ("You've turned an involuntary muscle into a voluntary muscle"). Attachment schemas vary in contextual range (only romantic love; all authority figures; turning workplace relationships into friendships for safety), so create JEs in each relevant context, and reach the generalized "all people" constructs.

## Trauma

- **Pragmatic definition**: traumatic memory is involved if the client describes *three or more* post-traumatic symptoms: anxiety or panic attacks; anxious hypervigilance; emotional flatness; dissociation, numbness; sensory flashbacks; avoidance behaviors; interpersonal invisibility; intrusive images or scenes; finding it intolerable to sit still without *doing*; surges of wretched somatic sensations; a large gap in autobiographical memory; narrative reference to an extreme ordeal; lack of a coherent autobiographical narrative; situational triggering into an intense emotional or altered state; ongoing nightmares or a persistently repeating dream. Not all therapy is trauma therapy.
- **Two types of traumatic memory**: *episodic* (the sensory, somatic, emotional memory of the ordeal) and the less recognized *semantic* **post-traumatic schemas** (what was learned: patterns of danger and vulnerability as the problem, tactics as the solution). Post-traumatic symptoms are generated by these schemas at least as much as by suppression of the episodic memory. Prior therapy can resolve the traumatic memory and leave the specific learning (safety through obesity) intact.
- **Suppression is itself a solution.** Flatness, dissociation, avoidance and lack of coherent narrative exist as means of suppression and vanish when suppression is unnecessary. Gradual pace, continual transparent checking and persistent prompting let the client find they have de-suppressed a memory that seemed unsurvivable; this disconfirms the expectation that de-suppression means permanent engulfment in agony, and when the therapist notes it and the client acknowledges it, it is a major breakthrough, sometimes complete depotentiation. A common ungrieved-grief schema: the grief inside is oceanic, bigger than me, and would engulf me forever, so I must keep it suppressed; finding grief finite and workable disconfirms it.
- **Affective vs perceptual flashbacks.** Stage fright was an *affective flashback* to a car ride; the shared trigger: "once I'm on either one—the bridge or the stage—*I can't get off*."
- **Delayed onset**: schemas don't fade, and can be reactivated by an anniversary, concurrent stress, a strongly replicating encounter, or a strong need to act against the tactic; then the schema stays on duty. Watch anniversary timing for triggers.
- **Over-generalized schemas**: an all-or-nothing post-traumatic schema ("If someone tries to make me do what they want against my will, I'll be killed unless I respond with submissiveness!") persists only while outside awareness. Once conscious, its falseness becomes apparent to other knowledge systems: "only that one time with that one person."
- **Complex attachment trauma**: for each distinct form of repeated suffering there is a separate schema (Dad's rage and shaming for mistakes; being denied dinner and confined for soiled pants; molestation by a visiting uncle). Every symptom is a coherent expression of at least one schema; manifested symptoms are the points of departure. Severe cases hold over twenty schemas, which advance nonlinearly; keep session notes per schema, with specificity.
- **Counteractive-first trauma presentations**: PTSD with extreme characterological avoidance calls first for somatic relaxation, breath awareness and yoga, then gradually for adjunctive TRP in tolerable forms, such as empowered re-enactment of specific memories.

## Neurodivergent clients

They often carry a lifelong sense of being *different*, socially marginal and alone, and are deeply feelingful even with flat affect. The core belief of being fundamentally defective or inferior has three avenues of disconfirmation:
1. Immersive revisiting of key scenes of mistreatment, experientially reversing the attributed meaning from "I deserve this because there is something wrong with me" to "this is mistreatment and a failure to be kind and caring, and I don't deserve it."
2. The therapist authentically experiences the client as an inherently valid, full member of the human family. Being seen that way disconfirms; the client dares to drop cover-up and masking behaviors, first in session.
3. Psychoeducation on neurodiversity: radical self-acceptance of a "different kind of mind" with strengths, and discovery of experiences of *rightness of fit*. People thrive only under conditions suited to their constitution, so thriving with the right fit means no fundamental defectiveness.
Shame ceases as the defectiveness model drains. A second focus is the fear of venturing outside the small social and sensory bubble, which dispelling shame and finding right fits reduce.

## Dissociative identity

Alters hold different memories supporting the old learning and are amnestic for each other, so do MR with each alter unless they temporarily blend. Disconfirming experiences available to some alters are unavailable to others; more co-consciousness makes success likelier.

## Children and groups

- With children, swift, thorough change is the rule, because schemas are not yet compounded with later adaptations. Pattern: discover the schema → a simple overt statement for integrated awareness and ownership → maintain the focus between sessions → contradictory knowing emerges spontaneously in daily life, or is found together in the child's own past → guide an explicit JE. Knowing the original learning experiences is unnecessary to reveal and unlearn the expectation.
- Respect a child's boundaries (declining a card as "our secret") as support for differentiation.
- Children believe "it's my fault" regardless of adult assurances. Play, puppets and a group's own symbolic characters (a "Divorce Dragon") can voice the schema; a therapist's puppet can question it and supply disconfirming fact. Adults often carry "It's my fault that my parents divorced" or "My existence destroys love" for decades.
- A teen's disruptive, unfocused behavior can be a solution to invisibility ("People only notice me when I misbehave"). Here the relationship itself was the reality-changing JE: extended, quality attention for simply being himself.

## Racism, homophobia and cross-cultural work

The phenomenological method lets a client feel respected on their own terms, across race, culture, sexual orientation and age. In an interracial session the therapist raises the priority of staying attuned to nonverbal signs of limits and holds maximum mindfulness of possible residual blindness from privilege, keeping the focus only on what directly maintains the presenting pattern. Personal learnings (a stepmother's "you have nothing important to say") can amplify vulnerability to racist devaluing, and that amplification is what therapy can dispel. Intergenerational transmission (a homophobic grandfather behind a mother's rejection) can be mapped in the client's own words and used for repair conversations rehearsed with role reversal. The outside climate remains to be navigated.
