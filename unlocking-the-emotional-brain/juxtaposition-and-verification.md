# Steps C, 1–2–3 and V: contradictory knowledge, juxtaposition, verification

## Where contradictory knowledge comes from

Step C finds "a unit of living knowledge that is personally known by the individual to be absolutely true and that strongly contradicts a major reality-defining construct." It comes from the client's existing store of knowledge or from new learning experiences, in daily life between sessions or guided in session. Sources include perceptions of the world, the client's own behavior, and new construals of meaning. The paradox of why clients already hold it: the schema lives in implicit emotional memory and the contradictory knowledge in conscious memory as taken-for-granted background, and the two have never met. Develop an "antenna" for spotting contradictory knowledge in what the client says.

**Kinds of contradictory knowledge** (from the book's case tables):
- *Opposite current experience* of the very thing the problem construct predicts (others were comfortable with a colleague's confidence).
- *The solution's unacceptable costs* ("I'm much better off by merging" vs "I'm much, much worse off by merging").
- *The solution's impossibility* (Dad is not capable of accountability or apology).
- *The client's own powers* of assertion and self-protection, created by new experience.
- *Another person's love that doesn't depend on the condition* (Uncle Theo's love doesn't change with imperfections).
- *Fault located in the other* (Mom rejected me because of trouble inside *her*).
- *Past vs present* (this situation is only *reminding* me of what happened back then).
- *The person is not the same as the model* ("my sexuality is *not* the same as my mother's").
- *The generalization is false* (it was my parents, not all people, who gave no caring understanding; it was that one man, that one time).

## Step C techniques

- **Opposite current experience.** Once the schema is retrieved and unsealed, the client starts noticing daily experiences that contradict it, which were invisible while the schema was compartmentalized. The tell is a report of violated expectation: "It was weird, how differently it went from how I expected."
- **Mismatch detection.** Retrieving the schema into explicit awareness hands new information to the conscious networks, which vet it against the client's library of personal knowledge; the brain detects inconsistencies by itself. So the overt statement and its card often surface contradictory knowledge spontaneously, as when a card read during the week brings "no boundaries equals death." It succeeds in a sizable fraction of cases, not all; when it misses, use other methods.
- **Overt statement as magnet.** Have the client say a sentence affirming the hidden construct, "even if it's mechanical at first": "My father is a man who's willing to recognize his own big mistake." Adult knowledge rushes in ("what could I be thinking? He never does any of that with anybody… What a joke."), with no counteractive message from the therapist. Distress at this moment marks that disconfirmation is experiential. Use it especially on a construct of possibility.
- **Making assumptions explicit**, then contradicting them with fact that attains subjective realness. To children sure the divorce is their fault: "do you think that people without kids ever get divorced?" ("Of course they don't") then "Well, actually, *lots* of people without kids *do* get divorced." Reassurance alone doesn't land; children usually don't buy it.
- **Empowered re-enactment.** The emotional brain responds to imaginal experiences as real. Re-enter the traumatic scene and coach the client into the assertive, self-protective response ("I'm going to help you through this situation in a whole new way"): supply lines to say, push ("*Make* him stop"; "Is he taking you seriously yet?"), add allies and authorities. Whether the child could really have done this is irrelevant; what matters is juxtaposing the persisting helplessness with a present experience of power. The voice goes from stiff to emotionally real. Use it *only* where the original situation gave early signs of danger that allowed a sooner, more assertive response; where no response was possible (a bomb exploding) it retraumatizes, so use other techniques.
- **Imaginal relative.** Find a person whose love was real (Uncle Theo). Evoke that warmth in the heart area, then have the client tell that person the overt statement of the emotional truth ("But you love me only because I seem perfect…"), and see their hurt at "a deep insult to his love." Leave a sentence incomplete for the client to finish ("He knows that his love is—"). Then build the JE by widening the picture to include the rejecting parent: "both kinds of love at once." Extend to every other person who would be hurt to learn their fondness was taken as conditional.
- **I'm in memory** (when a traumatic memory is reactivated; makes it subside fast instead of persisting for hours or days). First give psychoeducation: the present resembles the past, and the emotional brain mixes the two. Then: palm on the heart, as if comforting a terrified child, and say softly aloud, twice: "This situation is *reminding* me of what I suffered *back then*…"; "This raw fear I'm feeling is my *living memory* of what I felt and suffered back then."; and, with gentle patting, "It was so awful; it was so horrible for me—and this situation is reminding me of it." Give it on a card for between sessions. Each session, ask the client to say more about the living memory evoked, so immersive recall deepens over sessions with empathic accompaniment and a coherent narrative forms: "Nothing is invented in that process, only discovered." It works only after prior deep work has made it *experientially* clear that the source is past; merely explaining reassuringly that the ordeals are over is cognitive insight, counteractive and weak.

**Table 5.1, the JEs that I'm in memory creates (old knowing → new knowing)**
1. "Something extremely harmful and terrifying [or hopeless, devastating, etc.] is happening to me right now." → What I'm feeling is my living memory of what I suffered back then; the present only reminds me of it; it isn't happening again now.
2. "These extreme feelings and thoughts mean I'm crazy [or defective, dysfunctional, irrational, etc.]." → They are parts of my living memory of what I actually suffered back then.
3. Facing what I suffered would undo me; the memory is bigger than I am and would engulf me forever, so I must live disconnected. → I'm not engulfed by letting the memory into awareness; I'm larger than it; it is safe to fully inhabit my body, feelings and knowledge.

- **Present-moment connection for an inner child.** Young parts stay in the spell of the original learning and do not register later disconfirming experience. Prompt the inner child to notice what is present now: "it sounds like she's got that at the moment with you. She's got that heart-connection." Surprise ("Oh, that surprises her!"; "That's a mind-blower") both confirms the target schema and disconfirms it. Present-danger checks work the same way: "Is there any danger that's actually present right now?"; invite the young part to open her eyes and feel the room for danger.
- **The relationship as the contradiction**, when the schema's content makes the therapist's way of being a specific disconfirmation (see attachment-and-trauma.md for the decision path). A non-counteracting, accepting presence offered exactly when the client is in touch with the need not to conform disconfirmed the conviction that the only way to maintain power was to wield it from a position of enfeeblement and withdrawal.
- **Meditative states** do not as a rule dispel unresolved disturbances (*spiritual bypass*). But when features of a meditative experience specifically disconfirm features of a particular schema, and are used in a JE, true resolution can result. Facilitation must be "clean": zero inducing of special intimacy or fascination with psychic experiences. Ground existential hazards in the lower body (pelvic floor, legs) for a fully relevant disconfirmation.
- **Psychedelic sessions** give lucid access to deeply felt knowings with self-evident higher-order truth (see other-therapies.md).
- **Reframe a safety criterion** that keeps a JE from fully landing, by careful questioning: "In order to be attuned to danger, do we need to be sensitive?" then "Do you feel like this experience of fundamental consciousness is making you more sensitive or less sensitive?"

Whether contradictory material can be found without special methods depends more on the therapist's experience, repertoire of Step C techniques and ingenuity than on any decision tree.

## Running the juxtaposition (1–2–3)

- **When contradictory knowledge emerges**, first elicit it more completely if needed: "I could see from your whole manner that you know this in a way that is very real to you, not just as some idea… Can you tell me some more of this personal knowledge…?" Then guide explicit repetitions.
- **Step 1**: deliberately re-evoke the key target construct, with a softened voice: "Would you be willing to conjure up that scene at work in your mind's eye…" ("expressing any confidence makes you resemble Dad").
- **Step 2**: bring the contradiction into the same moment: "Can you feel that moment, to some degree?" A lower, slower voice shows immersion.
- **Step 3**: state the old knowing alongside the new, with empathy for both: "one part of you… knows… And at the same time, another part of you has a very different knowing… these two realities… each feels so real and true to you." "How is it to be feeling both of them together, like this?" Check the framing: "Does it feel true to describe it like that? Your old knowing right alongside this other new knowing that's so different?" Allow 20–30-second silences. Repeat about four times, then: "keep seeing both… when you open your eyes."
- **Explicit framing when the client may lose the thread**: "All we're doing is holding both experiences in awareness at the same time…" "Marinate… without trying to make anything happen."
- **Present-moment JE with the therapist**: "And how is it for you right now to be so in touch with that—your certainty that you will *never get* caring understanding for what hurts… and at the same time, recognizing that you are actually getting that kind of emotional understanding from me, right now?" When the client narrates something flatly, ask about both sides: "I don't know if you're expecting I might be like your parents… or if you're remembering that I'm different from them… Could you tell me about both sides of that?"
- **Juxtaposition cards** hold both sides: "I really want merging because… and at the same time, it's so clear to me how lost, invisible, and miserable the grown-up me actually feels in that kind of connection." A **two-sided card** carries a stem on the front ("If you do things right but bad things still happen, then…") and the construct on the back ("If you do things right, bad things don't happen") to elicit disconfirming examples. Have the client read the card before and during the triggering context ("The aim is only to stay in touch with what's on this card while you're at work, *not* to try to say anything confidently or do anything differently"), or keep it in front of them during the call with the parent.
- **Real-time JE homework**: each time the trigger approaches, think both sides ("Mom, now you're going out, and I always thought you'll die, but now I know that you will come back and live…"). Rehearse it aloud first.
- **Extend across contexts.** Apply the JE to the boss, the partner, each relative, each work setting. In complex trauma, where the same relational trauma repeated across life stages, expect an extended, nonlinear process that creates essentially the same JE in a range of contexts; on slippage, return to the origin scene ("of course, it sometimes still feels safer…") and track empowerment vs slippage each session.
- **Unpack a release.** "What just happened inside, what just now became clear to you?" or "What's making that difference?" Voicing it maximizes definiteness and confirms that the TRP happened.
- **The JE's signature**: *experiential dissonance*, an enriched extension of cognitive dissonance. The client shows a unique confusion about previously unquestioned reality ("beginning to get a little confused"; "trying to snap into coherence… total surprise… total contradiction"), awe ("Whoa, this actually exists?"), amazement, or an uncomfortable question ("If it's possible for that to happen, then why did I get parents who couldn't do it?") as grieving opens.

## Telling a JE from what resembles one

- **Positive thinking vs JE.** Positive thinking removes attention from the negative belief; a JE holds both knowings in one field. Ask what is making the difference: if the answer is lived evidence ("I've done portraits and I was able to get through"), it is contradictory knowing.
- **Competing preferences are not contradictory models.** Two conflicting *solutions* (block feelings and soldier on vs keep hoping for love) are different preferences, not mutually incompatible *models of reality*, so experiencing both is no JE.
- **A good outcome is not contradictory knowledge.** "I got away" reinforced a survival tactic rather than contradicting it: the schema formed before the outcome was known, and escape proved it worked.
- **A positive experience is not a disconfirmation.** "lasting therapeutic change occurs not merely by having strongly 'positive' experiences, but by having experiences that specifically disconfirm and nullify the mental models maintaining symptoms." The therapist's acceptance and empathy are not directly curative; their power lies in how they *specifically disconfirm* the client's models, and only when the therapist orchestrates a definite JE.
- **Lucky counteracting.** An offhand counteractive remark can trigger a real shift because it happened to contradict a schema; it can mislead the therapist into believing counteracting generally works. Use hindsight inquiry (see other-therapies.md) to find the schema it disconfirmed.

## Resistance to transformation

When well-guided JEs fail, disconfirmation is being blocked to protect against intense disorientation, loss, pain, fear or hardship expected if the model were no longer true. The most frequent: a schema construing the parents' cold, harsh treatment as one's own fault; disconfirming it means knowing it was *not* deserved, which means recognizing the abuse. Treat the resistance as a new coherent symptom: discover the sufferings it expects, transform those, then repeat the JE. Usually a few sessions.
- Ted: "I just want to fight it! It's fucking outrageous!" The therapist invited an overt statement *of* the resistance to Dad ("I *refuse to accept* that you can't give me…"), which keeps the contradictory knowing at the center and the client in the JE even while resisting. A grief stem followed: "If I really get it that my father will *never*…—". Typical themes: aloneness, grief, injustice, self-responsibility, fear of going forward without the parent behind. Make each conscious; some dissolve, others are accepted as life's uncertainties.
- Jane's "can't just sit in this heart-connection" was traced with "Which would do what?" to a second schema of punishment ("Children are seen and not heard"). Both had to be unlearned; after empathic understanding of both expected sufferings, return to the JE.
- When current experience seems to reconfirm the schema, decisive disconfirmation may need more precisely specific disconfirmation of each component construct. Jane's adoption schema yielded only to "I will never return you," said three times to the child part ("Wow. That lands.").

## Verification (Step V)

- **Probe in session**: "What's it like to see both and feel both now?" Then, with a lowered voice, deliberately re-apply former cues ("what about the danger of coming across as a know-it-all, like Dad, and being hated for that?"). Imaginally revisit the trigger and see what parts come up. Have the client re-say the old card sentences: little or no resonance means non-reactivation.
- **Probe in life**: actual re-exposure between sessions, and follow-up at months and years. Unlearning is established only when the pro-symptom position consistently no longer activates in former contexts. In Coherence Therapy, transformational change is not achieved until levels stay at zero effortlessly; a drop from 10+ to 1–3 with peaks means a schema is not fully unlearned. Ask which schema the remaining distress belongs to.
- **Persistent follow-up is a failsafe**: it reveals schemas that discovery missed.
- **Optimal markers**: the schema is too foreign to remember clearly ("Did I think that? I don't think that anymore!"; "just a memory now… the charge has been taken out"); puzzlement at the question because the answer is now obvious; "what was my *problem*?" These come with the schema's "withered lifelessness," effortlessness, curiosity, spontaneous compassion for the parent while still holding them accountable, and somatic signs (deep breath, relaxation, a lighter chest).
- **The therapist's named error**: assuming the pro-symptom position has dissolved because the symptom disappeared, without checking that the client has experienced their agency and purpose in creating it. Emma's voices returned four months later; the therapist had "neglected to cement the final block."
- **No maintenance practice.** A truly nullified schema stays nullified without practice; that is what transformational change means. A later upset (such as a gap in connection) brings a fitting feeling, not a relapse, and drives new learning.
- After the model is gone, skills methods that were counteractive (Kids'Skills, solution-focused work) become highly effective for building new skills, because they no longer compete with a schema.
