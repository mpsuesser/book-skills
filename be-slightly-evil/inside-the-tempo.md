# Inside the Tempo

The capstone model that pulls the playbook together: how competitive games are created out of VUCA, played through an opening, a mid-game and an end-game, and won by getting inside an adversary's tempo. Reach here for a sustained rivalry or competition (between people, teams, companies or products), deciding whether to move first or fast-follow, breaking a stalemate with an entrenched incumbent, deciding when to cash out or cede fronts, managing loyalties and alliances, or setting your own rules of engagement.

## Inside the tempo

Being *inside the tempo* of an opponent is often called being *inside the decision-cycle*, after Boyd's OODA (observe-orient-decide-act) loop. Rao prefers *tempo* because it drops the structural idea of a "cycle" and reaches past the rhythms of an adversarial decision process to its emotions and energy flows. In business it corresponds to disruption in Clayton Christensen's sense; in chess and martial arts it means playing your opponent rather than the game (Boyd: "fight the enemy, not the terrain"). It applies to cooperation too (an attentive parent or teacher can be inside a child's tempo), but adversarial contexts bring out its richness.

"Being inside an adversary's tempo is all about *wrangling luck to exploit habits.*"

## Wrangling luck

Life differs from artificial games in that luck is a variable you can influence far more powerfully, because you can change the rules open-endedly instead of playing inside a closed rule set with a pair of dice. "The open game of life, to a large extent, is about wrangling luck by playing with rules."

- An **adversary** is "anyone who is trying to create better-than-random conditions for themselves by creating worse-than-random conditions for you, via the meta-game of rule-making and rule-breaking." To compete you must do the same where necessary.
- Getting inside an adversary's tempo means recognizing and exploiting the ways they (or it, for organizations) are a *creature of habit*. Everyone is, to some extent, because attention is limited. Winning is exploiting your adversary's habits before they exploit yours.

**Competitive climates.** Luck is the scarce resource, because there are more ways for things to go wrong than right. In open competitive environments it is not just die rolls and card shuffles but a control variable, "*The* control variable". Wrangling it creates a competitive climate of two patterns:

- **Serendipity**: winning feels like things miraculously going your way and falling into place, unreasonably out of proportion to your actions.
- **Zemblanity**: losing feels like an overpowering sense of doom and snowballing misfortunes, out of proportion to your sins and stupidity.

The most general goal in competitive behavior is to create patterns of serendipity and zemblanity that favor you: drive serendipity towards yourself and zemblanity towards your adversaries. Direct more luck your way and more misfortune theirs, and you amplify the effectiveness of everything else you do.

## Creating a real-world game: people before process

The first order of business, once you decide to wrangle luck in a domain, is to *create a real-world game*. There are two setup moves, *people* moves and *process* moves, and the tenet is **people before process**.

**People setup: the pattern of conflict.** Create a configuration of alliances and oppositions. In chess two people simply sit down; in pickup soccer captains pick sides in minutes. In real-life adversarial environments, deciding when to cooperate and when to compete is non-trivial, highly situation-dependent, and can be harder than the play itself. Get the configuration right and the game may be over before it begins once the configuration is signaled, since weaker alliances often concede walkovers to invincible opposition. Before making any opening move, calibrate the default level of competitiveness or cooperativeness you bring. Being combative when it is time to cooperate, or brokering peace when competitive energy is needed, backfires: "if you want peace, prepare for war, if you want war, prepare for peace."

**The three laws of Slightly Evil loyalty**, for being neither childlike nor *Game of Thrones* about loyalty:

1. **Adult-adult or no deal** (in place of *win-win or no-deal*): don't ask for or offer absolute loyalties. Broken promises are inevitable under VUCA; people who trade in childlike absolute loyalty aren't worth dealing with unless they are actually children. Among adults, "loyalty is not a value you have to apply but a budget you have to manage." You will disappoint people throughout your life (if rarely as starkly as Batman choosing between his girlfriend and an idealistic politician), and managing how you spread the disappointment is managing your **loyalty budget**.
2. **Contingent but sincere**: don't play loyalty games. This is the line between slightly evil and true evil. Loyalty can be a game variable (win confidence in order to betray it; act adversarial to gain attention and then an alliance), but most of us aren't living a spy-versus-spy life. People forgive broken promises after a sincere apology, but not bad-faith promises, and faking good faith consistently is more trouble than it is worth.
3. **Never be your own #1**: it is easier to Be Slightly Evil on behalf of others. Imagine severe but not absurd stress (losing half your savings and having to choose which of two children to send to college). List the people in your life in decreasing order of the severity of situation it would take to make you betray them: a child near the top, a random nice-seeming waiter at the bottom. Now place yourself: what stress would make you break promises to yourself? Most people find at least one person ranks above them. "It is far easier to Be Slightly Evil on behalf of someone else."

**Process setup: rules of engagement**, "unilateral agreements with yourself that limit how you act."

- Their ethical function (how you enter the fray) is important but relatively minor.
- More important, they *manage risk*, limiting the damage when things go wrong.
- Their primary purpose is *directing luck towards yourself*: stacking the odds in your favor before play begins by deploying the advantages you already have. Few artificial competitions allow this (in the America's Cup the winners define the next competition's rules). In artificial games, rules of engagement are mostly about picking the right game, like a poker player choosing a table.
- Real life is about *creating* games: drawing your own boundaries and choosing which rules to bend or break and which to try to enforce. Your pattern of conflict has already drawn a boundary and pulled people into the fray, whether they want to play or not. Draw it smartly, around a region not entirely governed by any *single* existing game: some no-rules virgin territory if you are lucky, otherwise a domain of overlapping games none of which dictates all the action. Such an environment is somewhere between complex and chaotic ("chaoplexity"), so even just observing it demands constant modeling and remodeling to keep situation awareness.
- A **real-world game** is any set of rules of engagement that gets you and others moving out of situation-aware immobility in the face of chaoplexity. Real-world games are collisions between competing sets of rules of engagement, each chosen independently by a player and changeable arbitrarily and unilaterally as play goes on.
- Focus on the rules you make for yourself, not on consensus or inherited social rules; starting from convention is putting the cart before the horse. "The only rules that actually matter in competition are the ones individuals and organizations impose on themselves and voluntarily follow. Other rules in the environment are not rules at all, but *risks* to be modeled and managed." (Stop paying taxes because you think the government illegitimate, and you face the *risk* of being caught and punished by more powerful forces.) The playing field is a fuzzily bounded, leaky region of VUCA in which self-imposed rules collide, creating *conflict*.

## VUCA fields

VUCA (volatility, uncertainty, complexity, ambiguity) is the environmental condition out of which games precipitate; wrangling luck, influencing how serendipity and zemblanity slosh around, is wrangling VUCA. Some VUCA comes from the physical environment, but in competition much of it originates in *people*, in the social layer of play:

- **Volatility**: there are no pure friends or enemies. Most players are allies today, adversaries tomorrow, neutrals the day after.
- **Uncertainty**: potential allies and adversaries are fundamentally hard to tell apart, because situations and optimal loyalty patterns are murky.
- **Complexity**: try diagramming who-hates-whom and who-likes-whom across a corporate org chart; it is intractable beyond about a dozen people.
- **Ambiguity**: a feature of your mental model, not of the environment, marked by the *ifs, buts* and *maybes* in your thinking.

Social VUCA produces more possibilities than actualities, more flirting than commitment, more tentative probing than decisive action. Nobody wants to act first because the risks are high, yet not acting carries a huge individual and collective cost. *Risk* is a function of VUCA: the higher the VUCA, the riskier every path seems. The ifs, buts and maybes breed indecision and paralysis, so where an artificial game starts with a starter's pistol, a real-world game must be deliberately *precipitated* out of VUCA by an act of sheer will.

## Precipitating action

Creating a game means creating a **zone of artificial clarity** in VUCA; something as simple as declaring to yourself "I want a million dollars" is enough. Games differ in potential: a **high-potential game** has few rules and unleashes a lot of collective energy (technology revolutions); a **low-potential game** has many rules and unleashes little (political wrangling in Washington, DC).

Action precipitates because VUCA is unstable, and the instability shows as a paradox:

- *On the one hand*, not acting is often as risky as acting or riskier, so almost any decisive action may beat the status quo.
- *On the other hand*, acting first creates vulnerability: decisive action is a signal in a noisy environment, likely to be spotted, interpreted and intelligently answered by competitors.

When everybody is waiting or cautiously probing, decisive movement is hard to mask, because it has an **energy signature**; nonchalance can't hide serious, focused energy flows (walk into a big company's cafeteria at lunch and you can tell which teams are excited about real action and which are stuck in a fog of indecision). So playing VUCA chess means opening well with both colors: as the decisive first-mover and as the decisive fast-follower. Both create the game.

## The three phases of a game

1. **The opening**: players jockey for position to define the rules.
2. **The mid-game**: play continues after the system has lost its memory of the opening.
3. **The end-game**: unlike in board games, not a function of the rules but of conscious *time to cash out/cut losses* exit decisions.

Unlike artificial games, real-world games can begin stealthily and asymmetrically, some players starting before others, and close stealthily too: early big winners may quietly cash out and move on, and, as setup for the *next game*, try to keep potential competitors playing the *last* game as though it still mattered.

## The opening

"VUCA is a condition of noise waiting to be turned into information."

**Playing white** (first to act under VUCA): turn noise into signal, attract cooperation, and delay or misdirect competition. That is what banking a *first-mover advantage* means: masking your actions so potential adversaries ignore you longer than they should (an engineered delayed reaction) or, better, head the wrong way. Stealth and misdirection, as if in chess you could make several moves before your opponent reached the table and more while he played at the wrong board.

- Discover a **raw signal** (privileged market intelligence, say) that represents a prize worth fighting over. Send a refined version to your allies (otherwise what value are you adding?) and a noisier version to adversaries (amplified VUCA). It is like striking gold: tell your friends to come join you without triggering a general gold rush before you've taken what you want.
- Common fumbles: ignoring the competition too much, ignoring allies too much, or neglecting signal-generation. Get it right and you build runaway momentum and a solid trust-bank while sending competitors off a cliff. Amazon mastered this opening but has lately been burning trust for competitive maneuverability at a possibly unsustainable rate; Bezos's "being willing to be misunderstood" is effective for maneuvering but comes with a price.

**Playing black** (fast-following): first *detect* that moves are being made, read the first mover's opening correctly, neutralize feints meant to send you to the wrong game, then pursue in the right direction faster than they are moving, because you must catch up and overtake.

**Don't be too clever.** In slightly adversarial situations, unlike war, stealth and misdirection aimed at adversaries can alienate potential allies and destroy trust when you are "found out". There is a direct cost as well: allies are attracted by the chance to imitate your successful moves, and if you mask them too much, imitation and the cooperative scaling it brings fail. Sometimes complete transparency is the right white opening, as in the technology industry's *openness* strategy. Opening maneuvers must either protect allies (**shielding**) or anticipate and accommodate allied losses on each maneuver (**controlled losses**).

Many people drawn to Slightly Evil imagine they are in a vicious *Game of Thrones* world of constant backstabbing and unstable loyalties. The dark fantasy gives perverse satisfaction but is no more realistic than the win-win-or-no-deal view of unqualified cooperationists. Outside perhaps parts of the finance industry it is inaccurate, and operating as if it were true can backfire badly.

## The mid-game

The fog of indecision gives way to the **fog of war**: not *I don't know what to do* but *I don't know what the hell is going on.* Treat the mid-game as a fractal regime of sub-games and sub-sub-games. What keeps it from stalemate is that the non-human environment keeps changing, undermining the assumptions behind earlier successes and opening chances to change the leaderboard.

The key is **exploiting the complacency of power**. Once players have staked claims and secured some territory, everyone has something to lose, so each runs a defensive agenda alongside an offensive one. Defense gets much less attention and resource than offense, necessarily (winning would be worthless if holding the winnings took as much effort as gaining them), and the reduced effort takes the form of *habits*.

"*A habit is an efficient pattern of action dissociated from the logic that created it, and built around a fundamental desire or aversion.*" The efficiency comes from automation and suspended thought; the continuing motivation from the core desire or aversion. Habits and automation are the vulnerability that makes inside-the-tempo attacks possible.

**Habits as vulnerability.** Automation means steady, predictable rhythms, emotions and energy patterns: a fixed tempo, built on a stable decision-cycle. In OODA terms a habit is an OODA loop with the second O, *orient*, unplugged, leaving a non-adaptive **O-DA** cycle. *Unconscious* habits of adaptation are just more complex habits, not ongoing conscious orientation and *creative* adaptation.

How a challenger who has temporarily conceded a front reopens the competition:

1. **Create an opening**: watch the environment more closely than the complacent adversary, spot changes earlier, work out which of their assumptions the changes undermine, and start a sub-game to exploit it. Sometimes the complacency of steady profitability is all you need.
2. **Against an alert incumbent** (one sacrificing some profitability to stay responsive), create distractions on one front so you can open another in peace. An alert adversary is a bundle of simple and complex learned habits "*primed to respond faster and more instinctively to some threats than others*". These are their *strengths*, and also their *push-button vulnerabilities*.
3. **Use FUD.** Create a false signal suggesting that a watched assumption is being undermined, so their limited attention is absorbed on one front while you open another. You want instinctive fumbling driven by zemblanity, not decisive response: fear, uncertainty and doubt. "FUD is artificial VUCA created to drive up zemblanity for an adversary." Creating VUCA on a front where a strong incumbent feels strong, draining attention *without triggering action*, is what breaks a stalemate. It exploits **inattentional blindness**, the effect that lets someone in a gorilla suit cross a basketball court mid-game unnoticed: confusing but relatively legible action in one corner makes you miss incomprehensible action elsewhere, because attention locks onto what it can most easily turn into situation awareness.
4. **Exhaust orientation potential.** These tactics have predictable effects only when they target ingrained strong habits. An adversary running on habit is *thinking* (O-DA) but not *thinking about thinking* (O-rienting) in open-ended, creative ways. Whatever energy they have left for thinking about thinking is their remaining **orientation potential**. One front may not exhaust it; you may need to open front after front until all their withheld potential has moved into active executive attention.

A foothold creates a sub-game the adversary can't shut down immediately. Sub-games resemble top-level games but are more constrained by existing rules of engagement and yield smaller wins, because a game is already in progress around them. As action develops, energy shifts into kinetic form and each new wrinkle has less orientation potential to draw on: VUCA becomes dynamic VUCA, **VUCA(D)** (better called *kinetic* VUCA, after potential and kinetic energy). When all potential energy has been drawn into action and no new sub-game can start without stopping an old one, a cascade of ceded games and exchange sacrifices begins, setting up the end-game.

## End-games

With everyone's energy fully committed to interconnected games at many levels, you pass beyond the fog of indecision and the fog of war into the **fog of exhaustion**. Players who have already banked big wins often secure and liquidate their assets and leave the field if they can. Layer upon layer of gameplay has squeezed the intelligence out of the system, its rules having captured all available intelligence and capacity for reorientation.

Military thinking calls this the *melee*: energy only for instinctive action. You've learned all you will and used all your tricks; what remains is energy and muscle memory. It is relatively dumb last-player-standing action, driven by unforced errors, attention exhaustion and resource exhaustion.

- Players whose resources run out simply drop out.
- Players with resources left but attention fully absorbed on many fronts open fatal vulnerabilities for others to exploit, without any FUD: unforced errors. So **ceding some contests to create a reserve of attention**, before a fatal number of unforced errors accumulates, is a necessary strategy.
- Microsoft's loss of consumer leadership to Apple was more end-game exhaustion than clever maneuvering by Apple: with so much going on across so many fronts, especially enterprise markets, Microsoft lacked the attention and energy to respond meaningfully. As Apple's own games (smartphones, tablets) entered a late mid-game, Apple was being drawn into similar unforced errors.
- You can drive competitors into this state by forcing accumulating losses on them, chiefly by opening an exhausting number of fronts, because "creating games is cheaper for those with less to lose." But in end-games zemblanity also tends to take over *naturally*, without help.

End-games are messy. They may not be dynamic, but there is a lot of cleaning up: broken things everywhere, wounds and corpses, things collapsing under zemblanity forces too large to control. VUCA(D) becomes **VUCA(E)**, E for *entropy*.

## Why "inside the tempo"

Three parallel descriptions of the same process:

1. Adversarial players collectively outmaneuvering each other by creating games and games-within-games, maneuvering until players exit or die: the memetic equivalent of a genetic arms race.
2. VUCA becoming VUCA(D) and then VUCA(E): potential energy (capacity for open-ended creative reorientation) turning into kinetic energy (layers of habits carrying most of the energy flow) and finally entropic collapse.
3. Declining intelligence: an opening of great cleverness, daring and imagination, where heroes are born; a mid-game with more and more energy trapped in habits; an end-game that is mostly dumb energy with little intelligence and often no residual value, "sound and fury signifying nothing".

Because the sun pours low-entropy energy onto our corner of the universe, this game can create *wealth*, and a period of prosperity and peace before it all starts again. Partially adversarial situations drive up intelligence through open, Darwinian competition, as competitors keep getting inside each other's heads by exploiting habits of thought. Mental models grow and strengthen on all sides, codifying the latent truths of VUCA into the rules of a hierarchical complex of gameplay: a **landscape of institutions**. Such landscapes extract wealth from the environment for a time, then collapse under their own weight back into the primordial chaos from which new games begin: creative destruction.

Without adversarial competition we would collectively spiral into stupidity; by racing each other we outrun entropy for a while. Hence Rao's dislike of "the mind-atrophying idea of a permanent peace under conditions of permanent, unqualified abundance." "Competition is ultimately the essence of life itself. To be slightly evil is to embrace life."
