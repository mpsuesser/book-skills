# Orientation and Decision

The Orient and Decide stages of OODA: managing delusions, reading the attitude behind an interaction, handling conflict and negotiation, and deciding about sunk costs and retaliation. Reach here when someone's beliefs need managing rather than correcting, an exchange has turned into a status or harmony contest, a disagreement is heading for conflict, a negotiation is coming, a colleague balks at abandoning work already done, or someone has pushed you and you must decide whether and how to push back.

## Organizing the world's delusions

Rao's mangling of Google's mission statement: *To Organize the World's Delusions.* The master is Yossarian in *Catch-22*: after Clevinger mocks the superstition of moving the bomb line on the map, Yossarian knocks on wood, crosses his fingers and moves the bomb line over Bologna in the night. This is **ironically absurd behavior**, a kind of extreme method acting: he messes with the system while participating in its delusions himself, sometimes believing them through metaphysical confidence tricks played on himself, and is absurd without being random.

"Organizing delusions is at the heart of all manipulation." Work up to your **Yossarian belt** through the **hierarchy of delusion organization**:

1. **Getting out of the way**: don't challenge others' delusions; factor them into your thinking; arrange matters so the things and people you care about are out of the way of the impending train wreck.
2. **Creating a sandbox**: catalyze, encourage and sustain delusions that benefit you inside a safe sandbox, so they survive longer than they would if reality hit them early. The benign example is parenting; geeks call it a "reality distortion field". (A delusion peculiar to geekdom: assuming that pseudo-technical labeling equals inoculation.)
3. **Pouring fuel on the spark**: PR. Fuel and amplify stabilized delusions on a massive scale through mass media and 1:1 word of mouth: "managing perceptions", less politely "managing the optics".
4. **Manufacturing delusions**: a last resort, needing too much creativity to be worth it unless returns are very high. It is usually better to "amplify and domesticate delusions that you find in the wild."

**Simplify as you scale.** The more widespread a falsifiable delusion, the faster it dies. You can sustain an elaborate fantasy for one person, a few key beliefs for a large group, and at civilization scale only extremely simple, fertile fill-in-the-blanks delusions ("the Universe is here to do your bidding").

**Believe your own delusions.** Brittle, falsifiable delusions unmask their orchestrators when they collapse, inviting accusations that run from manipulation by silence to comparisons with Goebbels or Stalin (cite Godwin's Law against the latter). Even inviting such charges is the amateur way. The professional way is George Costanza's law: *it's not a lie if you believe in it.*

- That is hard with falsifiable facts, and method acting only goes so far, so rely on unfalsifiability.
- Choose delusions that it would actually benefit you to believe, at least for a while, and adopt them for exactly as long as they serve you.
- The goal is plausible deniability even inside your own mind against the charge that you don't believe what you pitch.
- Your lifeline to reality is your **capacity for doubt**, which keeps plausible deniability from hardening into denial long after the delusion's expiry date.
- Work only with delusions that are a sufficiently complex mix of **metaphysics, morality arguments, metaphor, narrative and facts**.

The fundamental axiom of delusion organization theory: "the bigger the lie, the easier it is to sell, and the biggest ones, bigger than even the civilization-scale ones, are the ones you deliberately sell to yourself." Fund an idiotic venture against all the numbers for your own reasons, and nobody can fault "I invest in people, not ideas... Well, it's a numbers game."

Unfalsifiable cover is still not enough. The Yossarian belt requires genuine ironic absurdity: trafficking in delusions without getting attached to them. Without a sense of absurdity you fall off the slightly evil path and become "yet another greedy hack, peddling subprime mortgages."

## Open and closed interactions; the four attitudes

An interaction is **open** if both parties are seeking to trade or discover information, and **closed** if even one seeks status validation, conflict or harmony instead. Either party can close it unilaterally, so most interactions end up being about social intentions.

Raw conflict framing ("You are either with me or against me") and raw status framing ("You are either better than me or worse than me") are children's games. Adults mix a status intention with a conflict/harmony intention, giving four attitudes:

- **Condescension**: I am better than you and for you
- **Contempt**: I am better than you and against you
- **Supplication**: I am worse than you and for you
- **Insolence**: I am worse than you and against you

Seeing both dimensions makes you twice as effective as people who see only one. The four attitudes pair into six basic types of interaction. Examples:

- Condescension meets contempt: "Well, I really don't have time for this, but I guess I could help you out." / "Oh I wasn't asking. We deal with this at the program level. You'll hear about what we decide at the next meeting."
- Condescension versus insolence: Al Swearengen and George Hearst in *Deadwood*.
- Condescension meets supplication: the stable base of many "Odd Couple" shows, which wander into the other patterns for variety.
- Mutual contempt: immediate breakdown and disengagement, so drama comes from forcing such people to work together. Buddy movies turn mutual contempt into mutual supplication.

The fun starts when overt attitudes don't match actual ones: the VC who is supplicating to the entrepreneur who is the new "It" person while the pitch looks patronizing/supplicatory; the poor, plain young teacher threatened by a rich, handsome student and insolent while the student supplicates for a grade.

For the Slightly Evil student these status-and-conflict games are time-wasters, though you must learn them because outside forces drag you in. Wherever possible move the conversation to an open one about generating or exchanging information, or disengage if that proves impossible.

## Conflict without ego

A: "We really need to prioritize the new version of our successful Widget." B: "I think we really need to consider a new Super Widget." A's options:

1. "I disagree, the stuff they called out in the review was bullshit." A warning shot across B's bows.
2. "Well, what's the cashflow picture for the next few quarters?" Framing a zero-sum discussion.
3. "Well, so long as our revenue from Widget doesn't decline until at least 2015, I am open to anything." Cautiously opening negotiations with preconditions.
4. "Which specific trends did you think needed a response?" A no-strings invitation to debate (or a feint to draw out intelligence for a more precise attack).

Which is smartest? *Insufficient information*: the context is missing. Product manager versus R&D lab manager means a budget fight; CFO versus CEO means something else (and salaries tied to stock price add a layer); two salespeople, one who likes babysitting old customers and one who likes pitching new ones, means something else again, and commissions change it further. Missing material facts unconnected to motivation are basic ignorance, and non-idiots simply reconvene once they have them.

What is buried in context is **individual motivations**: how yours relate to those of the people you are in conflict with. They stay buried because modern culture expects conversations to be objective, fact-based quests for "truth", even in close relationships. The result is a theater of superficial objectivity on top of a mountain of consequential context. A **3-layer model**:

1. **Overt objective material**, acknowledged openly.
2. **Extrinsic motivations**: functions of the environment, such as role and incentives. Sophisticated conversationalists gracefully foreground these as relevant or not ("I am not just saying this because I am coming at it from the CFO perspective" is a discounting move that deepens the conversation).
3. **Intrinsic motivations**: ego issues, defense mechanisms, status dynamics, possibly too repressed to be consciously accessible. For response 1 they might be: a snub from the chief marketing officer at the last party; insecurity about seeming "boring" next to R&D; a chip on the shoulder about R&D's childish silliness while A's group earns the money; buried anxiety triggered by B's plaid jacket, like one a sadistic primary-school teacher wore; unacknowledged gender dynamics and a felt need to be assertive; sexual tension; a sense of violence in the air (a powerful athlete facing a scrawny weakling, bullying instincts stirring).

The way through is not detailed dissection (useful offline, for learning) but **conflict without ego**, the martial arts ideal, where "ego" means all intrinsic motivations, known and unknown.

- **You cannot get mad at facts.** The sun rises; things fall; employees must be paid. Any emotion in play attaches to individual motivations, even when the facts concern people (you can't be mad that others are more attractive, only about the desires attractiveness serves).
- **Foreground extrinsic motivations** and drain them of unhelpful emotion; they belong in objective analysis and they are outside you. Response 2 does this: it puts the unspoken "don't touch my cash flow!" on the table and quantifies it, turning Super Widget from an existential threat into a trade-off to compute. That takes domain experience and conversational skill.
- **Bracket intrinsic motivations.** You can't foreground them: they may be unspeakable ("I want to whip your scrawny ass"), undiscoverable even with years of psychoanalysis (the plaid jacket), and are almost certainly irrelevant. Acknowledge and bracket the emotions and consciously let them go. Anger and pride are easier; a nameless one may show only as a slight tension in your lower back. Become aware of it and let it go without understanding its root cause. As long as the overt topic is genuine, you can let irrelevant things go. Mindfulness practice helps, but even awareness of how this works is half the battle.

**Why conflict remains.** "Violence is a *necessary* consequence of *unavoidable* ignorance on the part of systems that lack infinite wisdom." You cannot enlighten yourself out of it. The everyday form: ***The ideas of your friends are not always the friends of your ideas.*** Even two mindful, egoless people handling facts and extrinsic motivations well will still conflict, because complex intelligence won't emerge on schedule. We pursue "ideas" that seem right instead of mechanically working inferences from facts, and each person spots and pursues ideas differently, from different experience, pattern recognition and prior knowledge. When idea paths diverge you must capitulate and follow, or fight. (Spiritually framed: you fight today so the world is slightly more enlightened tomorrow.)

Eleanor Roosevelt: "Great minds discuss ideas, average minds discuss events, small minds discuss people." But jumping straight to ideas doesn't make a great mind; discussing ideas signals greatness only once events and people (context and motivation) have been dealt with. A friendship that survives diverging and converging pursuit of ideas gains depth. Relationships built on false harmony, or on one party cravenly abandoning their ideas to preserve the relationship, are weak and not worth much.

## The basics of negotiation

The best everyday negotiators look nothing like Hollywood's ice-cold types: a man who couldn't hold eye contact, stuttered and smiled too much played lowball and won every time; a genuinely scatterbrained, chattering, whining woman won big; Kevin-Spacey-in-*The-Negotiator* lookalikes blew sure-fire deals. Negotiate by how you think you should look and you become George Costanza swaggering into a deal for *less* money than NBC first offered.

The reason: in routine negotiations almost *all* the work happens away from the table, before the face-to-face encounter. Often the pre-work is so effective that the negotiation never happens or is a ritual. The tense, zero-sum, ticking-clock negotiation of the movies is the exception: no pre-work possible, artificially hard deadlines. The most common negotiations are the ones that never happen because a skilled person kept things from reaching the table, substituting dull pre-work for live negotiation and cherry-picking the *ideal* time to start instead of facing a fixed deadline.

Extreme-negotiation skills are expensive, slow to acquire and worthless for everyday use. "Bringing a gun to a knife fight is as pointless as bringing a knife to a gunfight", and bringing *any* weapon to what could be a handshake is beyond stupid. When you really face a gunfight negotiation, hire a gunfighter; don't try to become one at a weekend seminar.

Four elements, and BATNA is the only technical term you need:

- **Information.** The more that can be mutually agreed, the less there is to negotiate. "Good pre-work trumps good table-work every time": each fact moved from the "to be debated" column to the "common ground" column makes the table-thumper one bit less effective. Hostage negotiations are hard because there is so little information and time. The best way to avoid negotiating is to understand the other parties' options, costs and benefits better than they do and work out the best-for-everybody solution before reaching the table. The pre-work is definitions and technical analysis: define all options and model risks and rewards with decision theory, game theory, statistics and Excel. It is tedious gruntwork, and it turns apparently "human" factors into data (discounting-based analysis of future payoffs replaces loosey-goosey "win-win" talk with numbers).
- **Creativity.** After the analysis, too much uncertainty may remain, leaving room for he-said-she-said debate. Invent *new* options that use the information better by lowering uncertainty all round: turn a $10-million deal into a short-term $100,000 "pilot engagement"; date before marriage. Do the creative thinking ahead of time, or you get dragged into real-time creative storytelling.
- **Trust.** Hold "discovery" pre-work conversations that tease out what everyone knows and hasn't shared, and read mutual trust levels. Don't *attempt* the negotiation until trust is high enough all round, which may take months of 1:1, small-group and full-group meetings ostensibly about discovery and knowledge sharing (informal lunches, parties, seminars). Figure out:
  - Who is negotiating in good faith, and who in bad faith?
  - Who is free with information, and who is secretive?
  - Who are your allies, who is hopelessly intransigent and must be neutralized, and who needs gentle persuasion over multiple interactions?
  - How much of this trust information is common knowledge?
  - The crucial one: which parties will deal with each other for an indeterminate future, and who will exit? "About the *only* firm truth in negotiation" is that people about to end all relationships and walk away behave *very* differently from people unsure how long they'll keep dealing (the insight behind the iterated prisoner's dilemma). Think in terms of the pasts and futures of every interpersonal story involved.
- **BATNA** ("Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement"). If the deal isn't worth the information, creativity and trust pre-work, walk away on Day 1. BATNA itself takes little pre-work time but is the hardest element to master, because it means walking away *after* expensive pre-work, a potential cost greater than the other three combined (Kipling: risk the heap of winnings on one turn of pitch-and-toss, lose, and start again).
  - **BWTWA** (Being Willing To Walk Away), as when haggling for a cheap watch at a street market, is the simplest BATNA. All BATNAs are forms of walking away: at best a lost opportunity, at worst armed conflict. You can't walk away from a mugger; your BATNA is run or fight. In a salary negotiation it is to quit (perhaps losing recommendations); for an entrepreneur facing bad VC terms it may be to shut down the company. BATNA is hard because it is *always* a willingness to lose something, sometimes your life.
  - **Life BATNAs**: baseline commitments to yourself that let you cheerfully walk away from anything. Your BATNA for the whole "deal of life" is fixed: you are going to die, since your opponent, nature, is infinitely more powerful. Accept death as the BATNA for specific situations and, since nearly every other BATNA beats death, you become a very strong negotiator. The one alternative worse than death is torture (the booga-booga joke: the chief's sadistic OR-XOR bluff, "Booga-booga him to death!").
  - "Without a defined BATNA, negotiations are toothless." BATNAs make all negotiating at least slightly evil, even best-faith, informed negotiation, because your BATNA is a *unilateral* option whose consequences others are forced to live with. "It takes a slightly evil attitude to be willing to force potentially unpleasant consequences on others, regardless of what they want." Deceit, bad faith and bluffing add as much more evil as you like.

## Indifference to sunk costs

Slightly evil people look like extreme risk-takers but are often *more* conservative; the illusion comes from **indifference to sunk costs**. Eleven months into a twelve-month software build you find an open-source equivalent that two weeks of tweaking would make better than finishing your own: the eleven months are irrelevant, and the slightly evil decision-maker throws them away.

There is no absolute rationality here, though. Nobody can tell you what subjective value to place on an outcome (the generalization of "don't tell me how to feel"). If what you value in projects is war stories of delivering under pressure, one more war story may beat delivering earlier, better and cheaper. Halfway up a roadless mountain you find a road and a driver offers a lift: "There are many roads to the top of the mountain, and the view is *not* the same."

**Relative rationality** is consistency between the decision-making story (the expected value of a decision) and the experienced ever-after story (the realized value of the consequences). The irrationality is telling yourself one story to decide and another to live with the outcome: making the "rational" choice and then regretting the lost closure for life, or the reverse. That inconsistency drives individual rationalization; at the collective level, rationalization is triggered by conflict between different people's stories.

Sunk costs seduce people attached to their subjective past, who can't bear that who they were two days ago did something futile and want to redeem their past days. Slightly evil people, by nature or cultivation, can't think this way; they value external, tangible outcomes most. To others they look callous ("how could you throw it all away?", "didn't you feel like finishing just for the sake of closure?").

In collaborative work:

- The newbie mistake is absolute rationality: lecturing colleagues on why sunk costs don't matter.
- The sophisticated move: if a teammate's subjective rationality makes them react in ways that create *real* outcome problems, those costs are now yours too. A partner attached to finishing may rationalize ("we can't use that open source thing; we haven't tested it enough...") and, overruled, resent you and cause trouble later.
- The test: do the future real costs of the resentment outweigh the benefit of the sunk-cost-indifferent decision? If so, humor your colleague. If not, decide and deal with the fallout.

As situations grow complex and objective and social consequences blend into messy stories, sunk-cost indifference becomes the philosophical attitude we call *realism*.

## Vindictiveness, vendettas and revenge

Revenge is **vindictiveness** plus a capacity for long-range planning. Vindictiveness is the simpler, more fundamental trait: in tribal societies it produces vendetta dynamics; in civilized ones, revenge dynamics. "Vindictiveness and vendettas are quite rational, but *revenge* is not." To the extent your society is tribal, vindictiveness and vendettas are objectively rational; to the extent it is civilized, they backfire. Revenge is *never* objectively worthwhile, ironically since it takes the most intelligence.

**Real-time vindictiveness** is the natural tendency to push back immediately when pushed, as hard or harder, or, if you can't, to remember the slight and push back at the first opportunity. It is a hard-wired assumption that whoever initiates a conflict is probably in the wrong. Road-rage test: someone honks and gives you the finger. Either they did something dangerous and are blaming you, or you did and they are punishing you.

- Instantly honking back harder (assuming the first) shows a strong vindictive streak.
- Shrinking back ("Oh, what did I do wrong?") makes you a bit of a wuss, unless you have good reason to assume fault, such as being a new driver.
- Suspending judgment to investigate is the apparently rational, data-driven way, and where winning matters it is a way to lose. Such situations are fast and ambiguous, blame is shared and luck plays a role; if you don't act immediately your analysis won't matter. "Inaction is loss."

Vindictiveness is a status-leveling move, aimed at *getting even*: whatever the backstory, "*If you don't hurt them back, you've lost status points.*" So its intensity tracks perceived *status* loss, not material loss. The same damage reads differently as an open-handed slap (lowering the other's status), a backhanded slap (asserting your own), or a punch (status-neutral, context decides). Beyond proportionality, respond in the *same kind*: an eye for an eye.

Why vindictive pushback is rational where everybody knows everybody:

- You will meet the person again, and each of you will tell your version to the group. Fail to push back, and your silence reads as guilt: "Yeah, I yelled at him, and he had nothing to say. The jerk knew he was in the wrong."
- Pushback is the real-time equivalent of pleading "not guilty": the symmetry of the skirmish leaves a record that can be spun either way, and the he-said-she-said is settled by majority vote on social proof of character and power, not material truth. This is where tribal "honor" comes from, and why a reputation as someone not to be messed with is crucial in tribal cultures.
- Push back *harder* for a decisive outcome, especially before witnesses: you visibly penalize the unjustified conflict; the opponent personally learns not to mess with you, out of real fear; and even if they persuade friends they were morally right, you gain not-to-be-messed-with PR because you won the actual fight.
- The strong form: "*Even if you know you were wrong, if you ALSO know the other person cannot possibly know that, it pays to take offense and push back.*" Accepting blame without proof legitimizes the other's authority to act as judge, jury and executioner without proof. Unless they are perfectly virtuous, you have set up a corrupt mini-court to prosecute you, where everything said will be used against you and your clan.
- In the tribal world, generosity, benefit of the doubt, handing over information advantages without a fight, and turning the other cheek regardless of the opponent's character are "recipes for getting killed very quickly", which is why prisons have the first-day rule.

**Vendettas** follow when you can't respond at once (an indirect attack: insults spray-painted on your door, an ally beaten). Push-back-harder combines with **social compound interest**: the insult festers and grows with every moment and every retelling. This is not just subjective resentment: the status loss causes real, ongoing losses in *other* transactions with everyone who has heard the story.

- A vendetta is not revenge. It involves little deliberation, planning or subterfuge, since each move aims to show *raw* power and make the "not guilty" case to public opinion, not to show intelligence. Make your move visibly close to the original (they killed one of your sons; you kill one, or *two*, of theirs). Clever moves make the story socially illegible. Make it obvious you did it and why; symmetry cancels everything except the raw status accounting.
- Vendettas are unstable and escalate. Compound interest is ambiguous and real-world options are coarse: if tribal justice decides you are owed 1.2 sons, no move cancels the original, so you under- or overreact; and if the opponent has no son, a substitute (burning a house, kidnapping a daughter) adds ambiguity. "The status market has inefficiencies." Short-term it pays to overreact and leave the inefficiency to the other side. (Tit for Tat is an evolutionary equilibrium of the iterated prisoner's dilemma only under perfect information and zero ambiguity.)
- The only resolutions: draw a dividing line and keep a detente with occasional skirmishes so everybody remembers, or one group moves away until memories fade.

**Revenge** is what vindictiveness becomes in "civilization": weaker group memory, tribes that form and disband easily, many dealings with strangers, honor moved into medals, degrees and expensive cars, and disinterested third parties to adjudicate. Vindictive goal-setting (push back when pushed; compound-interest magnitude) keeps running on its tribal assumptions, and when civilization gets in the way (the tribe dissolves, the opponent moves to another city) you bring all your rational powers to *engineer* a payback opportunity. *The Count of Monte Cristo* is the archetype, and Dantes himself sees at the end that it is hollow: with no shared tribe and no interested third parties comparing your statuses, you are balancing an internal equation even your opponent can't parse.

- Unleashed in a non-tribal context, vindictive instincts can also turn on faceless *institutions*. At best this yields tough, assertive handling of phone customer service; at worst, the Unabomber. Revenge against an institution is as silly as revenge on the zoo lion that ate your brother: "It was looking for lunch." Hanlon's Razor: *never attribute to malice what can be adequately explained by stupidity*.
- The transition to institutions is tricky because judges are people too. In Premchand's *God Lives in the Panch*, a man's vendetta enemy is appointed to the council about to try him, feels respect for the *role* on taking office, and delivers a fair verdict.
- Revenge is "just plain insanity". That does not make vindictiveness or vendetta-seeking irrational. The world is *not* fully civilized. Where an extended conflict plays out inside the *same* tribal context (a corporate department) and reputations matter, and where the players are legible (you aren't imputing tribal motives to a system), **calibrated vindictive behavior** is valuable.
  - Fail to push back in the office and people walk all over you. A nominal peer who asserts authority by "delegating" work to you over email needs immediate pushback to set the right status relationship, possibly with a cc to your joint manager. That creates a problem for the manager, whose job is to civilize the essentially tribal interaction for the greater good.
  - Marketing and sales sometimes need a period of vendetta before they deal with each other with the right mutual respect instead of one walking all over the other. The CEO must civilize it without killing the tribal passions that also get real work done.
- Where the world is *not* tribal, vindictive instincts have only two outlets: forget, forgive and move on (probably to a different tribe), or go insane.
