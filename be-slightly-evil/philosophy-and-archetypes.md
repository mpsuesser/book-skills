# The Slightly Evil stance and personality archetypes

The philosophy behind the playbook, and the archetypes Rao uses to read people and life paths. Reach here for "what am I doing with my life?" questions, career forks, a person who has turned petty or vicious, questions of how far a persuasion technique may go, and any request to explain the philosophy itself.

## Moral minimalism

Four ideas sit behind the name, which mocks Google's "Don't Be Evil" (at immense scales of power, self-policing is too dangerous; only checks and balances built from outside forces pursuing *different* ends curb true evil):

- **The universe isn't here for our benefit.** It is not a benign environment.
- **Advantage Determinism.** Known and as-yet-unknown laws govern more of natural phenomena, human behavior included, than we like to admit.
- **Humility** towards nature and the unknown. Not social or interpersonal humility.
- **Acknowledge "Evil".** Whatever good and evil are, we each have a little of both. Acknowledgement beats denial.

Together these make "a pragmatic truth-driven philosophy of moral minimalism": every new true thing learned shrinks the domain where moral opinions are useful (no point having a moral opinion about gravity). Madame de Stael: "When you understand everything, you can forgive everything." Shrug off every bit of pointless moral "responsibility" you can.

The deepest idea is thermodynamics. Entropy makes the universe mildly hostile, not just indifferent; we are low-entropy creatures swimming upstream towards heat death. So the universe is, in a sense, Slightly Evil, and by fractal logic so is our nature.

**Amorality heuristic**: "an idea is Slightly Evil only if it is, in principle, equally valuable to both good and evil people." Anything that works better *only* if you are good is suspect.

Rao deals in the *how*, not the *why*. He filters out means no end could justify and leaves means-ends justification to the reader.

## Be somebody or do something

John Boyd (fighter pilot, author of Energy-Maneuverability theory and the OODA loop, who beat the Pentagon bureaucracy from inside and built a cadre of acolytes) challenged each of his followers: "in life you eventually have to decide whether to be somebody, or do something". Whistle-blowing is the kind of situational decision that can force the fork.

**Deathbed test**: ask what you want your life to have been like. If you instinctively picture yourself at the peak of your life, you are a *be somebody* person (CEO, tenured professor, rich and famous). If you picture the impact and are fuzzy on what you will be like, you are a *do something* type. Boyd's right answer: *do something*. Slightly Evil is a do-something philosophy ("I sometimes think I should have named this philosophy 'Do Slightly Evil.'").

The paradox of the two paths:

- The straight path defined by your own principles turns into spaghetti in the real world: you must twist and turn to get around those who deny truths and defend obvious falsehoods.
- The straight path of a standard career script demands philosophical twisting to justify it: "You sell your soul one career move at a time." The Golden Boy careerist.
- Uncompromising private principles that you do not seek to justify to others are necessary but not sufficient. Your terrain is the maze of truth-avoidance paths worn by be-somebody types and "paved by the medal-awarding priests"; your mission is to tackle head-on the truths they avoid.
- To be-somebody types the slightly evil moves look like "shortcuts", because they can't see how someone gets somewhere meaningful off the straight-line path and without awards to measure progress. The principled, truth-seeking path is "the faster road to meaningful destinations."

Legacies: being somebody gets a successful life that offended nobody, forgotten a week after death, medals in attic boxes. Doing something likely means dying destitute and unrecognized, with implacable enemies and loyal-to-the-death friends, while people scramble to preserve your unfinished junk. Boyd died in 1997 in near-poverty, never above Colonel; his papers are preserved at the Marine Corps Research Center at Quantico.

## The good/evil × optimism/pessimism 2x2

Exercise: put evil-to-good on the x axis and pessimism-to-optimism on the y axis, fill each quadrant with people (famous caricatures work best), and label the archetypes. With any 2x2 the challenge is to break the dichotomies locally for your own situation; airy dismissal plus a trite substitute is not dichotomy-breaking.

- **Optimistic-evil**: grand social engineering (Nazis, Communists). Puts dissenters from its idea of progress in concentration camps.
- **Optimistic-good**: New Age visions and traditional religion, Left and Right. Seeks positive resonance, labels non-scripted dissent "negative", and pushes dissenters into cultural concentration camps where cheerleading drowns them out ("Bright-Sided", Ehrenreich; a tyranny of the vocal minority). The heuristic has merit, since serious change needs collective energy and negativity drains it, but turns dangerous as runaway self-reinforcing positivism.
- **Pessimistic-good**: justice and protection of already-achieved good; dedicated cops, doctors, military types who see themselves as protecting the optimistic-good's innocence. Young, they hold unexamined social values; war and crime-fighting disillusion them into private values (Dirty Harry to *Gran Torino*). Tends to stability maintenance against destabilizing "evil".
- **Pessimistic-evil**: fatalistic, hedonistic individualism; human nature eternally flawed; zero-sum exploits and social hacks preying on the other three quadrants (Tony Soprano). Favors balance of power and eternal patterns of exploitation. Total disillusionment can turn pessimistic-good into pessimistic-evil (Two-Face), but most who walk that path end "bitter and ineffective rather than actively evil."

Realism and pragmatism are meaningless on this 2x2: every quadrant thinks itself realistic and pragmatic, and all can be right, because optimism and pessimism are attitudes about possibilities with no empirical basis, and good and evil are about intentions.

- **Naive realists** avoid taking any position on possibilities. They win when events are outside human control and miss opportunities that depend on social proof: things that become social truths if enough people believe them, beliefs that create social capital rather than truth.
- **Naive pragmatists** act only when there is a realistic chance of being effective and abhor empty gestures. They miss out when "making an effort" has its own signalling value (the politician's brave, futile attempt at a wicked problem earns points for trying). "Naive pragmatism is for bureaucrats, not effective politicians." They forgo "symbolic creative failure and its social rewards", which often makes them the most unrealistic people around.
- Rao's resolution: realism is a *desire* to see the world realistically, pragmatism a *desire* to be effective. Believing you have achieved either is a sign you are trapped in a quadrant; skepticism and doubt mark the sophisticated versions.

**Where Slightly Evil sits**: its obvious location is just southwest of the origin, but it overrides the dichotomies locally. On optimism/pessimism it is agnostic, yet active where social proof and signalling through empty or futile gestures are in play; it values social-capital movements around obvious falsehoods and ineffective behaviors. That takes doubletalk (stated beliefs differ from held ones), but "doubletalk is at least better than doublethink." On good/evil it drives towards action whether or not consequences are clearly good or evil, choosing churn over stability and acting for the sake of acting (creative destruction): not good (no belief in change as progress), not evil (no belief in value-driven stability). Label: "double-talking chaos-creation".

## No free lunch instincts: where slightly evil stops

Rao is at ease with conversational *jiu jitsu*, organizational politicking and sociopathic philosophical attitudes, but not with "subconscious brain hacking" of others (PUA, NLP, SEO-style persuasion: subliminal words, gestures, priming, sexual imagery). Such techniques "hack the overt intentions of whoever you are interacting with, and bring unrelated desires into play": the psychological equivalent of roofies. They work.

**No free lunch (NFL) instinct**: when your gut says stay away and you can't say why, there are hidden costs. All true knowledge is expressible as constraints or conservation principles, so assume that someone or something is paying for any free lunch. Until you are sure it isn't you, or someone or something you care about, don't use the partial knowledge, *even if it is true*, even if you are taken advantage of meanwhile. "If you don't know who the sucker is, it is probably you."

The costs of brain hacking:

- **Intelligence out of play.** Say hacking lifts cold-call closes from 1% to 10%. You have put the valuable intelligence informing the other party's intentions out of play: sold sex to a monkey that wants sex instead of graphic design to a company that needs it. Practitioners who promise to be "ethical" are trusting themselves under moral hazard with seriously incomplete information.
- Disabling the other side's intelligence is justified in outright conflict: "you don't want a fair fight if you can help it. You should prefer a dumb enemy over a smart one." Most everyday situations are only partly adversarial, and to the extent they are not, the other party's fully engaged intelligence is worth having. Even parenting very young children, some decisions for poor and uneducated people, and medicine benefit from everyone's intelligent engagement.
- **Slippery slope.** There may be a personal free lunch (10x sales; 8 of the 9 new customers didn't need it and quit, which is fine if retention is worthless and the fad pays), but easy rewards come with deep costs, the pact-with-the-devil kind. Once you start you escalate: "a company that starts down the road to evil in even a small way will end up totally evil." Poirot: *do not open your heart to evil.*
- **Dehumanization loop.** You can stabilize at slightly evil only if you always "pick on someone your own size": fair fights, like weights of increasing resistance, are the only way to grow; fighting below your weight class shrivels your muscles. Hacking teaches you to treat everybody as an adversary who *cannot fight back*, because of a skill they haven't learned to defend against. You lose non-adversarial relationships and the pleasure of fully human people; this is gollumization. Hacking *yourself* (self-suggestion, deliberate self-delusion) runs the loop fastest: "Self-gollumization is the fastest kind there is."

**The boundary**: "it stops being 'slightly' evil when you start impoverishing your non-adversarial relationships in any way, particularly the one with yourself." To a marketer: "keep your customers fully human."

Tests:

- After an encounter you "win", does the other person feel they *learned* something valuable at a reasonable price, or that they were played for a sucker or paid too high a price for the learning?
- Against rationalization via dehumanization: would you want to win *that way* against a spouse, friend, child, pet or parent? (An empathy calibration scale is useful in many situations.)

**The hard way flips costs and rewards.** Building a business reputation, a trusted clientele or a solid relationship the hard way trades easy rewards and deep costs for **easy costs and deep rewards**. Money is the perfect easy cost: past "problem solved" levels for your lifestyle, giving up more money is easier than exploiting a lifelong relationship for immediate gain "simply because you can". The deep rewards: understanding how the world works and growing peace with your place in it. The choice is self-reinforcing: accept deep costs and you grow shallower to insulate yourself; learn to enjoy deep rewards and you grow deeper.

## Why power corrupts: information parenthood

Research (Keltner) finds people give authority to people they genuinely like, and that *getting* power then turns them into jerks: impulsive, hostile, like patients with orbito-frontal damage. Being a jerk is different from being evil. Rao adds the *why*. People give authority for at least three reasons:

1. **Popular mandate**: liking. Primal; chimp troupe leaders hold power by inheritance, alliances and fights, and are deposed when their "total power" drops below a rival's.
2. **Presocial one-on-one**: A gives B authority if B can beat him up, physically or psychologically. Declining for millennia, but it still matters where 1:1 interactions dominate, as in sales.
3. **Valuable information or skills**: authority granted to people we neither like nor fear. Chief among such skills is seeing reality in minimally deluded ways, which can turn matters of opinion into matters of fact and overturn votes. Marooned on an island, people elect the jerky survivalist. A. G. Lafley: "A CEO's job is to interpret external realities for a company."

Reality is usually between neutral and slightly unpleasant, so interpreting it means keeping people connected enough to reality to be effective but not so connected they are demoralized. Call it **information parenthood**: you sustain a happy bubble for others while your own parent is reality, "Drunk and abusive Father Reality, not nurturing Mother Nature." Hence "Why am I the only adult around here?" and the loneliness of leadership: you survive without incoming empathy, become the sole reservoir of harsh information, and may drain your empathy into resentment and sadism, or regress and take a turn at being the child. Much executive coaching (Marshall Goldsmith) builds endurance at this game. The relief is colleagues tough enough to need no protection, which is why two-founder teams beat solo founders: two people who can be brutally honest with each other.

## Bitter, trapped loserdom

The diagnosis "Powerlessness Corrupts": slashed middle ranks get more burden without more efficacy, turn risk-averse and rigid, overcontrol their turf, demand tribute before responding, and vent on the more powerless (boss to worker to wife to child to dog).

In Gervais Principle terms (sociopaths, clueless and losers), this is the clueless finally getting clued in to their exploited status, too late. Losers, who never rise to this level, keep producer skills and stay mobile; these discover loser-level clarity after their individual-contributor skills have eroded. They can't move up or down, are too much company men/women to leave, leave only if fired, and silently endure as work piles on. This is "how petty bureaucratic tyrants are born." Unlike the regular clueless, they cannot be easily manipulated: run a mile. Their appearance is "the beginning of the end for organizations": smart people start exiting from top and bottom.

## Idealists and Tragedians: the Unreasonable Man Effect

- **Idealism**: belief in the perfectability of humans (*self-improvement*, *personal growth*). Mainstream, especially in America, where for the last century all politics and religion have been idealist (hippies and evangelicals alike; the Democrat-idealist/Republican-tragedian split is a mistake).
- **The tragic stance**: humans are unchanging, with limited, flawed natures that fail in predictable ways. Historically as popular as idealism except in the twentieth century. Slightly Evil, like all pop-Machiavellian philosophies, is tragic.

**The Idealist-Tragedian paradox**: idealism believes in change and creates unchanging humans; tragedism believes humans can't change and transforms them more radically. Idealism mostly decays into **adaptation**: cubicle dwellers who form a New Age commune carry their baggage and recreate the same politics; believers in progress hold *stable* marginal positions and maintain the status quo. "The revolution never comes."

**The Unreasonable Man Effect**: if humans can't change, improving your life means changing your environment. Shaw: the unreasonable man persists in adapting the world to himself, "Therefore, all progress depends on the unreasonable man." Whyte's true executive is the organization man most suspicious of The Organization, wanting to dominate, not be dominated. Behaving this way triggers creative destruction in the environment that turns around and transforms you, a Nietzschean self-destruction before resurrection. Any major project transforms *you*, not just its output. In *The Alchemist* the seeker after literal treasure is transformed as a side effect.

**Resolving it with Lego**: you are a particular construction of blocks. The design can be torn down and rebuilt; the blocks (abstract consequences of genes) cannot be changed. Idealist/Tragedian has the contours of Nature/Nurture: both dissolve into constrained design spaces, building blocks and path-dependent expression. Idealists only build up, never tear down, and trap themselves in cul-de-sacs of incremental change two ways:

- **Life choice**: they pursue change *directly* (the Zen retreat) instead of challenging *external projects*. The tragedian starts a business or writes a book, resists the resulting change, and finally *accepts* it as a rebirth. It is risky: success may bring drugs and depression, failure may bring enlightenment faster than meditation.
- **Metaphysical own-goal**: *continuously evolving perfection* (you are perfect at every point, only becoming "more perfect") makes it impossible to backtrack or admit a deep mistake. "It's a learning process" works until a mistake bankrupts, kills or psychologically destroys you.

Consequences of the tragedian stance:

- Idealists revere win-win over win-lose; Tragedians are neutral and pick the framing that suits the situation.
- Idealists revere long-term thinking; Tragedians seek the *appropriate* time horizon.
- Idealists seek "sustainability" or "sustainable growth"; Tragedians think both vacuous.
- Idealists try to be kind and end up unwittingly cruel; Tragedians are often low-empathy sociopaths who end up doing good without meaning to.

## Self, shadow and evil twins

Two readings of any life story (Rao's case: Lee, who rebelled against a Tiger Parent family, dropped out of an Ivy, did a decade of developing-world social work, then a master's and corporate HR, and asks how to manage career anxiety, aggressiveness versus patience, and conventional work versus "life-hacking"). Both readings are needed; neither suffices alone.

- **Pragmatic reading**: banal, cliched, easy to communicate, non-threatening. Situational details loom: career and financial expectations, goal setting, getting ahead versus getting along, work-life balance. Fixes made inside it are temporary band-aids and lead to imitative life scripts (the food blog, the Arduino robot, Internet Marketing from Bali) because that's what people in superficially similar situations do. The same situation yields different perceived quality: "Your journey from hell to heaven might be the very definition of a fall from heaven to hell for me."
- **Shadow reading** (the Jungian shadow): personality is a **self** (parts you accept and are attached to, seen as strengths) and a **shadow** (parts you reject as weaknesses and have an aversion to, mostly unconscious). You see your shadow only by projecting it onto external realities, especially people: your **evil twins**, in whom your shadow is the conscious self and vice versa. Your shadow shows in your own behavior only under extreme stress or extreme relaxation.

The self-shadow diagram: two overlapping circles. Self-only is **Addictions**; shadow-only is **Aversions**; the overlap, where you have engaged your shadow consciously, is **Freedoms**: traits that are neither strengths nor weaknesses but simply your nature. Addictions and aversions enslave. The life journey is integrating self and shadow until the overlap covers the whole personality. Crude map: in your Myers-Briggs type's list of eight cognitive functions, the first four are your self and the last four your shadow.

Sudden, dramatic career switches are usually moves from self-work to shadow-work or back: fleeing shadow stress into "self-expression", or tackling the shadow after self-indulgence has cost too much ("going over to the dark side").

**Shadowboxing**: work with self and shadow in fine-grained, continuous ways instead of big sudden shifts. Rao (INTP) found his fourth function, extroverted feeling, on the cusp: conflict avoidance and harmony-seeking that he rationalized as "good at peace-making". He deliberately resisted the peacemaking temptation and grew comfortable with overt conflict, which made him less manipulable by people who create scenes for leverage. He can now ride out others' storms without pouring oil on troubled waters, get aggressive in controlled ways, and briefly browbeat from an ideological high horse (it feels toxic, so he quits fast).

- Shadow behaviors in others feel like the definition of evil to you. To explore your shadow you must tiptoe into behavior that feels slightly evil *to you*. This is dangerous.
- **Safety belt**: engage real evil twins who are broadly viewed as valuable people: people who reach conclusions similar to yours (the "twin" part) through values you find repellent. Read them through an **evil twin filter**. Rao's evil twins prize "authenticity" and object to his strength, "managing perceptions and realities on separate tracks"; their strength reads to him as naivete about useful deception (Santa Claus, comforting the sick, pulling a fast one on adversaries).
- Face to face, pick only "slightly evil twins". A substantive encounter with a full-blown evil twin can take years to get over, and failing to get over it can grow a resentful us-versus-them philosophy built on one encounter.
- A slightly evil twin makes a good sparring partner for anyone grappling with a problem that requires shadow traits. The more your overlap grows, the more people you can spar with.

**Translating the shadowy to the banal**: shadowboxing pays off only when abstract decisions ("curb extroverted feeling") become concrete: which friends and hobbies, which promotions to angle for, which projects to pick and drop, how to act at a specific meeting or party, which authors to read. This translation is a *problem-solving skill*, the proof that you hold a narrative analysis and enactment language, not just a vocabulary. Starter exercise: pick a hobby through your shadow. Rao's extroverted sensing is seventh of eight; when relaxed he enjoys exercising it, which explains his boyhood astronomy (self motive: interest; shadow motive: the relaxing act of observation) and his new binocular birding. Gift-giving rule: give a shadow-based relaxant, never a shadow-based stressor.

For Lee: re-narrate the situation in self/shadow terms, then ask what to do to integrate the two.

## Hobbes versus Rousseau

Thought experiment: you are the fastest, benevolent gunslinger in the Wild West, who never intervenes directly. Two unarmed men are about to fistfight over a sack of gold they found together. Do you toss each a gun, making a true Mexican standoff whose escalated cost (death, not injury) pushes them to negotiate a share (Hobbes)? Or appeal to their noble instincts (Rousseau)? Does the size of the bag matter? (Modern politicians would argue until it was too late.)

- **Hobbes** (1588-1679): in the state of nature humans are competitive, violent, corruptible. Evil is natural, good an aberration. Tragic.
- **Rousseau** (1712-1778): humans are harmony-loving, cooperative, perfectible. Good is natural, evil an aberration. Idealist.
- They are evil twins: similar stories of how political order emerges, opposite prescriptions. Today's conservatives descend from Hobbes and progressives from Rousseau; the dialectic has ossified into a detente that produces no political innovation.
- Updated by Fukuyama (*The Origins of Political Order*): the egalitarian units of the state of nature were kin-groups and tribes, not individuals; individual personhood emerged around the 13th century in England. The adapted pair of assumptions still applies.
- **Recognition, not scarcity.** The naive belief that we compete or cooperate according to scarcity or abundance misses this: above a minimum size, the gold is an excuse for a fight for *recognition*, forcing the other to recognize you as worthy via submission. The urge is quixotic, since recognition from a defeated adversary is devalued by the defeat. Identity politics is mostly recognition-seeking. For Hobbes (and Hegel, Nietzsche, Fukuyama) the struggle is unalterable; for Rousseau a pathology to be shed.
- Texts: *Deadwood* (Hobbesian); *The Dark Knight* (Rousseauish outcome despite the Joker's Hobbesian experiment). The Internet is a new state-of-nature experiment, closer to the original because individuals have never been more powerful relative to tribes and states: bet on Hobbesian or Rousseauean dynamics?
