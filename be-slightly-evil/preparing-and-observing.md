# Preparing to play, and Observation

Status play, reputation, opening moves in a new group, and the Observe stage of OODA: reading people and listening. Reach here when choosing what status to play, deciding how much of yourself to show, joining a new team or place, facing an agent you suspect of padding a bill, blocking or protecting work through rules, sizing someone up, judging whether someone is telling the truth, or absorbing an attack.

## Status 101

The breakthrough question: "how do I know what status to play in a given situation?" Until you ask it you are in **locked-status mode**: the status you feel stands in a predictable, unchanging relation to the status you play. Your status behaviors are "in firmware rather than consciously programmable software."

**Felt, played and perceived status** have almost nothing to do with each other fundamentally. A beggar, waiter or doorman can feel and play high while perceived low; a CEO who anxiously seeks approval feels and plays low while perceived high. Felt and played are the ones you control, giving four patterns (felt, then played):

- **LL** (feeling low, playing low): validating an "I suck" life position and enjoying moments of perverse vindication is easier than doing something about it; done through Berne-style games. Symptom: can't graciously accept a compliment ("Your lasagna turned out great!" "Oh, it's a little too salty."). Separate this from cultures where self-deprecation is the polite response.
- **LH** (feeling low, playing high): needs to "prove" the played status by lording it over someone nominally lower who is too constrained by situational rules to bite back. Classic sign: "being rude to waiters". Watch for it in a date or an interviewee; those situations bring it out.
- **HL** (feeling high, playing low): "gracious" behavior, considerate, scrupulously nice, alert to every chance to make you feel good, yet it reads as reverse flattery or *noblesse oblige* because the person is perceived high and doesn't mean to act truly low. Opening a door is symbolically low but an effective HL move done right ("door opening is probably the *e. coli* of status science").
- **HH** (feeling high, playing high): the rarest. Spoilt children; leaders and emperors on ceremonial occasions; beggars, with nowhere to go but up, who win psychologically if they make you feel low, money or no money; doormen, bouncers and waiters drawing derived status from a uniform.

**Absolute versus relative fixedness**: if the cause is intrinsic and psychological, the same pattern shows in *all* situations. If it is extrinsic and social, the pattern varies with perceived relative status (the other's perceived status minus your felt status). Both are predictable; relative personalities just need more data. There is no formula for telling which: watch the same person across many situations, with many others of varied relative status.

**Status matters if it matters.** It is a variable whose importance is a self-fulfilling prophecy: gravitate to locked patterns and you spend energy preserving them and can be manipulated. If status doesn't matter to you, it becomes a situational control variable against those to whom it does. Break locked felt-played patterns and felt status weakens, since it needs the nourishment of a projected, validated status. After enough years of breaking patterns unpredictably, felt status vanishes into a **status vacuum**; the firmware decays. Good actors (William H. Macy) play the whole status spectrum convincingly. Two status-vacuum people recognize each other, drop status manipulation and "spar with other weapons."

Failure mode: break patterns *predictably* and you lock in new ones. One man got hooked on pushing buttons and enjoying the reactions, which fed a felt-high pattern and made him manipulable through his button-pushing instinct. To break patterns randomly, play each situation for your situational objectives; those will stand in a random relation to any locked pattern.

**What status to play** depends on what you want the other person to do. Adapt to their predictable status behaviors:

- **Threaten** their projected status and they move to defend it.
- **Validate** it and they may express gratitude or ignore you.
- **Attack, then validate**: the "give people a way to save face" sequence.

Their reaction depends on how they perceive *your* status. Pick a position relative to the one they want to hold; which side, and how far above or below, depends on whether you attack or validate. Distance and sign both matter: to validate with compliments you can't be too far below (flattery isn't valued) or too far above (patronizing). Stay credible on the status signs the other reads (if they only see clothes, a slob can't do much). Don't shift far, because locked-status people can't parse status-shifters and respond with suspicion, distrust or confusion. Pick a position and hold it for the situation; shifting mid-situation is not a 101 skill.

Playing status right is "overwhelmingly important. You can do everything else right and play status wrong and you'll fail." You will play it wrong often, so spread risk across many situations and people. You can opt out only by staying inside a group whose locked patterns complement yours, in a small universe of situations. If your life means constantly meeting new people in unfamiliar situations and wanting different things from them: "Play status or crash." The unmanaged rate of complementary collisions is too low.

There is no turning back: shriveled status firmware can't be revived, and people you deal with a lot *will* know you for a status-player. Manage that by openly acknowledging it and making your underlying *values* understood and accepted. Unstated values get you seen as an "opportunistic, two-faced politician" (dangerous); projected values get you seen as "worldly wise" (safer).

## Show your true colors: the obvious wolf

Pick a villain style: the obvious wolf, evil from minute one, or the wolf in sheep's clothing who "shows his true colors" at the pivotal scene. Choose **"obvious wolf," but done intelligently.** Long-term sweetness is hard, painful, pointless acting, and unmasking is inevitable; "The mere fact that you put on a show is a hanging crime, socially."

Done right, showing your colors amplifies influence. (Possibly apocryphal: on *The Hunt for Red October* set Sean Connery blew up at a stagehand on purpose, so the other actors were tense and his entrance on the command deck seemed commanding.) "To wield influence, it pays to appear predictable in very simple ways around others. Fly your true colors high."

- Expressing yourself *completely* is childish: it publishes all your buttons for anyone to push.
- Identify a simple subset of your most natural behaviors and become very predictable to specific groups.
- Publish only buttons others fear to trigger: "the only buttons worth publishing are the ones others are afraid to push." Buttons others want to push get you manipulated, flattered, or pulled into a co-dependent mutual-reward-button-pushing loop.
- Temper is the bluntest such button. Subtler: a reputation for predictably asking a specific question ("do you have data to back that up?") and for mercilessly skewering those who answer badly. Once or twice is enough. Because the predictability is self-aware, you can override your published response when necessary.

## Small, honest moves

A **principal-agent problem** is an asymmetric situation in which the party you pay for a service also decides what is necessary and what to charge, so the agent is tempted to pad the bill (rife in car repair). A **small, honest act** wins a suspicious principal's trust: an anxious Rao expected a transmission bill in the hundreds or thousands and was charged $35 for an oil change, the cracked part replaced free.

An unscrupulous agent can use small honest moves to lull a suspicious principal, then "make out like a bandit later" when a better opportunity comes. Most uses are outright evil, so the main value is guarding against the move. Slightly evil uses exist, especially when the principal is a jerk who deserves it (Andy Dufresne in *The Shawshank Redemption*).

## The first day in prison

Prison lore: beat somebody up on your first day, or worse will happen to you. All organizations are psychic prisons, and so are apparently open networks: "prison is other people." Expect a first-day dynamic in parties, blogosphere niches, Facebook groups and soccer games, and at work, where doing anything significant draws opposition.

Three ways to deal with opposition:

1. **The recommended and stupid way**: engage it directly in a cooperative spirit. It works only for a genuine, easily cleared-up misunderstanding. Otherwise you get **death by consensus seeking**: a glacial pace, a project weighed down with crud added to please others, likely to die under unreasonable expectations. Consensus-building has its place in the playbook, rarely as a way past obstacles.
2. **The smart way**: acknowledge true conflict and decide obstacle by obstacle whether to go **through** or **around**.
   - *Through*: confront openly, and either win them over without conceding much or get a more powerful decision-maker to rule for you.
   - *Around*: misdirection, distraction, pre-emptive neutralization before they know what you are up to, sidelining, flattery, stealth, divide-and-conquer, **momentum judo** (accelerating their efforts to failure instead of resisting them), strategic leaks.
   - Going around is cheaper and less damaging. Rao almost never goes through; nor do most effective people.

**Exception: when you are new to a place, go through once.** Plowing through opposition shows willingness to fight, force of will and social intelligence about status hierarchies: make an example of some unfortunate opponent. Conditions:

- Show going-around and consensus-seeking behavior in parallel, so you aren't labeled a bull in a china shop.
- Pick a target nominally bigger than you (older, bigger paycheck, nicer title, better track record) who isn't well-liked or connected, but not so big that open conflict looks unhealthy for the *organization*. Set up and win an *underdog* fight in which you aren't too much of an underdog.
- Pick someone with line responsibilities. Going through is almost never right against staff bureaucrats.
- Plan to neutralize collateral damage, and make sure the right audience is watching.
- Don't start it. Wait for a legitimate provocation, ideally an instance of a behavior pattern everyone already objects to. A good strategy: wait for a provocation over email and produce a **calibrated over-reaction**.
- Don't rope in the people you expect to judge in your favor: show them you handle confrontation unaided and that impressing them isn't the point.
- Use weapons you know. Email suits only if you have email-fu: civil, understated surgical strikes with words.

## Work to rule

The labor movement's **work to rule**: stick religiously to job descriptions, policies and procedures. No stopping, no deliberate slowdown, no deliberate errors, no creative passive-aggressive obstruction. It often brings things to a standstill. It isn't even slightly evil.

It works because employers and employees conspire in the fiction that a corporation is defined rational roles running rational, sufficient procedures. The rules sit well below the minimum needed to run the business; even disengaged minimum-effort "losers" do more than the rules ask. Petty bureaucrats (the "clueless") are effectively in work-to-rule mode all the time, which is why they are parasites who earn their keep only when others use them as pawns.

The most dangerous threats a worker can make, intended or not:

- "Not in my job description."
- "I am just following the rules."
- "Our policy is..."
- "We are not allowed to work on weekends."
- "I am not authorized to do that."
- "I don't know what the policy is on that, I'll have to ask my manager." Especially choking: so much is undefined that the default rule (defer to your boss) chokes the boss if enough people invoke it.

Rules mostly protect and insure rather than enable and create. Best-faith people cite them only when unreasonable demands or risks threaten their protections; at any other time these lines signal micro-level work to rule.

Uses:

- **To block**: sticking to the rules is one of the easiest ways to block, slow or disrupt something you disagree with. "If you can't sustain an overt battle over something, don't fight; merely execute strictly according to the rules. Due diligence is a powerful weapon." The target can't easily fire or discipline you for following rules and must fall back on vague charges like "bad attitude" (one reason layoffs are popular: they need no reasons).
- **To lead**: protect a **sphere of improvisation** for your reports, a safe zone slightly beyond the roles and rules, and *accept the blame and responsibility*: they get the benefits of going beyond the rules, you take the risks. If your team can't escape consequences by saying "my manager said it was okay", you are not doing enough for them.

## Read people by what they consume

Confirmation bias means one of the easiest ways to figure someone out is the information they choose to consume. Humphrey Appleby knows newspapers "pander to their readers' prejudices"; only Jim Hacker grasps the implication, that each paper's readership is a type. Test yourself on your own country's papers, magazines, authors and channels: what person comes to mind? Then examine your own reading and the books you quote: how do you appear?

This is "not blatant stereotyping, it is blatant *archetyping*", a subtly different and more defensible typecasting that is right more often than wrong. Reading and viewing tastes are among the most robust signals because they are hard to fake: dress, mannerisms and body language can be put on, but nobody talks convincingly about books, shows and ideas they barely know. In an interview, ask the candidate to discuss what they recently read or watched; you'll detect faking even if you haven't read it. Ignore political correctness, boldly jump to conclusions, and be ready to self-correct.

## Candor, cursing and clarity

Newbies obsess over lying and lie detection to feed a romantic "gritty realist" self-image. That is dumb:

- Expert-grade lying and lie detection take years. Unless you are in law enforcement or spying, it is like buying assault rifles to barricade yourself in a crime-free suburb.
- Well-adjusted people rarely lie outright about anything consequential; getting caught is too risky. They mumble, talk in circles, avoid subjects, evade, deflect or equivocate, all easy to detect. The skill worth learning is challenging these behaviors without provoking anger.
- The everyday world is "slightly timid, risk-averse and benign". Lying lives at the extremes: harmless white lies at one end; professional, risk-managed, money-making lying (marketers, cops, con-men, spies) at the other; plus a handful of big institutional lies.

The skill that matters is telling people who are telling the truth from people who *think* they are. Two **pseudo-truth-telling behaviors**:

- **Candor**: leaning back, "let me be completely honest here", sincere and disarming. *They believe what they are saying.* Routinely candid people can't bear the stress of evasion; candor relieves the pressure of bottled-up, emotionally loaded ideas. Truth-telling requires separating feelings from facts and telling *yourself* the truth first; the candid blurt unprocessed thoughts. Find the truth by guessing their feelings and correcting for them: listen like a therapist.
- **Cursing**: an angry opinion seasoned with curses ("That asshole was out to screw us all along! We got played."). Facts fused with emotions, sliding into aggressive posturing under threat. The narrative bolsters sagging confidence, seeks validation, and simplifies unpleasant realities in self-serving ways ("being 'blunt' blunts the truth"). Expect key details to be missing.

Both come from internal narratives grounded in unprocessed denial and rationalization; you are being invited into a fiction built for self-protection, and it is tempting because every sign of truth-telling is present.

**Genuine truth-telling feels like clarity.** The speaker has processed their thoughts, separated fact from feeling and the known from the new, and offers something deduced to be both true and unknown to you; you feel a moment of expanded clarity. Candor and cursing provoke emotional responses instead. In rhetorical terms: separate the *logos* from the unconscious *ethos* and *pathos*.

## Cold-blooded listening

"Cold-blooded listening is, for slightly evil sociopaths, what nice, good-natured 'active listening' is for losers." The model scene is in *Glengarry Glen Ross*. The morning after the office burglary, the washed-up salesman Levene rants at the manager Williamson, and in the rant tells him that if he's going to make something up he should be sure it helps. Williamson asks how Levene knew he made it up. He had told a customer the contracts had gone to the bank; for once he had left them on his desk, and only the burglar could know. "You robbed the office."

What Williamson does:

- Insults and *ad hominems* roll off him. He is confident in his self-assessment and feels no need to defend himself or even acknowledge the rant.
- He *does* listen, and picks out the *one* piece of actual information, let slip by accident, that is useful to him.
- He tests it as a hypothesis by following the clue and watching Levene fumble before revealing his conclusion, and he never explains the question. Like any good alpha, he assumes he owes no explanation and will get a revealing answer without giving one.

The skill goes beyond thick skin, to hardened-cop "anything you say can and will be used against you" listening. It requires:

- **Impassivity through practice.** The only route to total impassivity under strident criticism and insult is practice, and it means developing "a certain capacity for contempt."
- **Contemptuous but clinical listening.** Don't treat what is said about your personality as feedback worth answering, but keep listening, objectively, "like a researcher observing an angry animal in a cage." Prime your radar for information useful to you, not information the other party *thinks* you ought to know and is maliciously delighted to deliver.
- **Listening is not agreeing or debating.** Listen, draw your own conclusions, and walk away, or steer the conversation onto terms useful to you. You needn't convince them of your conclusions or even share them.

Most people's opinions, especially on *your* personality, can be ignored; you are shooting for effectiveness, so don't expect to be liked as well.

This is not a defense mechanism. Two errors under criticism: (1) taking it as true without analysis and earnestly trying to change (born of low self-esteem); (2) "explain it away", deciding it says more about the critic than about you (born of denial). Cold-blooded listening requires high self-awareness and self-acceptance, so that conversations can be processed in *transactional* terms (what information of value is here, in this situation) instead of by defending a fragile ego against poisoned barbs from inconsequential people. An ego-defending Williamson would have missed the clue.
