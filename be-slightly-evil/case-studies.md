# Case studies

Rao's worked cases, each applying the playbook to one everyday situation. Reach here for a peer treating you as a subordinate or a hostile email thread at work ("Pistols, at dawn"), free-agent anxiety and "am I doing enough?" ("Napping in the trenches"), and the small status rituals of hallways, doors and queues.

## Pistols, at dawn

K, a single mom who put herself through graduate school while working as an admin assistant, became a sales analyst on big bear-hunting sales teams at a Fortune 500 company. A senior salesman, J, began treating her like a secretary through innocuous "requests" ("could you add this note to slide 12"). She didn't report to J; both reported to T, a laissez-faire manager; the team ran on "collaborative" group email with tactical cc'ing and backchannel sidebars. She complied, got stuck with her old career's tasks, and was shut out of the work she had trained for. After an egregious email she asked how to reply so as to "fix" things and get the respect she deserved.

Rao's analysis:

- There were many better replies than the ones she'd thought of, but none would work. She lacked the email-fu to sustain an elevated sparring match without continued babysitting, and email-fu can't be learned in the heat of battle. A one-off tactical email would only prolong the misery.
- She forgot **the first rule of dueling**: "as the challenged party, you need to exercise your prerogative to choose the time, place and manner of combat." That is the great advantage of being the reactor rather than the instigator. "Don't choose a drunken fist-fight in a bar simply because that's where the challenge was thrown down. If pistols at dawn work better for you, choose those."
- The problem wasn't really J, the tactically superior email-fu warrior. It was K failing to see the consequences of working, at her skill level, under a lazy, conflict-avoiding manager hiding poor team management behind *laissez-faire*. She faced default unfavorable perceptions: female in a male-driven workplace, a known secretary history, ingrained and easily triggered compliant admin behaviors. Fighting one J and one email at a time was impossibly hard.
- **Email** suits small, real-time perception corrections, a couple of sentences mixed judiciously into routine communication. It is a terrible medium for large perception corrections or for setting favorable defaults.
- **The ideal what-if**: a backchannel chat with T *early*, at the very *first* sign of the secretary perception, suggesting that T clarify roles, responsibilities and expectations at the next planning meeting. That is one **crucial conversation** instead of a hundred email-fu fights between a green belt and a black belt.
- **How to hold a perception-setting conversation**: the wrong way is honest explanation of the specific grievance ("J is treating me like a secretary"). It puts you in "complaining to Mommy" mode, which managers, especially laissez-faire ones, hate and handle terribly, and it risks poking at dangerous interpersonal histories you may not know about (J and T's). *One* right way, at K's level of skill and information: flatter the manager's management-style conceits and make an abstract suggestion: "Really like your style, and how you don't micromanage us; I expect you're going to do a basic roles/expectations thing at the next planning meeting, and then put us on autopilot?" This won't always work: a different T may be too lazy or scared, may need more hints, or may be smart and thin-skinned enough to resent the back-seat driving and the transparent "you've probably already thought of this" attribution. The point is that you can prepare, pick the right attack and make it count without juggling operational details in real time. Avoid having to solve an interpersonal meta-problem inside a reply to a specific email.
- Success would have set favorable initial conditions: room to build a real "sales analyst" perception, a more controlled environment for learning email-fu, a handicap on people like J for whom laissez-faire is an advantage, and groundwork for any later intervention by T.
- For K it was too late: she had been labeled a secretary type and, through her actions, signaled assent. The damage isn't irreversible: with the next team, read the members accurately and run the **early context-setting** strategy. A band-aid reply to J was still needed, understood as a band-aid, not a solution. (The band-aid is a small, real-time correction of the kind email suits, such as the calibrated pushback against a nominal peer who "delegates" by email, in [orientation-and-decision.md](orientation-and-decision.md).)

The moral: "you can't always pick your battles, but you *can* choose when, where, how and with whom to fight." Isolated tactical problems are very rare. If you are framing a situation as "how do I respond to this email" or "what do I say in this meeting", you are probably misframing a broader problem and "heading towards your personal Vietnam". There are no formulas:

1. Analyze the complete situation and context with eyes wide open, informed by self-awareness of your own limits and an understanding of the others' personalities.
2. Choose deliberately when, where and whom to "work" to get where you need to be.
3. Turn the analysis into action through a mix of immediate band-aids and well-timed strategic interventions.
4. Expect to fail often anyway, since some uncertainties can't be modeled, and course-correct at multiple levels with feedback.

This approach is **"investment" rather than "spending"**: instead of every interaction starting from square one, you accumulate a favorable reputation and a repertoire of practiced skills, "like gaining new weapons faster than you lose lives in a video game."

## Napping in the trenches

Kai, 26, recently self-employed after directing marketing at a construction company, covers expenses of about $2,000 a month with 15 hours a week of consulting and side projects, exactly what he wanted, yet his days fill with dread that he should be working harder, and part of his head keeps saying he should be freaked out. The dread is the heart of the matter; the rest is situational detail. Everyone has felt it: "If you haven't experienced it, you're either lying or clueless."

**The scripted/unscripted × failure/success 2x2.** In the paycheck world, scripts tell you how to behave on success and failure: marriage, a VP promotion, a first kid and a big bonus come with conditioned rewards and social support, like trading arcade tickets for a fixed menu of prizes; failure comes with standard consolation prizes, safety nets and coping scripts. You can tell when you are lucky or unlucky because you have a **narrative frame of reference** to calibrate your responses, built from others' experience. In the unscripted free-agent world there are few behaviors to imitate and few social cues. Failure is simple (run out of money, return to the scripted world or spiral down). The trouble is *success*: enough to continue indefinitely, plus a small discretionary surplus. Paycheck scripts are discredited, or unavailable (try getting a mortgage on volatile income). Retirement calculators are useless when personal and business money mix, and you constantly choose between options the paycheck world seals apart, such as a hunch about Netflix stock versus an idea for an app. Radical success doesn't help: "this is too good to last; am I making enough hay while the sun is shining?"; "where can I safely put this cash I am spinning right now?"

**The key word is *insurance*.** It drives script-building for anyone who lacks a script for the rewards of success. Diminishing marginal utility doesn't apply to money because one category of uses, buying insurance, never loses value however much you have: a free tetanus shot, then health insurance, then life insurance, then college savings; new projects, worry about projects reaching end-of-life, diversification; fear that dollar devaluation will wipe out a $5 million stash. It also hides as **experience maximization**: fear of missing out on *good* things (the bucket list) is insurance thinking too, since with infinite time missing out wouldn't worry you.

Why isn't insurance bought with dollar *n*+1 worth less than with dollar *n*? In an open world you can't estimate scenario probabilities and buy cover in order of likelihood: unknown unknowns aren't even on the list, and even known risks can only be lowered relatively (avoid flying and a plane hits your house; build a bunker and the crash releases a toxin that seeps in). The Indian fable: a king's boon makes him unkillable by day or night, by human or beast, indoors or outdoors, and he is killed by a half-man-half-lion at dusk on a doorstep.

So the process never ends, and the rich are usually *more* careful with money than the poor: luxury spending is just the lifestyle, and the bulk of their wealth goes into increasingly complex insurance to *preserve* it. "The richer you get, the more lifestyle design becomes lifestyle insurance": hedge funds, mountain cabins, offshore accounts, a jet on standby to a secret island, cryogenics, and the holy grail of immortality-seeking (Rockefeller striving to live past a hundred and dying at 98; Kurzweil's reported 150 supplements a day). The rich friend who buys a $1000 bottle of wine but won't give you $1000 for your app is protecting a designed lifestyle: the ability to keep drinking that wine through a zombie apocalypse. That $1000 isn't in the diminishing-utility zone; it represents millions in *expected costs* across the scenarios being insured against (the bunker cellar, the anti-aircraft guns, the reactor, the fallout shelter for the reactor). We all think this way, on smaller scales. Without a "reasonable" story (one everybody else follows), anxieties are limited only by imagination. The scripted have the same anxieties but find comfort in numbers; collective scripts are self-limiting, and if everyone around you accepts a pattern of insurance, however irrational, you feel safe accepting it and relaxing.

**The trench soldier.** A WWI soldier lives cold, wet, bored and diseased, with death ever present. Can he take a nap? Yes: even there you hit a cognitive and physical limit, stop devoting resources to survival, let go, and nap. The human condition is the same for everyone: every future script ends with your death. So the answer to "*am I doing enough?*" is *always* no; there is always more, all the way up to the best available immortality scheme of your lifetime, and mortality frames your behavior whether you notice or not (the soldier, the millionaire with his wine, the "if it's Tuesday, it must be Brussels" experience-maximizer). "Just because there is always more you can do – and you should, it is the definition of being alive in a way – does not mean you cannot take a nap." Take the nap; you can fret about the next dollar of insurance when you wake in 45 minutes.

## Hallway Chicken

The little dance of getting past someone coming the other way in a hallway or lobby. Unlike road chicken, you win by giving way *first*, but in a status-enhancing way that looks gracious. Each encounter is a small social win or loss.

The key: "*slow your movements down to below the walking tempo of the oncomer.*" Slow movement signals higher status, and it also solves the collision-avoidance problem efficiently. The analogy is dropped phone calls before call waiting: if both parties call back at once, both get busy signals; the fix is to wait a random period and a longer one after each busy signal, which is how engineers design back-off protocols for re-establishing dropped network connections. Whoever keeps dancing rapidly side to side is more likely to end up taking the right of way, generally the lower-status outcome. Encounters start with matched tempos because people walk at the flow's pace or faster, never slower, so train yourself to slow instantly, or walk with slower, longer strides.

## Door-holding and illegible queues

Readers' analyses of the status dynamics of holding doors:

- **EB's 3-second rule**: hold the door if the person behind is within 3 seconds of it. You lose status by failing to hold within 3 seconds or by holding too long. A person held for is expected to walk faster, even if loaded down, and loses status by not speeding up. Male behind male: the rule is fairly rigid; female behind male: relaxed to about 5 seconds; follower carrying too much to open the door: greatly relaxed. In a stream, once you hold the door, the next male behind is obligated to take over until relieved by another male or the stream ends, and loses status by failing. (Rao doubts that the non-speeder and the stream-shirker really lose status.)
- **BE, swing direction and air locks**: in 1:1 encounters the direction the door swings, combined with prior relative status, decides whether you should open or be opened for. With more people and doors everything changes, and the trickiest case is the air-lock vestibule (doors, a few steps, more doors). Many women don't know to step aside in the vestibule so the man can reach and open the second door; on a date status can be lost by fumbling this dance. The **air-lock slam dunk**: a fast walker slows enough to let a single companion pass, then steps aside so they open the second door for him too, an unambiguous status statement; in a larger group he drops to third position so he is first fully through without holding any door. If someone stands waiting for you to open the second door, re-level by going through it and walking on without holding it.
- **G, make the favor explicit**: hold the door briefly, then transfer the effort to someone (preferably male) in the other party as they come through, with a genuine smile and "Got it?", and if the group is appealing, engage them once inside. The favor is explicit, they can't help but follow your direction (a small status loss whenever a man does what a stranger tells him), and you are the one offering politeness, speaking first, and holding the option to reopen the interaction: the prerogative of higher status.
- **RB, culture matters**: a Southerner raised to hold doors for anyone, using about 20 yards as the threshold. Asked whether he is holding the door because she's a woman: "No, Ma'am. I'm holding the door for you because I'm a gentleman."
- **KB, natural time constant rule** (Rao finds it more sophisticated than the 3-second rule): going the same way, hold only if the other will reach the door before it would naturally fall closed. A moment of graciousness gains status; looking too eager to be gracious loses it; not holding leaves a poor impression. Going opposite ways, always defer; if both defer, let the other be gracious to avoid an impasse, with a minor status gain because you offered and you do actually have somewhere to be.

Rao's own five lazy heuristics:

1. Door pulls open towards you and people are coming the other way: step aside and hold it.
2. Door pulls open on their side: wait for them to pass through; if one of them holds it, go through (rare; people handle one-way traffic in batches).
3. People behind you, whichever way it opens: if they are right behind, do the "relay handoff" (hold the door propped open while passing through, not from the side); if they are more than a few paces back, give it an extra-hard shove so the swing-shut is delayed. That is a bit callous, since they must hurry, but you get points for trying without slowing down.
4. With a group you know, out in front: hold it for everybody. If someone else is holding it, pass through with a nod and never offer to take over.
5. Old, physically disabled or over-burdened people: always hold it. That is a practical reciprocity norm, not a status ritual; failing it looks socially inept and childishly inconsiderate.

The two-door vestibule has no elegant solution; it is just physically awkward.

**Illegible queues** (an exercise Rao leaves open): third in line for coffee behind someone standing too far back, so the line's direction is unclear, Rao watches a self-important-looking man stand in the "ambiguous zone" where an inattentive cashier won't know who is next. The move is just short of cutting in line: an ambiguous challenge, an attempt to fork the queue with plausible deniability ("Oh, I didn't realize you were in line"). It works best where several lines converge on one service point, where creating and manipulating "queue illegibility" gets you a couple of places ahead. Crowded countries queue closer together, so what looks like cutting in a high-personal-space country can be a genuine misunderstanding from a low-personal-space one. The questions to work through: how does this move's risk/reward structure differ from outright cutting, and what defenses are available?
