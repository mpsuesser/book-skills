# Action and disrupting an adversary

The Act stage of OODA and the tactics for disrupting an adversary. Reach here when turning an opponent's argument against them, facing or using bureaucratic stalling, deciding how much of an advantage to use or what kind of deception is defensible, containing a failure, deliberately annoying someone, neutralizing a "data-driven" blocker, defusing someone who storms up angry, interrupting, or dodging a decision whose options are all bad.

## Hoisting by their own petard

The favorite tactic of many effective people, and "the grand-daddy" of slightly evil tactics, since many others derive from it. A petard is a small French bomb for blowing up fortifications; the engineer lighting it was sometimes caught in the ropes and hoisted up with it. Today the phrase means using somebody's own arguments against them, like using an opponent's momentum in martial arts.

Bob blocks Alice's small-group decision because he "cannot support back-room machinations" and wants the all-hands meeting. A week later Bob needs Alice to sign his purchase order; she refers it to the committee: "I thought you were all about open and transparent processes?"

Why it works:

- In civilized settings we pretend everyone is reasonable and argue on the surface. Human reasoning is not mechanical: we pull favored argument patterns from a playbook in memory, in near stimulus-response fashion, often learned by imitation and never examined, and associated with having "won" before.
- Winning is rarely about facts or truth. It is about ending the interaction in a preferred status relationship. Real arguments do nothing to status (a chess win raises you among geeks and lowers you among jocks), so an argument that scores a status win is probably a rationalization.
- **The test**: you needn't find where reasoning stops and rationalization begins. Just note whether an argument moves status. If it does, file it away: "used in reverse, it will cause the reverse status change."
- The tactic works because the argument pattern is *weak*, not strong. You can challenge it the moment you spot it and enter a long argument, or file it away as a petard for later.
- The opponent won't attack the reversed argument, since that would undermine their own earlier use. (This is how cultures of consensus grow around bullshit arguments nobody wants to call out. Sitcom plots run on the resulting status see-saws.)

Raise your own game: use weak logic only in petard-hoist defenses, and build your own arguments carefully, avoiding any that could be turned on you later. That takes honesty and self-awareness and one key question: "am I making this argument to achieve a favorable status outcome for myself or to arrive at a real truth?" Status goals are "the bread and butter of Slightly Evil thinking", but reason is a dangerous, double-edged tool in status games: use other tools. Rule of thumb: useless arguments that move status without discovering truth are most often applications of unexamined "values", which are rarely profound moral positions and more often "a crutch for lazy thinkers."

## Crisis non-response (creative inertia)

*Yes, Minister* and *Yes, Prime Minister* paint an inverted MacLeod hierarchy: Members of Parliament are the losers, ministers the clueless, the civil service the sociopaths (though Hacker sometimes wins by a streak of sociopathy and the civil service sometimes loses through cluelessness). In "A Victory for Democracy" the Foreign Office uses its standard four-stage **creative inertia** response to any crisis:

1. We say nothing is going to happen.
2. We say that something may be going to happen, but we should do nothing about it.
3. We say that maybe we should do something about it, but there's nothing we can do.
4. We say that maybe there was something we could have done but it's too late now.

Use it when you want to avoid action. The best counter is a *fait accompli* (Hacker sends an airborne battalion to the island on a "goodwill mission" through the Defense Ministry before the Foreign Office notices). Arguing at any stage means you have already lost, because the strategy's whole point is to waste time until it is too late. If you use the stall yourself, watch your flanks.

## The hierarchy of deceptions

Functioning as a human requires a minimum level of deception, and the more complex your life, the higher it is (Santa Claus for a quiet family; far more for a President). The path of slightly evil deception "begins with your attitude towards lying, but ends up at Russian roulette."

**Principle of conservation of deception**: "*at any given level of moral and intellectual development, there is an associated minimum level of deception in your life.*" Professed absolute honesty invariably turns out to hide self-deception. If you aren't deceiving others, you are likely deceiving yourself, or in denial. You lower your level of deception only through further intellectual and moral development: you *earn* higher levels of truth, and that takes intelligence, not pious intentions.

**Anatomy of deception.** The question arises when you have a skill or information advantage over another decision-maker. How you use it depends on four things: alignment of intentions, your relationship with the other party, the relative value of a win to each side, and your degree of moral certainty about your intentions. At one extreme (aligned, close, wanting them to win) a parent lies to simplify a problem for a child and secretly helps them succeed; at the other (fully adversarial, more to gain for you) you pull out all the stops. Deception is very nearly amoral (white lies, nurturing lies, society's polite fictions; destructive lies), but it is fundamentally friendlier to evil, "so any use of deception is at least slightly evil."

**The hierarchy**, least to most sophisticated:

1. **Outright lying** and fabrication of evidence.
2. **Misdirection**: no lie, but you foreground a pattern of true information likely to lead to false conclusions.
3. **Withholding of information**: no lie or misdirection, but you share nothing you don't have to.
4. **Equivocation**: sharing information ambiguously. It keeps plausible deniability against charges of lying, misdirection or withholding, and relies on the other party's predisposition to draw certain conclusions.
5. **Not-correcting-others**: none of the above, but when others draw false conclusions you could correct, or miss inferences obvious to you, you selectively don't help. If they make no mistakes and miss nothing, you have given away your entire advantage.

Going up the hierarchy brings progressive minimalism and growing social, moral and legal defensibility (outright lying can be perjury; not-correcting-others appears entirely defensible). The less sophisticated forms are harder to justify, which is reason enough to climb. The sophisticated forms take less energy, both to use and to cover up if discovered, but more skill. They can also help: "When you get good at not-correcting-others in helpful ways, you become a good teacher."

**Moral certainty and Russian roulette.** Deception needs moral certainty about your intentions to be justifiable, yet the question comes up precisely when you aren't sure you are right. Model it: with a decisive advantage you win for sure if you use it and lose for sure if you give it all away; cede half and you leave the outcome to fate. The rule: "give away the advantage in direct proportion to the amount of doubt you feel." It is fair in a cosmic sense, and it keeps moral doubt from paralyzing you into not acting. This is the appeal of the movie villain who plays Russian roulette when he could simply shoot. Rao: "I almost never lie outright these days. To the extent possible, I try to turn moral ambiguity into Russian roulette."

## The art of damage control

Slightly evil means the gambler's life: bets and risks, bigger rewards than drones who merely work hard, and occasional failure. That raises one of the worst pieces of advice going: **"You must be willing to look foolish."** It turns the manageable problem of damage control into a holy cross to be passively borne, a pious atonement for the sin of risk-taking. It belongs to "you must accept the consequences", and no, you shouldn't. That line leads to taking one for the team, *hara kiri* and captains going down with the ship: martyrdom fit for real battlefields, which are rare. "Most of the time, nobody needs to die."

- Accept that failure is imminent the moment the signs are clear, and stop fighting for success. But don't switch into noble passivity; you are not a defendant awaiting trial (unless you've broken the law). Shift rapidly into **damage control mode**.
- Nobody is watching to see karmic justice done. There are gleeful onlookers enjoying their *schadenfreude*, evil and slightly evil people hunting for opportunity in your fall, well-meaning sympathizers who tempt you into passivity when you need to act, for a big enough failure an angry mob, and innocent, hapless fallout victims who will pose a moral quandary.
- As a principal (if you aren't prone to denial) you see failure coming before others do, so you have the most time and control over consequences, including how, where and when others find out and how they react.

**Damage control** means "predicting the unmanaged course of events, designing interventions to minimize fallout, and optimally distributing the residual impact among all exposed parties." Trade off impact on trust, credibility and future opportunities; salvage material assets; and decide how foolish you can afford to look. Looking foolish is serious business, "the most damaging" consequence: reputations take long to build and minutes to lose, and while assets, trust and credibility can be rebuilt, the "foolish" pigeonhole is very hard to climb out of. You might buy back 10 units of lost trust with 1.5 units of looking foolish; such calculations must be made. The do-nothing defaults will be unfavorable, especially since others shift into active damage control the moment they find out, even if you don't. If you are really smart, the optimal hit isn't negative at all: you play the failure so that you escape adverse consequences and perhaps come out looking good.

It is slightly evil because it slopes towards true evil. "Success has many parents, while failure is an orphan": on a win you fight for as large a share as you can (for yourself or those you represent); on a failure you dissipate consequences as widely and as far from yourself as possible. True evil lies in the power to make innocents, scapegoats and fall guys, bear the worst. For many, damage control is just finding who can most safely be blamed, and those easiest to hurt and last to find out are usually the most innocent (though rarely is anyone truly innocent; every stakeholder is complicit to some degree). You cannot rise nobly above the blame game: "When a true failure looms, you must play or be played." Draw your own lines in the sand. You may choose to shoulder more than your share out of altruism, but make it calculated rather than clueless altruism.

## Annoying others

Wanting to be liked must be overcome on the slightly evil path. A stiff-faced smile under criticism isn't enough; you must get comfortable actively provoking dislike and dealing with it. Three stages:

1. **Learn to be annoying with no purpose in mind**: the learning, practice and play stage.
2. **The danger zone**: getting addicted to button-pushing *schadenfreude* for the hell of it. Feeding self-esteem by baiting others is as limiting as fishing for compliments; both idioms come from fishing because they are behaviors at the same level. The addicted become lifelong contrarians and trolls-without-a-cause; smart people stop taking the bait and the contrarians are left baiting idiots.
3. **Selective, calibrated annoyance for good reason.** "People who are in an annoyed state behave more predictably than those who are in a non-annoyed state, where they are actually thinking." When you need to stop someone thinking too much about something and the more benign methods (flattery, distraction, avoidance) have failed, escalate to annoying them.

**Annoy through strengths, not weaknesses.** Attacking weaknesses doesn't annoy, it insults, buying anger and resentment that may come back as vindictiveness. Annoyance through a strength makes people frustrated with themselves rather than angry at you; annoyance fades (unless you reinforce it into a permanent state) while insults are carved in stone; and it neutralizes their *most* effective behaviors when those don't serve you. This is part of a broader rule: "all arrested development is caused by strengths, not weaknesses". Get too good at something, become addicted to its rewards, and your behavior around that strength turns predictable, however effective.

Choose strength vectors on which you are much weaker than the target, or can appear much weaker because they underestimate you. All annoyance tactics derive from the *natural* behaviors of stupid, illogical, uncreative and unintuitive people and rely on the Dunning-Kruger effect. In natural form they are poorly timed lash-outs at threats to self-esteem; in deliberate form they are used for specific effects.

## Derailing the data-driven

The Whitehall bureaucrats around Humphrey Appleby kept Jim Hacker between a rock and a hard place: flood him with so much information he couldn't find what he needed, or withhold so much it wasn't there to find, combining filtration with distraction by irrelevant information. "In information wars, filters are power and useless data are weapons."

When agendas diverge, whoever controls the data flows into a decision usually wins. Two levels of work:

1. **Frame the decision**, which sets which data count as relevant. This is the stronger lever: misframe a decision and you send someone down an irrelevant bunny trail with an illusion of choice. If parents' opinions matter more than teachers' on a question, encourage your opponent to survey teachers, preferably with a detailed survey at a teacher conference in six months rather than a quick online one next week.
2. **Hide some data and exaggerate the importance of other data.** If you can't distract someone from the important data flows entirely, use **data judo**: they end up doing the wrong things with the right data.

Paper-era version: boxes of papers each night, the important ones deep in the last box (as defendants do with class-action discovery), signed late at night without reading. Hacker's mistake was delegating the definition of "important" to his nominal underlings: overloaded, he asked for only the *most* important papers; feeling excluded, he demanded *everything*. There is no real protection against people with more direct access to data (you are the lower riparian state on the river), but you can set explicit **relevance criteria**: "send me everything about issue X, but leave me out of the loop on issue Y." Hiding information from a relevance filter is harder than hiding it from a generic importance filter.

**The clueless data-driven decision-maker (CDDD)**, Hacker's successor since "analytics" caught on around 2006:

- Often has a process-discipline background such as six sigma.
- Loves anything with statistical cachet ("A/B testing", "ARIMA model").
- Has rudimentary training in statistics and probability.
- Conflates more sophisticated analysis with more useful results.
- Confuses precision with accuracy: worries about data quality and forgets data relevance.
- Is a **formula geek** rather than someone who looks at specific numbers: hazy on the concepts behind regression or SQL but good at *using* the tools (running regressions, fitting curves, talking R-squared, routine queries and reports).
- Is far more confident in this clueless state than paper-era predecessors, mistaking skill with more powerful tools for greater personal competence (like a driver feeling surer of their sense of direction than a walker).

CDDDs lack taste about when and how to use the tools, a sense of what data is missing, and the ability to improvise. They think "drilling down" means more detailed reports and finer slicing. Really drilling down is acting like a detective following an instinctive trail of questions from clues that seem significant. "Intuition in data-driven thinking doesn't vanish, it merely moves from the answers to the questions." Being *intelligently* data-driven is asking the right questions at the right time, which takes hard thinking and timing, not technique. After a CDDD team's thirty-minute data dump, the smart executive asked the ONE question that reframed the issue and made all the analysis irrelevant.

CDDD failure modes:

1. **Time and data**: more data is useful only if it is generated and intelligently analyzed *faster than options are expiring* on a ticking clock.
2. **Drunkard-and-keys effect**: looking in the glare of wherever data is available rather than where data is needed to lower risk.
3. **Going beyond "roughly right"**: if an eyeballed graph trends roughly up in a straight line and that's good enough for the decision, complicated math about exactly how straight is wasted. The test: "can ANY possible conclusion from this mathematical refinement exercise actually swing the decision the other way?" If no, the work is not worth doing.
4. **Sampling framing**: they grasp the technical side (randomness, i.i.d., true random versus chain versus convenience samples) but not the simpler framing questions. A technically perfect A/B test is useless if you are asking the wrong question.

All four come from failing to *think* at the pre-technical level: asking the right questions and examining underlying assumptions and hypotheses, a stage with no formulas or processes.

**Against a CDDD who is in your way**: the digital too-much/too-little dump still works (a massive Excel sheet; a query interface that can do far too much and is built to drill down in the wrong directions). The deeper lever: CDDDs act out of risk aversion but are hazy about *what data reduce what risks and uncertainties*, and their risk aversion is *absolute*, demanding the same certainty for every decision whether or not enough information exists. So they hurry to the technical parts, which feel like progress. Encourage their quest for a false sense of security and hurry them into technical exercises, one technique per failure mode:

1. **Time**: if the decision defaults to an option you like when left unmade, suggest deferring it until more data is in. If the analysis supports you, you look smart; if not: "it's too late now, we're already committed. Second-guessing now will be very costly."
2. **Drunkard-and-keys**: if the data needed to move the decision out of your gut is unavailable or too expensive, find the most convenient red-herring data source and suggest the CDDD study *that*, ideally by chairing a *committee* to study it.
3. **Roughly right**: if all you care about is "up", suggest the most complex technique available ("we really need to do a logistic regression here; there may be some implications if it turns out we are on an S-curve"). They will happily study the technique or hunt for an expert. Sending people on useless learning missions and digital wild-goose chases ("black swan chases") is among the best ways to distract them from substantive issues.
4. **Sampling**: a real thinker settles qualitative, narrative questions ("are women really the target market here?") before technical ones ("is this i.i.d?"). To distract a CDDD from the important questions about a sample, scare them with methodological ones: "Are we *really* sure this time series is i.i.d?"

Using these techniques wastes talent. If at all possible, point a CDDD where they can do good; their false confidence (tool competence mistaken for insight about data) often makes that impossible.

## Rebooting conversations

A store manager returns from lunch, frappuccino in hand, and a customer who has waited fifteen minutes over a botched return launches into an angry outburst. He waits for a pause and takes charge: "No, no, NO. That is not the way. Let's start again. Hi! My name is ___, and you are?" Forced to restart with introductions, she is embarrassed at having railed at a stranger without checking whether he deserved it. This is the extreme case, like sidestepping a raging bull.

The common case is the **soft reboot**. Trigger: someone (the "bull") comes up to you unexpectedly in an emotionally charged state, most often anger, fear or sullenness, in casual, work or professional settings where you have no particular obligation to be nurturing (not with spouses, kids or parents).

- "Whoa, whoa, calm down" or "time out, time out!" are dangerous: they implicitly accept responsibility for being the calm adult and give the bull permission to continue. The emotionally violent deliberately exploit this against people whose desire for peace and harmony is a known weakness.
- What you want is reboot control without responsibility for the overall calm, only your own. Leave the bull responsible for their emotions, and take responsibility for the situation only when YOU decide to, even if that means letting it spiral with "nobody in charge" for a while.

**The trick**: "*you repeat all or part of their opening line, but with zero emotional content. Deadpan.*" It works about 80% of the time. Sometimes turn the assertion into a question, by interrogative intonation or a minimal word substitution:

- "HAVE YOU HEARD? THIS IS BULLCRAP!" / "This is bullcrap?"
- "THEY'VE MOVED MY DESK TO THE BASEMENT!" / "They've moved your desk to the basement?"
- "I am screwed." / "You're screwed?"

Why it works: a charged stimulus makes your emotional reaction race ahead and censor the options your cognition generates. The reaction may be empathetic (you mirror the emotion as an ally), sympathetic (you calm and moderate before deciding whether you want responsibility for the blow-up) or complementary (cringing before rage). Repetition is cognitively lightweight and fast enough to prevent the emotional hijacking; doing it drained of emotion takes some nerve but is straightforward to practice. The bull usually hears it as a request to elaborate, and elaboration takes coherent thought, which slows them down. Your neutral repetition replaces their charged opening as the stimulus you respond to, so your own thinking is less constrained, and you haven't accepted responsibility before deciding you want it. When it fails, the bull displaces the emotion onto you ("don't ... just repeat what I say, I am MAD", "How can you just sit there?"), but you have still bought a favorable starting point.

## How to interrupt

From Keith Johnstone's *Impro*. The ineffective way: wait until the speaker finishes the point you object to, form your reply, then cut in with "excuse me, but..." or "sorry for interrupting, but...".

The effective way:

- Interrupt *as soon as* you've roughly understood that an objectionable point is being made, possibly before the speaker finishes it, and *before* you've decided what to say.
- Think out loud, "Aaaaaahhhhhhhhh!" or "Ehhhhummmmmm!", clearly, stretching the sound over several seconds until the speaker stops and looks at you.
- Make it patently clear that *you haven't yet decided what to say and are thinking about it*: look up, down or into the distance, as when absorbed in thought, not at the person you interrupted.
- Don't stage it; smart people see through an artificial version. *Actually* start at the *real* right moment, set by the content being said rather than by forming the intention "I need to stop this", then tune out the room and shape your response. Your timing tells the other smart people whether you know what you're doing or are faking.

Why it works (Johnstone): status. Important people are confident enough to say, in effect, "I disagree, but I am important enough that you should all shut up and wait while I figure out why, even if it means wasting 10 seconds of everybody's time." Rhetorically, if you wait until you've formed a reply, the speaker has already used the point, moved on and built momentum. You needn't look or sound superconfident, but any uncertainty you show must belong to the real-time *thinking*, never to doubts about whether the interruption is socially appropriate (Vincent D'Onofrio's Robert Goren in *Law and Order: Criminal Intent* looks fumbling and uncertain yet communicates mastery).

How evil is it? Good when you are shooting down a truly flawed and stupid point: you save everyone time and spare an idiot embarrassment. Slightly evil when you stop a shaky argument only to build an *equally* shaky one in its place, ensuring your smarter tactics win because you aren't sure substance will. Plain evil by whatever standards let you sleep at night: "I am not your priest. Draw your own lines in the sand." It can backfire at the wrong time, place or status situation; a fairly safe setting is a meeting you control substantively and procedurally (you chair it and have dictatorial authority over the decisions).

## Dodging decisions: emotional charging

People detect question evasion well. Its cousin, **dodging decisions**, gets less attention: when a decision is framed so every option is bad, replace it with another decision that lets you do what you want with far less blowback.

In the first season of *24*, Jack Bauer asks to question a critically injured girl, the last person to see his missing daughter. The "father" answers: "Let me go in and check if she is up to it first." A straight "no" invites argument. Changing "can I question her?" into "let me judge if she's up to it" creates a safe way to say no later. "Up to it" is a judgment call with wide latitude, "a subjective judgement masquerading as a data-driven one" that even doctors would struggle to override for a parent, and it buys an indeterminate amount of time. Nobody can argue with a reasonable-sounding dodge that never says "no" but moves the "maybe yes" somewhere it can slide into "no". (The "father" turns out to be an impostor, but the tactic is worth learning without such stakes.)

**The key**: "make the original decision dependent on another decision which requires your subjective interpretation of some emotion-laden missing information." Had he been the real father, the follow-up would run: "she seems really on the brink, I can't risk letting you question her." / "my daughter could be in real danger, it'll only be a minute." / (angrily) "If it were your daughter in there, you wouldn't allow it either."

- The displacement to a subjective, emotional decision is hard to challenge, because the emotion sneaks in later; the original dodge looks like a quest for missing objective information. If the judgment is questioned, there is a legitimate excuse to get angry. Hence **emotional charging**: "Every significant 'people' decision is like a battery which can be charged up with useful emotion if you think hard enough." Inject enough subjective, emotion-laden information indirectly and nobody can question your right to make the call unilaterally. Every emotional-charging dodge seeks refuge in "the fortress of 'don't tell me how to feel.'"
- It beats common dodges like referral to a committee, which cedes too much autonomy to a body that might run amok instead of performing its appointed "death by consultation". With emotional charging you still decide, and you still choose the option you wanted.
- It can also reel in and *speed up* a decision outside your locus of control, which one-way dodges that only delay cannot do (Palpatine using violence on Naboo to grab power while making it look like a subjective emotional burden).
- It is particularly effective against self-styled data-driven decision-makers: "Subjectivity is their Achilles heel."
