# Story structure and raising the stakes

The Comic Throughline for shaping any comic story (film, novel, play, episode, long sketch), and the full method for comedy and jeopardy. A comic story "has to work first as a *story*." Structure "gives jokes a place to happen. It tells the audience whose story to follow. If they don't know who to follow, they don't know who to care about. If they don't care, they don't laugh." Comic and dramatic structure are the same; the difference is tone (exaggeration, perspective, inappropriate responses, the wide gap of the comic premise), and the two part ways only at the ending.

## The Comic Throughline

The barest bones of a story in ten sentences or less, so you can tell with minimum work whether the story is whole and solid. Grow the one-sentence premise to a paragraph or two:

1. Who is the hero?
2. What does the hero want?
3. The door opens.
4. The hero takes control.
5. A monkey wrench is thrown.
6. Things fall apart.
7. The hero hits bottom.
8. The hero risks all.
9. What does the hero get?

Write each answer down and boil every step to one sentence: "if you can't boil it down to a sentence, you don't have a fix on the information yet." It feels like paint-by-numbers at first; after a few runs it disappears from view and you use it to check your work. Its main job is revealing flaws: far better to find that you have no monkey wrench now than after 120 pages of rambling screenplay or 400 pages of comic novel. Run several fresh ideas through it; it gets easier each time. It won't solve plot mechanics (how Aunt Celia robbed the bank in Cleveland while she was in Hawaii); it tells you whether the story is complete and authentic.

### 1. Who is the hero?

"Until you decide who your story is about, you have no hope of discovering *what* your story is about." The hero is simply the protagonist; smaller-than-life adventurers like Yossarian work fine. For a comic story, start with a strong comic character (perspective, flaws, humanity, exaggeration). Two heroes are possible, but each is the hero of his own story; declare a single individual and develop the story through him. Write a paragraph, then boil it to one sentence, and don't stop until you can. Vorhaus's Albert Collier (1915): perspective curiosity, a tinkerer driven to invent; flaws including gawkiness, intellectual arrogance and a fear of heights; humanity including compassion and a strong desire to change the world; exaggerated in his spectacularly unsuccessful inventions and a blush at a lady's naked ankle. "A young man who's really going places—if only he can stay out of his own way."

### 2. What does the hero want?

Two needs: "the outer need is what the hero thinks he wants and his inner need is what he really wants." *Tootsie*: get work as an actor / discover his true self. *City Slickers*: have a raw adventure / have an authentic life experience. Archie Bunker: validate his bigoted view of the world / understand a difficult world. Over and over in comic movies, "no matter what the hero thinks he wants, what he really wants is love," and not only romantic love (a calf, a daughter, the rebel alliance). "If you can't find any other inner need for your character, assign the need for love." Universal needs are fine, since detail makes them your own. Be reckless and bold; "you can't go back and fix a broken story until you've broken it pretty good in the first place."

### 3. The door opens

Thrust the hero into "some new and challenging world, a place away from home, literally or figuratively, where he gets a chance to go for the thing he thinks he needs." Dorothy's tornado. In fish out of water the fish leaves the pond; in character comedy the opposites meet; in a powers tale the hero finds the magic. The opening door "upsets the applecart": from that moment things can never be the same. It can be a problem or an opportunity, a threat or a welcoming hand, but it makes the hero an offer he can't refuse, "so make your opening door as compelling or as dire you can. Then yank your hero through it." It can offer what he wants but maybe can't handle (Cinderella's ball), or look like his worst nightmare while serving his inner need (the career woman who inherits a baby in *Baby Boom*); often it looks great to the outer need and terrible to the inner, or the reverse. One sentence: the door opens when he joins the circus. In a sitcom, the outer need can drive an episode or a whole series.

### 4. The hero takes control

He enjoys early success and thinks things are going his way, but it's "only a surface triumph, the appearance of success" ("Not triumph, an incredible simulation!"): the inner need hasn't been touched and he hasn't been tested to his limit. "Think in terms of *early success* and *surface success.* Make things good for your good guy here... Above all else, make him unaware of the greater battle that looms ahead." Complete "The hero takes control by..." with one event, then list five smaller ways as future details (*Tootsie*: signing the contract, buying clothes, doing well on camera, befriending Julie, standing up to Ron).

### 5. A monkey wrench is thrown

"A new, bad thing": a screw-up, a new threat, a new character, a complication. In comic stories, especially on film, the new bad thing is a change in the hero's state of mind, and in a sitcom it lands at the act break, *the moment of maximum remove*, when "it dawns on the hero just how distant she is from her goal." Find it by asking "When does something go wrong?"

"In a comic story, the monkey wrench is usually thrown when the hero falls in love." The key word is *loyalty*. The hero starts loyal to himself and his goal; the monkey wrench brings *displaced loyalty*, and suddenly the story is "about a character wanting two things that are mutually exclusive." In *Tootsie*, the longer Michael keeps up the pretense, the closer he gets to his acting goal and the further from Julie's love. "Once you've got everything going your hero's way, pull the rug out from under him. Drop love on his head. Make him want two things and make it so that he can't have both." You'll need a character for the hero to become loyal to, with a throughline of his own: Mr. Comic Opposite, the person who can give your hero the worst possible time.

### 6. Things fall apart

Litter the tale with bad news, a series of negative events boiled to one sentence ("Things fall apart when Michael becomes trapped in the role of Dorothy Michaels"). The key phrase is *caught in a trap of his own making*: as original and displaced loyalty pull apart, the hero finds it's his own damn fault (the coach in *The Bad News Bears* loses his players' faith through his own bad attitude). Familiar structure is no fault: hit songs are in major keys, and conventional stories, conventionally structured, are far easier to write, read and enjoy. Transcend the structure with new comic characters, inventive details and plot twists. When the story reaches a point where something's got to give, you're ready to wrap up.

### 7. The hero hits bottom

The moment of truth, "what makes the story real." Julie tells Dorothy, "I really love you, but I can't love you." Time is running out, and the hero must choose "between his original self loyalty and his new displaced loyalty. It's a choice, in a sense, between 'me' and 'you'": two things he really wants that are clearly mutually exclusive. Use the Force or refuse it? Save the calf or save your life? Many fine comic stories come down to life and death, because the greater the jeopardy, the greater the comedy. If the story won't point toward an ultimate conflict, key elements are still unknown, or "the story you're chasing is a dog." Revise at this level while it's cheap: Vorhaus changed a hero's inner need from "learning true values" to "learning to sacrifice" because the second made the final choice harder.

### 8. The hero risks all

"With no certain hope of success, the hero in a comic story hurls himself into the abyss. He abandons his entire investment in his original goals, sacrifices everything for the sake of his displaced loyalty. The key here is that the hero does the right thing even if he doesn't know whether it will pay off." So often in comedy he risks all by coming clean, confessing the lie that carried him through the story: Michael rips off his wig on national television; Mary Richards yells at Ted and Lou to grow up, not as a strategy but because she can't live the lie a minute more; the coach tells his team, "All I really care about is you." "The moment of truth fulfills the inner need." It runs both ways like clockwork: know the inner need and you know what the moment of truth must be, and vice versa.

### 9. What does the hero get?

The natural ending of a comic story is a happy one: "It's organic," and an unhappy ending would forfeit all the comic currency the tale has earned. A story exists to instruct ("If you do a certain thing, here's what happens"). The comic hero is a *double* winner: he gets both his original goal and his new one, serving his self loyalty and his displaced loyalty. Luke destroys the Death Star and acquires his manhood; Billy Crystal goes home with a renewed love of life *and* the calf. The reversal feels phony only if earlier events don't justify it; in a well-crafted story it's "the *only authentic outcome possible*." Michael must win Julie, because revealing his true self is the only act that could, and he finally gets work because the sacrifice taught him who he is. "A happy ending is the icing on the comic cake, and if you don't give your audience the whole cake they may feel cheated."

**A whole run in one paragraph:** An over-the-hill tennis star wants to be a star again (outer) and to find the off-court meaning of "love" (inner). The door opens when she must take on her comic opposite as her doubles partner. She takes control by improving his game, and they start winning. A monkey wrench is thrown when she finds she loves him, though she knows she hates him. Things fall apart when their romance wrecks their game and the partnership dissolves. She hits bottom when she can win the tournament or win her man, but not both. She risks all by giving up her shot for his love. She gets both: the title and the man.

## Comedy and jeopardy

"There is a strong causal connection between comedy and jeopardy: The greater the jeopardy, the better the comedy." It's tension and release at story scale. In *Lethal Weapon 3*, a lusty truck driver's advances are funny because the truck is a runaway and Danny Glover may die. The *Ghostbusters* go from a harmless investigations service to a marshmallow man ravaging Manhattan: *raising the stakes*. "Do it every chance you get." Attach a cash prize to the trophy; make the friend with the disfiguring disease the character's mom; make the tax cheat cheat big, get caught, and stand to lose *everything*. A home-brew cold remedy that gets the in-law "TV drunk" is bad; the night the other character brings his prospective boss to dinner, it's a story.

There are two ways to raise the stakes, and the best stories raise them both ways at once (*Trading Places*: one man has a lot to lose, the other a lot to win).

### The price of failure

A $2 roulette bet has no price for failure: "No tension equals no release. No release equals no laugh." Make it $2,000, his last two grand, sixty grand owed to the marinara people, a brute called "Fingers" waiting outside, and a promise to pay for his daughter's ballet lessons; no, knee surgery; no, a lung transplant. "Always, always make matters worse." Put characters where "if they lose, they lose it all."

- **Shop for consequences as a list.** Can't get cash from the ATM: he can't buy *People*, can't take out his girlfriend, misses an overdue alimony payment, a gunman kills him, the world ends. "Logic and comedy are not always close friends."
- **Go for the emotional core.** "Genuine raising of the stakes takes place on an emotional level." The robber is the bigger physical threat, but the ex-wife wields more emotional clout, so make the ex-wife and the robber one and the same.
- **Pile on the witnesses and allies.** An actress with stage fright: make it cataleptic fear, fill the house with agents, producers, friends and relatives, add castmates and a director counting on her, then a stalker holding her daughter hostage backstage.
- **The threat may be implied.** Gilligan faces not getting off the island, but also not pleasing the Skipper, and that second threat, closer to his emotional core, drives the comedy.

### The prize for success

Jeopardy includes hope. Mighty Casey has no fear of failure, but his need for success, and everyone's hopes on his shoulders, are unbearable. In *Groundhog Day*, life is hell for Bill Murray because he hopes so much to win Andie MacDowell's love, and "things become funny to us in direct proportion to how hellish they are for him." The person inside the moment, full of hope, can't see the joke; "it's where you want your characters to go." List the good consequences too: with that ATM cash he'll buy a winning lottery ticket, meet the mortgage, or go to a museum, meet an artist, fall in love and live happily ever after.

"It's hard to find the humor of a scene just by asking, 'What's the humor of this scene?' But it's easy to ask, 'What's at stake?' And when you know what's at stake, you'll know what's funny, too. People laugh because they care." Best of all, "overwhelming, unbearable, excruciating hope and mind-numbing, soul-killing, shorts-soiling fear exist side by side in the same character at the same moment" (Yossarian hopes to fly enough missions to go home and fears they'll raise the number again).

### Story logic versus story dynamic

Piling on ("as if that weren't enough...") raises the fear of straining credibility. But the comic premise wrote logic out before the story began: an audience that accepts a mermaid in Manhattan wants laughs, not logic. "When you're confronted with a choice between *story logic* and *story dynamic,* always make the boldest, noisiest, most dynamic choice, even if it beggars credibility... Too much is never enough; you can always make a bad situation worse." An audience can say "I don't buy that" (disdain) or "I can't *believe* that" (wonder), and outrageous storytelling, not logic, earns wonder: Indiana Jones faces a dazzling swordsman and just shoots him. "Comedy is not technical writing. If you build something genuinely funny, no one will care if there are a few pieces left over."

(Here *story logic* means plausibility of events. In sitcom outlining, the book uses the same phrase for a character's inner reasons; see `sitcom-and-sketch.md`.)
