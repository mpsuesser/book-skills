# The Comic Toolbox

Vorhaus's claim: everyone *can* be funny. People who aren't usually lack two things: an understanding of what's funny and why, and the far more important *will to risk*. Comedy is tools. "The unstructured mind asks, 'What's funny?' and instantly gets lost in a mass of amorphous goo." Every tool in this skill swaps that question for a smaller, concrete one that can be asked and answered. "The rules don't confine, they define": the more useful rules you apply, the better you understand the problem you're solving.

The tools overlap ("an ocean is blue, but it's also wet"), and the best comedy crosses lines of definition. Classify only as far as it helps: "push everything into pigeonholes and in the end all you get are squished pigeons."

## Foundations

### Comedy is truth and pain

Every joke rests on a truth and a pain, and together they are its theme. The henpecked man who goes to the right wall in heaven because "My wife told me to": the truth is that some men are henpecked, the pain is that they're stuck with it. The greeting card "I'll bet you think this envelope is too small for a present... Well, you're right": truth *(I'm cheap)*, pain *(so you lose)*.

- **To explain a joke**, ask what truth and what pain it expresses.
- **To make one** (truth + pain = comedy), pick any situation, sum up its truth and pain, then apply a tool. Studying for a test: the truth is it's important to pass, the pain is you're not prepared; add exaggeration and you get "I'm such a lousy student, I couldn't pass a blood test."
- **Truth and pain must be general to the audience.** "The class clown tells jokes everyone gets while the class nerd tells jokes that only he gets." The solipsist light-bulb joke makes the audience work too hard, and the moment passes. What makes a thing funny is how it hits the *generally held beliefs* of the audience; people who don't get a joke, or take offense, usually don't accept the truth it presents. Big truth and pain (sex, death, religion) and small (the dieter who'll start tomorrow) both work, so long as the audience shares your points of reference.
- **Sometimes you just tell the truth.** The after-dinner speaker who says "I'll try to be brief. (Pause.) Thank you and good night" gets the laugh by owning up to what everyone knows: speeches run long and audiences get bored.

### The comic premise: the gap

"The comic premise is the gap between comic reality and real reality." Whenever a voice, character, world or attitude looks at things from a skewed point of view, there's a gap, and "comedy lives in this gap." It exists at every scale: Snoopy is a dog in real reality and a World War I flying ace in comic reality; "I haven't had sex in a year." "Celibate?" "No, married." sets a real-world reason against a comic-world one; even a pun is the gap between how you expect a word to behave and how it behaves ("I haven't had a bite in a week." So I bit him.).

- Once you can see the gap everywhere, reverse the process and *invest* it in material of your own.
- A fast way to open a gap: take what's expected and insert the opposite.
- A premise should fit in one sentence or less. "If you can't boil your comic premise down to a single line, the chances are that you don't quite have a handle on it yet."
- The comic reality reveals the truth in the real reality (Snoopy shows that *people pretend*). "Look to your normal characters to reveal the meaning of your comic premise, and look to your comic characters to reveal its humor."

### The comic character

Comedy flows from character. Asked how to make a script, a scene, a story or a single line funny, Vorhaus always gives the same answer: "invent characters, invest them with strong comic perspectives and flaws and humanity, exaggerate those attributes, then turn these creatures loose upon the world." A comic character has four elements:

- **Strong comic perspective**: "a character's unique way of looking at his world, which differs in a clear and substantial way from the 'normal' world view." It is the character's own comic premise, the gap the laughs spark across, and it motors the comic engine. These may be "the three most important words in this book." "Show me a comic character without a comic perspective and I'll show you a straight man." Gracie Allen: innocence. Groucho: leering cynicism. Jack Benny: tightwad, so "Your money or your life" gets "I'm thinking..." The stronger the perspective, the funnier; the dull ones "travel the least distance from commonly held perspectives."
- **Exaggeration** takes the perspective "to the end of the line": push and stretch it until it's far enough from ours to be funny. Gracie isn't innocent, she's the *ultimate* innocent. "Most failed comic characters fail as a function of their limited exaggeration." There is no such thing as exaggerating too much. Exaggeration works on all three of the other elements and widens the gap.
- **Flaws** are "failings or negative qualities within a person's attributes or aspects." They open emotional distance so the audience can laugh in comfort ("a thing is only funny if it happens to the other guy"), and they create conflict inside the character. No flaws means generic, and generic isn't funny. A virtue taken too far is a flaw (Charlie Brown trusts too much). "Find a flaw and you've found a comic character."
- **Humanity** is "the sum of a character's positive human qualities that inspire either sympathy or empathy or both" (*I like him; he's like me*). Flaws drive a wedge so the audience can laugh; humanity builds a bridge so it can care. "For every flaw, there is an equal and opposite humanity." It must be part of who the character is: pasted-on humanity ("he's a hit man, but he loves his mother") gives you a cartoon, not a character. An indomitable will is one of the surest ways to create it.

The full build (examples, the character template, flaw-versus-perspective synergy, your own comic perspective) is in [characters-and-premises.md](characters-and-premises.md).

### Tension, release and jeopardy

Starting a joke builds tension, often as the question "What's this joke about?", and the punchline releases it as laughter. The more tension stored, the bigger the release. "To make a joke funny, delay the payoff; to make a situation funny, create dire circumstances."

"The greater the jeopardy, the better the comedy." If you want the audience to laugh, make your hero sweat. When a scene isn't funny, swap "How can I make this scene funny?" for "How can I raise the stakes?" Split that into "How can I raise the price of failure?" and "How can I raise the prize for success?", break each into the several outcomes the hero fears and the several he craves, and end by asking and dismissing the question "Is it logical?" Depth is in [story-structure.md](story-structure.md).

## The working method

Whether you're writing the comedy yourself or coaching a writer, run it this way:

1. **Name the job and open its tools** (see the router below). Find the subject's truth and pain first; every tool builds on them.
2. **Shrink the question.** Move from the general to the specific ("life is better there"). The book's own chain: What's funny? → What's a funny story? → What's a comic character in a funny story? → What's that character's strong comic perspective? → How can I make it stronger still? Each tool hands you a question like "Who could give this person the worst possible time?" or "Where would a wedding typically *not* be held?"
3. **Generate by the rule of nine.** "For every ten jokes you tell, nine will be trash." Make lists of ten or more, fast, quantity over quality. A *jokoid* ("not a joke, an incredible simulation") is welcome in the first pass: it holds the place where a real joke will go.
4. **Mine, then refine.** Pick the one in ten. Rewrite each jokoid by asking what, in the abstract, the funny idea is, then finding stronger language for it: put the funny word last, sharpen the detail, push the exaggeration further, make the reader *become* the target instead of observing it, aim for the 3-D joke that tells the story, tells the truth and tells a joke at once. Ear tickles come last. Test against what the audience knows and will accept.

A piece is ready when you can name its truth and pain, state its gap in a line, and point to a payoff that lands at the end.

## Why isn't it funny?

Match the symptom, then open the tool's file for the full method.

- **They don't get it.** The truth and pain aren't shared, or the puzzle lacks a clue. Build the missing clue into the joke itself. *Sufficient information*, in [process.md](process.md).
- **The laugh is soft.** The payoff comes too early or trails inert words. *Positioning the payoff*: "When in doubt, put the funny word last."
- **The line keeps the reader at arm's length.** The reader observes the target instead of becoming it, the remove of a jokoid. Rewrite from inside the target's head ("How many Amish does it take to screw in a lightbulb?" "What's a lightbulb?").
- **The character is flat.** The comic perspective is weak or under-exaggerated, the flaws are missing (generic), or the humanity is missing or pasted on (no one cares).
- **The scene is flat.** Nothing is at stake. Raise the price of failure and the prize for success, close to the character's emotional core.
- **The response is only mildly odd.** You want a *wildly*, not a *mildly*, inappropriate response.
- **The premise won't fit in a sentence.** You don't have a handle on it yet.
- **The story produces no laughs.** No one to follow means no one to care about, and "if they don't care, they don't laugh." Run the Comic Throughline and look for the missing step.
- **The sketch fizzles.** It has no force of opposition, no set glue, no winner, or it ends by fading out.
- **The repeated joke has died.** A running gag doesn't run in place: change its detail, its importance, its source or its timing.
- **It leans on puns or alliteration.** Ear tickles are sequins: they make a marginal joke marginally better but can't make a bad joke good, and a pun has no truth and no pain.
- **The audience turns.** The rules of your comic world changed midstream, or you violated their expectation. You can defeat an audience's hope, never its expectation.
- **It feels borrowed.** Clichés are both pitfalls and openings: defeat the expectation the cliché sets up.
- **It's fine but not great.** Good is the enemy of the great. Rewrite it anyway; the second attempt draws on the first.
