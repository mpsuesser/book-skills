# Joke and line tools

The local tactics of *The Comic Toolbox*, for jokes on the page, the stage, the card or the cartoon. Each tool is a question you can answer with a list. Run the rule of nine on every one: ten answers, fast, then keep the best.

## Opening the gap

### Clash of context

"The forced union of incompatibles." Take something from its usual place and stick it where it doesn't fit: a hooker in a convent, an elephant in a bathtub, a calf in a car in New York (*City Slickers*). It works at every scale, from the premise of a novel (*Gulliver's Travels*) or a series (*The Beverly Hillbillies*, *Green Acres*) to a joke ("Marry in haste, repent in Reno") to an oxymoron, a little clash of context ("honest larceny," "drowning in the fountain of youth," "television reality"). Lift song lyrics out of their music and they turn strange; paste a hair-transplant ad's headline, "Flirt With Confidence," onto the impotence ad on the next page.

- **Method:** name the thing, list where it belongs, then list where it doesn't. "There are so many more places where a thing doesn't belong than where it does." Weddings happen in churches, parks and homes; try a car wash, a pawn shop, freeway traffic, a ball game, a shopping mall.
- **Sample answers:** kid president, a lunar golf course, a beauty pageant in prison, Picasso painting velvet Elvises, Madonna singing opera.
- **High concept**, an idea you can pitch and understand in a sentence or less, is very often clash of context: a boy in a man's body (*Big*), a man in women's clothing (*Tootsie*), a mermaid in Manhattan (*Splash*). "Clash of context is a high-conceptician's best friend."

### The wildly inappropriate response

Clash of context played on attitude instead of place: an attitude set against a circumstance it doesn't fit. In *Life of Brian*, a shopkeeper makes Brian haggle over a gourd while Roman guards hunt him down, a petty attitude against a dire circumstance. Reverse it and you get a dire attitude against a petty circumstance: a fear of heights so strong she faints stepping off a curb. The more incompatible the pair, the funnier.

- **Method:** pick a situation, ask what the logical response would be, then find its opposite "or any of a host of other equally wrong answers." Silent respect suits a funeral, so give the mourners kazoos. Or tubas. Or automatic weapons.
- **Make it wild.** "We want a *wildly*, not a *mildly*, inappropriate response." Add exaggeration: asked if it was good for her too, she holds up a 10 like an Olympic judge; exaggerate again and actual Olympic judges are in the bed.
- **It springs from comic perspective.** The gourd seller haggles because his strong comic perspective says haggling is God.
- **Scene to response:** backyard barbecue → militant vegetarianism; baseball game → cheering for the vendors; death row → a giggle fit; firing an employee → sadistic delight; wedding night → vow of celibacy.

### Telling the truth to comic effect

State the obvious. "I'm bald... I haven't combed my hair in seven years, but on the other hand, I haven't had a bad hair day, either." "He has the attention span of a two-year-old." When a joke bombed, Johnny Carson shot the audience a look that said "Well, that joke didn't work," and the truth behind the dead joke got the laugh.

### Telling a lie to comic effect

Locate the truth of a situation and say its opposite. "This sure is a long line" is true and dull; "If this line gets any longer, they're going to assign it a zip code" gets the laugh. "I only want you for your mind." "This book is worth $14.95." The two tools pair up: if a situation doesn't call for the truth, it calls for a lie. At the dentist's: "Novocain? No thanks." In shark-infested waters: "Now I get my period!" The category doesn't matter; the point is "a reliable place to go when we need a joke and we need it now."

### Exaggeration, everywhere

The character tool works on any material: push it until it's writ large. "If you can't be right, be loud." Applied to a late-night commercial: "If you act now, we'll include the entire state of Rhode Island at no extra charge!"

### The promise of a title

"Every title makes a promise," and work that breaks it loses its audience. Used as a back-door brainstorm: write a list of titles, ask what each one promises (*Our Hundred Years' War* promises an old married couple who've loved and hated each other since the dawn of time), then develop the most promising.

## Shaping the line

### Tension and release, and positioning the payoff

The longer you can delay the payoff, the funnier the joke, because "the mind has to contemplate the question of the joke." Vorhaus sets his name joke inside a dream so the tension can build ("My name is John Vorhaus, and of course that rhymes with whorehouse... so now I'm changing my name." ... "My new name? Vordello."). Stand-ups take the long way round to milk the moment.

**Positioning the payoff**: save the funny word for last, for three reasons. You get the full release of stored tension; the laugh drowns whatever follows the punchline anyway; and, most important, all the setup has been delivered before the answer arrives.

- Right: "If the universe is constantly expanding, why can't I find a place to park?" Reversed, you answer the question before you ask it.
- Right: "Stop feeling sorry for yourself—you miserable loser!" Wrong: "You miserable loser, stop feeling sorry for yourself."
- Right: "If you want to make God laugh, tell him your plans." Wrong: "Tell God your plans if you want to make him laugh."
- Right: "I was voted in high school least-likely to complete the fifty-yard dash." Words that trail the payoff are "inert matter, like argon gas. Purge them from your work."

A joke that doesn't work often needs only its parts rearranged.

### The rule of three

Two points define a line, a line sets a direction, and a direction creates an expectation the third point can defeat: setup, setup, payoff. "The first iteration presents the theme. The second iteration validates the theme like the second point defines the line. The third iteration violates the sequence. *Introduction, validation, violation.*"

- **Method:** find two things from the same set, then something that extravagantly doesn't belong (a forklift, a backhoe loader, a tea cozy). Beethoven's favorites: Handel's *Messiah*, Mozart's *Requiem*, Bon Jovi's *Slippery When Wet*. The further the third item sits from the set, the bigger the payoff.
- **Exactly two setups.** One gives too little to build an expectation; a third is redundant and drains tension. "Don't over-explain."
- **Combined with the inappropriate response:** two appropriate items, then a wildly inappropriate one. "Three things you should never say to the widow at a funeral: 'He looks so peaceful.' 'He's gone to a better place.' 'He was great in bed.'" Three things to bring to an IRS audit: your returns, your receipts, a small-caliber weapon.
- When two voices or characters agree, a third who disagrees in an exaggerated or inappropriate way is a comic opportunity.
- Vorhaus finds it often forced, but "it's often the shortest route to a joke. Or if not a joke, a jokoid."

### Jokoids

"Not a joke, an incredible simulation." It looks, sounds and walks like a joke but isn't funny, and there may be no more useful tool. A jokoid fills the place where the real joke will go, and fixing one is "corrective surgery under local anesthetic," far easier than writing a perfect joke on the first try. Draft with total license to put down jokoids. In rewrite, ask what, in the abstract, the funny idea is, and find new language to express it more strongly.

- **Close the remove.** "How many Amish does it take to screw in a lightbulb? Two—one to screw in the lightbulb and one to wonder what it's for" is a jokoid; "What's a lightbulb?" is the joke. In the first the reader *observes* the Amish person; in the second the reader *becomes* one.
- **Sharpen the nouns.** "A man's commitment to women's liberation lasts until the next bikini comes along" became "...wilts in the face of a wet T-shirt": the same truth and pain "in a slicker package, with sharper detail and more visceral kick."
- Why a jokoid falls flat: the wording is wrong, the drawing unclear, the exaggeration not extreme enough, or the reader has to work too hard, or not hard enough. A failing joke may need adjusting, not replacing.

### The doorbell effect

"Everything's going to be fine as long as that doorbell doesn't ring," and the doorbell rings. "Give your character a strong expectation of a certain outcome, make your audience believe that the expectation is valid, and then defeat the expectation as rudely and ruthlessly as possible." It works best when it catches the audience as well as the character, which is why the stock sitcom version, known too well, falls flat; in *Life of Brian* the Romans search the hideout, leave, and come straight back to search it again. Expectation → defeat: an ATM withdrawal → the machine takes your card, your cash and your wallet; a goodnight kiss → a goodnight kick in the groin. For stand-up: "admit" (a lie to comic effect) that hecklers scare you comatose (exaggeration), beg the crowd not to heckle, and someone will. "It's a lock."

### Clichés

A cliché is a suit bought off the rack, and an audience will bust you for laziness or for not noticing. Clichéd subjects (airplanes, sex, mothers-in-law) demand material that much more personal and keenly observed. But "every cliché creates an expectation, in defeat of which you can create a joke, or at the very least a jokoid."

- **Phrases:** "You can't please everyone, so you might as well please your boss." "On a clear day you can see the smog." "Don't bite the hand that feeds your ego." "A fool and his money are soon partying."
- **Situations:** move the car chase off the street, onto a container ship, into the zoo or a department store, and you dodge the cliché, defeat the expectation and clash the context at once.
- **Characters:** give the clichéd figure a wildly inappropriate attitude, like the beer-swilling redneck who raises prize peonies and reads Emily Dickinson, in a Victorian ball gown, at open-mike night.

### Details

The difference between a good comic moment and a great one is often detail. Not "a dog" but "a dyspeptic rottweiler with the words 'Born to sniff crotch' branded on his haunch"; not "walk" but prance, dance, or ooze like melting snow. Detail makes the work vivid, which deepens emotional investment and so stores more tension to release, and it sharpens your own picture of the work.

- **Method:** simple substitution, one detail for another: "all gain and no risk." Keep asking whether you've pushed it as far as it goes (menthols or regulars? a Daytona or a Hyundai?). No detail is "the best"; use the ones that delight you.
- **Too far is the right place to start.** Excess detail turns distracting, especially in script directions, where it pulls the reader off the page, but "it's always easier to go too far and then pull back." "Detail, not variety, is the spice of life."

### Ear tickles

Words that please the ear or look good on the page: alliteration ("Semite with a scimitar" beats "guy with a sword," maybe 5% or 10% funnier), internal rhyme ("comic toolbox" rhymes its vowels; "levity toolbox" doesn't), puns, funny words, hard consonants, joke names. A pun is "a word or phrase bent or twisted in such a way that it suggests something new without leaving its original meaning completely behind." Twist it too little and it solves itself; too far and no one can solve it. Vorhaus rates puns low: the best you can hope for is a big groan, and "a pun has no emotional content. There's no truth and no pain in a bent phrase."

All ear tickles are sequins at best and "tacky and self-conscious gaud" at worst: they make a marginal joke marginally better but never make a bad joke good. Add them very late, only where they don't harm the meaning, for "an extra 5%."

## Working across a piece

### Virtual humor and the 3-D joke

The best lines "tell the story, tell the truth, and tell a joke, all at the same time": the *three-dimensional (3-D) joke*. They come two ways: as found art, when characters and story are well built, or a dimension at a time, rewriting a line that only moves the story until it also jokes, then until it also tells a truth.

*Virtual humor* is the strategy: strip a scene or joke to its essentials and state its story, its truth and its humor in the abstract, then rebuild every moment to serve all three.

- A diva seduces her hunky stage manager. Story: a woman uses power for seduction. Truth: women can be as exploitative as men in the right circumstances. Humor: she'll fail because he's too dim to notice. So: she takes off her blouse. "I suppose you know what this means." "Sure. Costume change."
- Hating cats. Story: I want cats to stay away. Truth: cats can be annoying. Joke: the hatred shown inappropriately. Their intersection: "The only good cat is a doorstop."
- When stuck, write in plain English the point, the story and the joke of a passage. At worst it shows what's missing. Ask "What's this scene, story, or joke really about?" instead of "Why won't the damn thing work?"

### Microconflict and macroconflict

The big conflict is the *macroconflict*; the small skirmishes are *microconflict*, and they should reflect it, "the microcosm which reflects the macrocosm" (synecdoche, the part for the whole). Two brothers battle over their father's estate (macro) and over who gets the Corvette, who's stuck with the ormolu clock, and whose pen signs the will (micro). A nun running a brothel wants to save the fallen women and squirms at casual nudity and dirty jokes. A stand-up's jaywalking arrest is man versus law, played out in mocking the cop's haircut and fingerprint ink on an Armani suit. "If your character is having a bad day in a big way, don't neglect a single opportunity to give him a bad day in small ways, too": a man who has lost his job, his wife and his home should step in dog poo too.

### The eyebrow effect

Red Skelton evens out his eyebrows one pencil stroke at a time until they run into the next county. Set up "a situation where the solution to a problem creates a slightly larger problem, the solution to that problem creates a larger problem," and so on over the horizon. A bumbling murderer wipes his prints, burns the monogrammed hankie, sets the curtains alight, escapes with a fire extinguisher, realizes the extinguisher has his prints on it, and runs back into the blaze. It's making a bad thing worse with the character as his own cause, comic to us and deadly serious to him. The trigger is a character whose flaws make him stupid, scared, stubborn or desperate enough to stumble in; then "just add detail and you're golden." It can drive a whole story: a time traveler goes back to un-hit his thumb with a hammer, and each fix makes history worse.

### The running gag

"A running gag doesn't run in place." Jokes live on surprise, so a repeat must change. Four ways to change it:

- **Detail:** Murphy Brown can never find a secretary who works, and each new one is a different disaster (an obsessive talker, a Satan worshipper, an escaped convict). The structure stays the same and the substance changes.
- **Importance:** the *Millennium Falcon*'s hyperdrive fails each time with more danger attached.
- **Source:** everyone in *Catch-22* says "That's some catch, that Catch-22," and each speaker means something different.
- **Time:** *Annie Hall* ends on the chicken joke it opened with, now carrying the whole film's weight.

Some audiences want the familiar buzz, and catch phrases can run all the way into icons ("Isn't that special?"). The best of both worlds meets the expectation and bests it at once: everyone shouts "Norm!", and we know a Norm joke is coming but never which one.

### Callback

A direct reference to an earlier joke or idea. In *Tootsie*, Dorothy blames her heavy makeup on a "mustache problem," and later Julie kisses her and says "I feel that mustache." Callback is the way to "button" a scene, story, essay or script: Hannibal Lecter ends the film "having an old friend for dinner." An essay about hating to trim the hedge, through a chain of disasters ending ten weeks out of commission, closes with "At least I didn't have to trim the hedge." Knowing your callback tells you where the piece ends.

## Problem sets

"Treat the joke as a problem to be solved." At the simplest, apply a tool to a situation: clash of context on a ghost town gives an aircraft carrier in the Mojave; an inappropriate response at a pep rally gives the band playing a funeral dirge; bend a common phrase and the pessimist says, "If I drink this, I'll probably spill it all over myself and ruin my shirt."

For a family of jokes, write the problem set first: the conditions every answer must meet. Comic phobias:

1. What is a trivial or absurd fear?
2. What word for that fear sounds good with the *-phobia* suffix?
3. Is my solution likely to be understood by most readers or viewers or listeners?
4. Does my definition twist or amplify the meaning of the phobia?

Solve all four and the joke works (Anachrophobia, fear of being out-of-date; Bibliophobia, fear of the Dewey Decimal System; Chihuahuaphobia, fear of yapping small dogs). Solve three or fewer and it fails: Agoraphobia is a real fear; "Agraphaphobia, fear of early Christian writings" won't be understood; "Angoraphobia, fear of angora sweaters" doesn't twist the meaning. An ear tickle adds bounce (anachrophobia echoes arachnophobia) but stays out of the set because it isn't vital. Pair problem sets with the rule of nine and the power of the list; the method is a little mechanical, and that's what makes it a reliable place to turn when you're stuck.
