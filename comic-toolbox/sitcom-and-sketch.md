# Situation comedy and sketch comedy

Form-specific structure. For the underlying story shape see `story-structure.md`; for character building see `characters-and-premises.md`. "Don't fool yourself into thinking that situation comedy is only about jokes."

## Situation comedy

The goal is to write better than the *best* sitcoms, not the worst.

### The spec script

A *spec* is a sample episode of an existing show, written to prove you can capture its characters' voices, story structure, jokes and rhythms. Choosing the show:

- **A "smart" show**: hip, hot and current, since no one hiring reads specs of silly or derivative shows. Hits get "specked to death," so get ahead of the curve and spec a smart new show before it becomes a hit. If it fails, you still have the experience of the work.
- **A show you like**, or the essential enthusiasm goes missing, and "its absence always shows."
- **A show that plays to your strengths**: gags for a gag writer, heart for a writer with heart, kids for one who writes kids well. Listing your strengths and weaknesses also shows where your craft needs work.
- **A show where you can find your soul.** This is "the one key question": no sitcom script is any good unless the writer puts real heart and emotion into it.

### Learning the rules

Learn the show's rules and follow them. Al Bundy always loses. *Murphy Brown* runs secretary gags but never builds a story around a secretary. *Mad About You* stories turn on conflict between Paul and Jamie. Bob Newhart gets a telephone monologue. On *Gilligan's Island*, no one ever escapes. Know what type of comic story the show tells (*Taxi* is center and eccentrics, so its stories put Alex at the center of an eccentric conflict). Read scripts and watch episodes over and over, until you grasp not just form and structure but "its hidden logic, its taste in stories, and its sense of humor." Questions to answer:

- Which character gets the main story? Who gets the secondary stories?
- Is someone a straight man?
- Do characters tell jokes and wisecracks, or do all the laughs come from their comic perspectives?
- What language do these people use? What topics are taboo?
- Do they refer to the outside world, or live in a sealed sitcom bubble?
- Will given characters act the fool?

### Act breaks

Sitcoms run in two acts or three, and each act ends on an *act break*: a big dramatic moment of expectation and dread strong enough to carry viewers across the commercial.

- **Two acts:** the break is the *moment of maximum dread*, when things are as bad as they can get. The husband is banished to the couch; the pipe in the basement bursts and the water rises; the boss arrives just as the cough medicine kicks in.
- **Three acts:** *trouble is coming* at the first break (a bad thing looms) and *trouble is here* at the second (its consequences hit home, roughly the two-act moment of maximum dread). Bart learns he owes a term paper by morning; then he wakes at dawn, asleep on his work.

Every break needs a pervading "Oh no!" feeling, so this is where making bad things worse pays off: "comedy is less about laughs than about willful, perverse destruction of a character's serenity and peace."

### The arc of stability

Sitcoms are circular: things end more or less where they began (the character who moves out at the act break moves back home by the end), because audiences return to see their favorites doing what they did last week. The change is subtle, and understanding it is the key to sitcom structure. Stories run from *old stability* through increasing *instability* to a *new stability*, frequently a trip from *denial* to *acceptance*.

- Old: a husband and wife love each other. Instability: they feel mutually unloved, underappreciated, taken for granted. New: they rekindle their romance.
- Old: a girl is in denial about her parents' death. Instability: a pet dies, and at its funeral she falls apart and opens to grief. New: she accepts their death.
- Old: a man has a job. Instability: he's laid off. New: he gets a new job.

"If you can't reduce your story to something *this simple,* then you don't fully understand it yet." The arc is an abstraction, so one good old stability / instability / new stability yields dozens of stories; explore them all rather than assume the first is best.

### A-story, b-story and theme

The *a-story* is the main story, the big problem and the heavy emotional issue. It usually goes to the star and plays the episode's theme at its largest. The *b-story* is smaller and lighter, carried by secondary characters. In a well-crafted sitcom the b-story comments on and amplifies the a-story: "the a-story as the melody and the b-story as the harmony." Mr. Wacky hassles his boss for a raise while his kids dun him for a bigger allowance. They can also intersect when one solves the other (Wacky's dilemma over fudging his taxes is settled by making his daughter do her own algebra homework). Thematically linked stories are harder and stronger.

### The shortcut: introduction, complication, consequence, relevance

- **Introduction:** the thing that starts the trouble (an out-of-town guest arrives, an old girlfriend turns up, a first date looms).
- **Complication:** the thing that makes the bad situation worse (one character takes cough medicine; another brings the boss home for dinner. A character weaves a lie into an English essay; the essay wins a major prize).
- **Consequence:** the result of the conflict between them (the cough medicine blows up in the boss's face).
- **Relevance:** the theme, "best expressed as an imperative," an instruction, a call to action: Stand by your friends. Do the right thing. Communicate!

*Gilligan's Island*: aliens land on the island; Gilligan befriends them and they agree to take him home; fearing they'll be exploited, he lets them leave without him; do the right thing, even if it costs you. "Everything you need to know is implied in those four sentences—if they're the right ones." Generate a long list of four-sentence stories when shopping for ideas, and the good ones show quickly. It works for any story, not just sitcoms.

**The implied fireworks scene.** A well-structured story implies a big climactic scene where the fireworks explode, the pies get thrown or the secrets come out, and you can draw a clear line to it from the introduction. Home-brew cough medicine implies outrageous behavior in front of the worst possible person at the worst possible time; any lie implies the scene where the truth is told. It often turns on a *decision* that is the key to the whole story: a retired football star offered a comeback must finally decide about his lingering pro dreams. Naming the scene tells you where to look.

### Story outlines

Before the script, write a full *story outline*: a present-tense telling with as much detail, real emotion and funny incident as you can fit, around ten pages, the blueprint for the script. Beginners skip it, and "that way lies madness": story problems are "several orders of magnitude easier to fix" in outline, with fewer words to change. "Obsess on the outline."

Examine each draft for **problems** (flaws in logic or sense) and **opportunities** (places to put the comic tools to work: exaggerate a reaction, clash a context, find a better enemy, make a bad situation worse). Problems come in two kinds of logic:

- **Plot logic** is outer logic, the sequence of events the writer imposes.
- **Story logic** is the characters' inner logic, the reasons they have for behaving the way they do.

Every move must satisfy both, or you get *plot robots*, characters who exist only to move the story forward. Mr. Wacky can't just wake up wanting a vasectomy; his girlfriend's pregnancy scare drives him to it, and now he wants to get to that hospital room as much as you do. Justify every move by who the character is, what he wants and how we understand him to behave.

Rewrite the outline again and again, over hours, days or weeks. Each pass goes deeper and finds more fun: three or four drafts in, Dwight has *two* girls after him, or Wacky falls for the girl's mom.

### Story to script

A script from a thorough outline is translation; without one, it's "panning for gold with a shrimp fork." Still expect *the Grand Canyon effect*: you won't find all of a story's problems until you go down the trail into the script. Length: as long as the show's own scripts. Write fully, cut whatever is irrelevant, and mirror the show's format. Present it professionally (names spelled right, clean layout, no typos), because a spec lands in "the black hole of spec scripts," and a reader may judge the whole by one random page. So it must work *on every page*.

## Sketch comedy

For opening and exploring a comic moment rather than telling a whole story. Structure answers "Where do I go next?" Vorhaus's nine-point method:

1. **Find a strong comic character.** Use perspective, exaggeration, flaws and humanity. Sketches are built on characters (Edith Ann, Belushi's Samurai), but a character alone is a static picture; "Comedy is conflict."
2. **Find a force of opposition.** Either a comic opposite (Kramden needs Norton) or a normal foil (introduce the Coneheads to normal earthlings). Every character needs a strong desire to *win* or to *get away*, and best of all, first to win and then to get away. Without one they just make pleasant conversation.
3. **Force a union.** You need *set glue*, a compelling reason they stay together for the life of the scene: a talk show, a prison cell, a room in hell, a shop run by a lunatic (Monty Python), a radio interview (Bob and Ray). "Virtually any situation will do, so long as it's easier to get into than out of."
4. **Escalate their conflict.** It starts small (what time is it; the coast route or the mountains) and gets worse right away. "Make it loud and angry. Make it personal. Make it physically violent if at all possible." Push toward the emotional core: "We're lost" → "You never ask for directions" → "Why are you so stupid?" → "I want a divorce" → someone getting out of the car at highway speeds.
5. **Raise the stakes.** Bring in new risk or reward so the scene is "suddenly about something new, different, and fundamentally more important": the fallen woman the priest is saving puts his celibacy at risk, or a shoe shine turns into class warfare. Ask "What's the worst possible thing that could happen to this person next?" and make it happen.
6. **Push the limits.** Make the bad things worse: more priests, fewer clothes, cops, a broken bone, every pie thrown. The best sketches push to "raving madness, full psychic deconstruction." Exaggerate and ignore logic. "Stick to the typical and your scene will die of ennui."
7. **Seek an emotional peak.** Strong opposites in real conflict in a closed situation are a pressure cooker, and a peak emerges from the ruin. "Create trouble, increase trouble, compress trouble, and push, push, push."
8. **Find a winner.** Closure: somebody wins, somebody loses, everybody wins, or everybody loses. "It doesn't matter how the story ends so long as it *does* end." A sketch is in trouble when there's nothing to do but fade out and cue the applause sign.
9. **Change the frame of reference.** Cap it with a twist that pulls back and shows the reality to be some kind of fantasy. The couple fighting all the way to the party turns out to be test-driving a car at a dealership to see if it can survive their fights. It "explosively releases all the tension," and it's the way out of a sketch with no natural exit.

**Write a beat outline first.** A *beat* is a single piece of action or conflict ("Bob and Ethyl argue about a book"); a *beat outline* tells all the actions and conflicts in present-tense prose, numbered. It beats "hunt 'n' hope," and broken structure is easier to fix there than in script.

**Implied opposition.** In a parody sketch the force of opposition can be the target itself (Chevy Chase's Gerald Ford; *Wayne's World* against public-access TV). The danger is that the idea goes flat without a strong story and real conflict: the Church Lady parodies sanctimony but still needs a true enemy, nothing less than SATAN. Characters who become icons become franchises, which is one more reason to start with strong comic characters.

**Another lens: create, then destroy reality.** Entering a scene raises "What's supposed to be funny here?", which is tension; destroying the scene's reality releases it. A gas-station attendant tells a kid the hose pumps only "tire air," useless for basketballs (tension: "Is this guy on the level?"). He argues more and more persuasively, a crowd takes sides, he pumps the ball until it explodes ("See, kid? I told you this air wouldn't work"), and the frame changes: it's a training film on "How to spot morons in the workplace."

**Worked outline:** a self-styled expert on TV knows nothing (comic character); an interviewer is out to expose him (opposition); the show is the set glue; the challenges escalate; the stakes rise when his advice proves deadly to followers; the limits are pushed when he can't deny he's a fraud; his nervous collapse is the peak; the interviewer wins; and the frame changes when they turn out to be brothers fighting over who was mother's favorite.
