# Characters, premises and types of comic story

Depth for building comic premises, comic characters and comic pairings, and for choosing the kind of comic story to tell. The core definitions (the comic premise as the gap; strong comic perspective, exaggeration, flaws, humanity) are in `SKILL.md`; this file is how to build with them.

## Building comic premises

**Real reality / comic reality lists.** Down the left of the page write ten real realities; down the right, ten comic realities that conflict with them: going to the store / shopping for Uzis; a cop stops a pickup truck / it's full of space aliens; the Magna Carta / written by e.e. cummings; a high-school shop class / taught by Albert Einstein. One way in: think what's expected, then insert the opposite. If the exercise feels hard, the mind needs more structure, so narrow the question to one type of conflict.

### The three types of comic conflict

Every gap turns on conflict, and the deeper the conflict, the more interesting the premise. Comic conflict mirrors dramatic conflict (man against nature, man against man, man against self) on three levels:

- **Global conflict**: people against their world. It comes in two forms:
  - A *normal character in a comic world* stands in for the audience and represents real reality, while the situation is the comic reality (Marty McFly in the 1950s, a Connecticut Yankee at Camelot, Bill Cosby among Fat Albert and Old Weird Harold).
  - A *comic character in a normal world* carries the premise with him (Michael Dorsey becomes Dorothy Michaels in *Tootsie*).

  Robin Williams looks at a straight world in a bent way; Cosby looks at a bent world in a straight way. Global conflict is often social conflict, an individual against a whole structure (Bilko, Private Benjamin and Bill Murray in *Stripes*, all against the US Army). A businessman against the IRS gives the IRS audit from hell; reverse it and the comic character is one insanely belligerent auditee.
- **Local (interpersonal) conflict**: battles between people with an emotional bond. They care about each other, even if they hate each other; that's what separates this from a cop against a con artist. It's either a comic character against a normal one (Mork and Mindy) or comic characters in opposition (Felix and Oscar). Make the IRS agent the businessman's ex-wife and you have local conflict. Examples: an astronomer marries an astrologer; a prude and a pimp join forces.
- **Inner conflict**: a character at war with himself, "the richest conflict" in all storytelling. Either a normal character becomes a comic one, a change of state (*Tootsie*; *Big*'s kid in an adult's body), or a comic character fights himself without changing (Murphy Brown's doubt). The easy route is to turn a character into his opposite: a kid becomes a CEO, a housewife becomes a NATO commander, a football player becomes a ballet star.

The best comic premises exploit all three at once. In *Trading Places*, each man struggles in his new world (global), with the people around him (local) and with his new persona (inner). Calvin fights authority, Hobbes and his own flights of fancy; Nancy and Sluggo never battle themselves. "Which is the more interesting strip?" A stand-up's airplane-food bit takes off once the flight attendant is an old enemy trying to poison her and she's an acrophobe who never should have flown.

Note how the question narrows. "What's a comic premise?" founders; "What's a conflict between a normal character and a comic character?" is already into specifics.

### The inner comic premise

"The gap between how a character sees himself and who he really is." The flaws are his true nature and the comic perspective is his fantasy self-image: war hero / 4-F weakling, genius / stupid, loved by all / loner. It's an angle to play sometimes, not on every character.

## Building comic characters

The comic assembly line: build lots, fast, and expect most not to be keepers (the rule of nine). Two minutes a character is enough.

**Comic perspectives** are single, clearly defined ways of seeing the world: virginal schoolboy, newborn baby, curmudgeonly oldster, cockeyed optimist, testosterone poisoning, perfectly paranoid, know-it-some. Filter the same thing through each: a woman's naked breast is the bridge to manhood for the schoolboy and lunch for the baby; the know-it-some says the submarine sandwich was invented by the Earl of Submarine, and the paranoid wonders if it's poisoned. "Virtually anything can be filtered through a comic perspective, and virtually any point of view can be a comic perspective." Then exaggerate, like an SAT analogy: fearful → jumps at shadows; joyful → all manic all the time; drunk → stewed to the gills; likes cats → feline-obsessed, owns twelve dozen.

**From flaws.** Shop for nouns (a dictionary works; "none dare call it cheating") and extrapolate a character: greed → Scrooge; laziness → Andy Capp; fearfulness → multiphobic weenie; envy → covetous co-worker; baldness → wannabe sex symbol. More flaws make a richer character ("a comic character... is the sum of his flaws": Al Bundy is sloppy, sexist, selfish, with smelly feet). A flaw needn't go to its "appropriate" owner: give prudishness to a stripper, an air-traffic controller, the President. Physical attributes can work as flaws without being bad things. Flaws are subjective, though: the stutterer in *A Fish Called Wanda* was a comic flaw to some and abuse to others, so weigh what your audience will accept, tolerate and get.

**Make the elements fight.** "In the best comic characters, flaws and perspective go to war." Lucy's "There's nothing I can't do" is aided by her impulsiveness and thwarted by her incompetence. Build the lines of conflict on purpose: if the perspective is fearlessness, assign phobias as a flaw and a superhero's desire to serve others as humanity, so "some part of your character must naturally be wrong when everything else is right." If it's "love conquers all," give him selfishness and guilelessness. That puts him in "a painful box," exactly where comic characters belong. A flaw can double as humanity: Lucy's impulsiveness gets her in trouble and makes us love her more.

**Balance flaw with humanity.** Hannibal Lecter works as a comic character: perspective "People are food"; flaws a mountain of evil; humanity intelligence, wit, manners, loyalty, fair play and an incredibly strong will to win ("He'll stop at nothing to eat your face"). "The worse you make some aspects of a comic character, the better you must make others." A classic humanity: he'll do the right thing in a pinch (Dan Fielding, Louie DePalma). Others: Otto's romantic soul, Charlie Brown's vulnerability. Candidate qualities: loyalty, honesty, generosity, humility, sense of humor, curiosity, vulnerability, strength of will, innocence, patience, physical strength, physical beauty.

**The template.** Hit the marks:

> CHARACTER: Spenley Cruntchwhistle. COMIC PERSPECTIVE: expert on everything. FLAWS: know-it-all attitude, massive holes in his knowledge. HUMANITY: well-meaning, sincere, helpful. EXAGGERATION: knows the seven chief exports of Bulgaria.
>
> CHARACTER: Ophelia Barnette. COMIC PERSPECTIVE: body is a temple of the holy spirit. FLAWS: shyness, smoldering libido. HUMANITY: loyal to her friends, desperately wants love. EXAGGERATION: undresses in the dark, even when alone.

Read famous characters the same way: Charlie Brown is a perennial loser whose self-pity would alienate us were it not for his long-suffering patience; Sam Malone is a supremely sexist egomaniac, and when he reveals his insecurity we all fall in love. Layer more perspectives, flaws and qualities for depth; hitting the four marks is enough for simple construction.

**Character keys** are "the small ways a character behaves that tell us the large ways he'll behave as well," the intersection of microconflict and comic perspective. Use them to introduce characters. The innocent opens her mail and cries, "Look, honey! Ed McMahon says I may already have won!" *Tootsie*'s Sandy ends her toast with "I'm sorry, this is a really stupid speech" (neurotic, insecure), and Jeff wants people to say of his play, "I saw your play, man. What *happened*?" (the auteur pose). Lay in precise, powerful keys early to build audience allegiance.

**Your own strong comic perspective** is your comic voice. Vorhaus found his in a Las Vegas casino: "The world is my circus," everything happening just for his amusement. Build a library of comic perspectives, and notice that almost every joke you meet is a function of someone's: "I can think for myself, can't I, dear?" shows a husband whose perspective is that the decision of the wife is final.

## The law of comic opposites

"First create a comic character and identify her or his strong comic perspective. Then seek the diametric opposite of that perspective and assign that opposite to a second character. Now lock them in a room together." Felix, all anal all the time, and Oscar, the crème de la slob. In *Midnight Run* a super-rational bounty hunter is literally handcuffed to a neurotic: a forced union. Gracie, the ultimate naif, and George, the ultimate cynic. Pitches: a scrooge marries a spendthrift; a priest inherits a brothel; a debutante takes a street punk to the prom.

The other way in: ask of your character, "Who could give this person the worst possible time?" A priest gets more trouble from a hooker than from an insurance salesman; a self-centered, arrogant football star meets a self-centered, arrogant diva. "What's the comic opposite of a scrooge?" is far easier to answer than "What's a good idea for a movie?"

## Types of comic stories

Knowing which kind of story you're telling swaps "What's a comic story?" for "What kind of comic story shall I tell?", then "What are examples of *this particular kind* of comic story?" Keep each idea to a single sentence at this stage and resist the details. The categories bleed (*Northern Exposure* is all of them), so run a favorite idea through several types for depth and texture.

- **Center and eccentrics.** An everyman surrounded by comic characters; he's our eyes and ears. The premise is the gap between his normal perspective and their comic ones (*Taxi*'s Alex Rieger among Tony, Jim, Louie and Latka; Mary Richards against Ted's ego, Lou's gruff exterior, Murray's nebbishness, Rhoda's kvetching). To build one: a central character with a normal, non-comic perspective, a situation (where he works or lives), and about half a dozen others, each with a strong comic perspective defined in one sentence. Vorhaus's *We're In This Together*: Sally Crowder, long-suffering mom of identical teen triplets; Chip, whose life is a shrine to Elle MacPherson; Scooter, who'd sell the cat if the price was right; neighbor Mrs. Brickle, who thinks fudge cures everything. The lesson: "a comic story is not about a setting or a situation or a predicament, but about strong and enduring lines of conflict between and among the characters." Center and eccentrics tells you at once who the hero is, who he's up against and where the lines of conflict lie.
- **Fish out of water.** Clash of context story-wide: a normal character in a comic world (time travel: "take a typical dude and put him in a faraway place, either literally or metaphorically") or a comic character in a normal world (alien comedies; *Splash*). The displacement can be internal (*Big*, *Tootsie*). "The more he doesn't belong, the better." The new world is often the character's comic opposite: the woman who wants a baby least gets one (*Baby Boom*); the woman who fears adventure stumbles into one (*Romancing the Stone*). Put heroes through hell; they're comic characters, not friends. Exaggerate until the fish is "dying and desperate... flopping around on the beach." Examples: a nun who doubts her vocation runs a casino; a wicked old man gets sent to heaven by mistake.
- **Character comedy.** "Direct emotional war between strong comic opposites" (Kramden and Norton, Bertie and Jeeves). It's often character romance, haters who'll become lovers (Sam and Diane): "if they were any less annoying to each other, they'd be a good deal less funny to us." It needs strong forces driving the pair apart and equally strong ones holding them together (related by marriage in *All in the Family*; life-and-death stakes and a ticking clock in *48 Hours*; sexual chemistry, "endocrine superglue," on *Cheers*). The nemesis needn't be a diametric opposite, only a *catalyst for misery* (a cop partnered with a dog, his mom, a kid). Examples: a doctor marries a hypochondriac; a lady lawyer defends her con-artist ex-husband.
- **Powers.** The comic premise is the magic itself. Audiences grant "willing suspension of disbelief" and will swallow any explanation for an invisible man (stealth technology, radiation, pickled cloves of garlic), but "you can't change the rules in the middle": ghosts who walk through walls in scene one must still walk through them at the climax. Use one simple premise ("witches exist," "people can change bodies"), make the power new and fascinating, and be consistent. Powers stories rarely reach deep emotional conflict; "a talking dog will only carry you so far."
- **Ensemble comedy.** A group in conflict with each other and the world, ideally with no single hero: a *group protagonist* that opens the door to real emotional issues. "The key to an ensemble comedy is the group's commonly held goal or enemy" (the *M\*A\*S\*H* unit uniting when the wounded arrive; the Golden Girls against aging). Layer enough conflict *within* the group: scientists fighting a monster must also be at each other's throats. Draw the characters too broadly and you get *Gilligan's Island*, all global conflict with nothing at stake; too strident and you kill the fun. It's the hardest type: treat every character as the hero of his own story and interleave the stories.
- **Slapstick.** The easiest: "There is no *there* there." The slapstick character never doubts himself (Gilligan never questions his "Gilliganness"; Clouseau never doubts he'll solve the crime), so the premise is "the gap between the slapstick character's self-assurance and his manifest incompetence." Self-doubt denied makes it physical, not cerebral comedy: film, TV, stage and the funnies, not fiction. "Slapstick comedy is abuse comedy," and it works because the target gets what he deserves: "A pie in the face is funniest if the face belongs to a pompous jerk." Create characters with delusions of grandeur, the "tall poppy" we want cut down, and put them in situations designed to torment those delusions: an arrogant English professor coaches a working-class soccer team; a spoiled yuppie is cast away on a desert island.
- **Satire and parody.** "Satire attacks the substance of a social or cultural icon or phenomenon. Parody attacks the style of an art form." Both find their premise in "the gap between the world as they present it and the world as their audience understands it to be," so the audience must step outside the work to get the joke, and must know the target (a parody of horror movies is lost on someone who's never seen one; the class clown parodies the teacher, the class nerd a book no one else has read). The danger is that a reader who leaves the page may not come back: a Gandhi reference makes him "call up his file" and breaks the narrative flow, and "unless the joke is a damned good one, you stand to lose more than you gain." The best work runs on two levels, a framing story that holds on its own while the allusions play to those who get them (*The Bullwinkle Show*). Exaggerate: satire and parody need a huge gap between story and target. Satire picks a clearly defined real-world target (a theme park based on the works of Proust). Parody must match the form and structure of what it parodies (the life of Buddha as a musical with the shape and self-serious tone of *Jesus Christ Superstar*; a jugband plays Wagner's *Ring*).
