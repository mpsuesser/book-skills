# Process: risk, rewriting, feedback, audience and practice

The emotional and working side of comedy. "Without the proper emotional grounding, the tools themselves are useless." Use this file when a writer is blocked, afraid or perfectionist, when material needs rewriting and polish, when taking or giving notes, when weighing an audience (what it knows, what it will accept, what offends), or when building a practice.

## The will to risk

What unfunny people lack is "the *will to risk*," and "the will to risk is really the will to fail." It is a tool, learnable like any other, and it makes all the others easier to use.

**Eradicate bogus thinking.** Two kinds:

- The *false assumption* is "It won't work; they won't like it." It's false because jokes sometimes work: assuming failure is exactly as wrong as assuming success. You won't know until you try.
- The *faulty association* leaps from their not liking the joke to their not liking *you*, then to "when they don't like me, I can't like myself," and on to the biggest bogus thought of all, "If I fail, I die" (hence the stand-up's "I died out there"). It's faulty because "people aren't thinking about how you look to them. They're far too busy worrying how *they* look to you."

**Kill your ferocious editor.** Tie the bogus voices into one knot and label it "my ferocious editor." Its job is to keep you from bad mistakes, and sometimes it's right (don't mouth off to a cop), but it "simultaneously underestimates your chances for success and overestimates the penalty for failure." In the generating stage, silence it. In the polishing stage, bring it back to life as your best friend, "demanding and unrelenting in pursuit of quality."

**The rule of nine.** "For every ten jokes you tell, nine will be trash. For every ten ideas you have, nine won't work. For every ten times you risk, nine times you fail." It's a *useful fiction*, not a truism, and it liberates: "When you expect success, you fear failure," so dropping the expectation drops the fear. It also proves that the process of failure is vital to the product of success, since a body of work needs hundreds of failed jokes. In practice: lists of ten, quantity not quality, against a deadline (ten funny sports-team names in five minutes or less; ten funny names, starting with Julianne Potatoes and Spenley Cruntchwhistle).

**Lower your sights.** "Hope of success can kill comedy just as surely as fear of failure. With the rule of nine, we attack our fear of failure. By lowering our sights, we attack our need for success." Concentrate on this chapter, this paragraph, this sentence, this word. "Require of yourself only that you do what you can do now."

**Positive reinforcement as self-fulfilling prophecy.** "Applaud small victories. They make big victories grow." Finishing the exercise is the win; take it. Each win strengthens the sense of yourself as someone who can do the job, which improves the next job. "The better you imagine yourself to be, the better you become." The shift is to *process, not product*: attend to the work in front of you, not the applause to come, and the game becomes one you must always win.

**Concentrate on the task at hand.** When envy or competition floods in (a friend just got the gig you wanted), go back to the one thing you control, the words on the page. Finish a small chunk, praise it, press on. "This war is won in small battles." Pitching forward on your face is good: at least you're moving forward.

**The fraud police.** Every creative person fears someone will discover they have no business doing what they do, because creators keep taking on bigger challenges and are always swimming in strange waters. Denied, the fear becomes hidden tension that degrades the performance; admitted, it vanishes. "Once you tell people what you're afraid of, you no longer have to worry about their finding that thing out," and since they fear the same, "you become their hero." A first-time stand-up who says "Be gentle with me, it's my first time" takes the audience off the hook: they no longer have to judge, so they can enjoy. "To divert the fraud police, invite them into your home." The inner fraud police are the ferocious editor's goon squad; launch a pre-emptive strike and "the fuzz becomes your friend."

## Rewriting and polish

"The real work of comic writing takes place in rewriting." That's where jokes become three-dimensional, characters become true and stories shake out their problems. "If you're not willing to commit to rewriting and editing, you might as well go drive a truck." Every urge to improve the work meets an equal urge to protect the ego ("it's fine just the way it is," "maybe nobody will notice"). "Will you serve your ego, or will you serve your work?" Serve the work.

- **Mining and refining.** You don't dig a gold necklace out of the ground: you grind a ton of ore for an ounce, then smelt and polish it. Raw material and finished product are different things. Mining pours everything onto the page; refining rewrites a word, a sentence, a chapter. "Concentrate on mining when you're mining, then concentrate on refining when you're refining." Separating the two lifts both, and material only thought about goes only so far, so get it on the page.
- **Write fast, write long.** It's easier to cut than to add. Let a 120-page script's first pass run 160 or 170; for five minutes of stand-up write fifteen; for one column write two or three. Take the *challenge to cut*: what survives a 50% cut has passed a rigorous test, and knowing the draft is a third too long turns editing into a *happy ending*. Drill: write a one-page character description, then cut it to half a page.
- **Good is the enemy of the great.** Improving often means you have to *give up some gains*: a beloved joke, a favorite character, a scene that works but not quite. *The hill-climbing problem*: you stand on a decent hill and see the mountain, but the way there runs down into a foggy valley. You know what you'll lose and not whether it will pay off, but the choice is "maybe don't succeed" against "surely don't succeed." *The myth of the last great idea* is the fear that your last idea was the last good one you'll have. It's a myth because the rewrite draws on everything the first attempt taught you, including the only-okay joke itself; "taking this work to the next level is now a function of drawing on the work *at this level*." Rewrite your favorite piece; it's "unconditionally guaranteed to improve."
- **Trust yourself.** "To validate your 'yes,' you have to authenticate your 'no.'" A long record of saying "No, that doesn't work" and rewriting lets you believe it when you finally say "Yes, *now* it works." Avoid falling in love with your jokes. "Avoid closure; the longer you put off saying you're done, the better your finish will be."
- **The wince factor.** Read the work back and listen for what makes you wince; a songwriter did this with her lyrics. It's "less to do with how to be funny than with how not to be not-funny." When you're unsure whether something works, spend the skull sweat to settle it. "It's far better for the line to make you wince now than to make someone else wince later. Police your prose."
- **How done is done?** There's no tool for it. Sooner or later you "hit the wall," the point of diminishing return past which more work is just noodling. You also know when you haven't hit it, when you're cheating the process. "If you push your limits, they become less limiting."

## Beta testers

We get too close: we fall in love with our jokes, ignore plot holes and laugh at jokoids. *Beta testers* (the term comes from software) test the work before the public sees it and tell you what works, why, and mainly how to make good work better. One smart, honest, clear-eyed, hard-working tester is enough; more is better, up to the point where "too many cooks pop the souffle."

- **Finding them.** "Peers make your best beta testers": people at about your level, in your area, for whom you return the favor. Mothers feed the ego; paid readers cost too much; spouses and lovers are supportive but the relationship can turn tumultuous. Genre knowledge helps (for stand-up, at least a fan), and they must give full, concrete responses, not "I liked it." Keep potential employers, clients and buyers out of it; save those contacts for the polished work. "The most important quality for a beta tester is a willingness to help."
- **Training them.** "People like these are made, not born." Ask for a clear path to improvement, neither a pat on the back nor a blanket rebuke, and tell them what you're trying to accomplish. Teach *large notes* (structure, theme, story, characters) and *small notes* (line notes). Ask for honesty and *constructive criticism*: "all news is good news if it helps improve your work." Seek detail: which lines got a laugh, and where they got confused, unamused or bored. Tell them which notes helped most. It's a long-term relationship, and testers get better with time.
- **Using them.** Train yourself to accept notes: say as little as possible, and don't argue, defend or explain. "I'm only there to listen." You needn't agree with any of it ("you are God"), and you shouldn't rewrite just to please the tester, or you serve two masters. Adopt what's useful and set the rest aside after careful consideration, but treat the tester with respect. When a tester rejects a joke, replace it anyway, just to prove it can be done. The hardest exercise in the book: rewrite a piece from someone's notes, assuming every note is dead-on correct.

## Audience

"Your audience is your friend. Earn their allegiance and they'll forgive a multitude of sins." Know what your audience knows, what it will tolerate and what it finds funny; then you can work out how to amuse it, or whether you can at all (*Spinal Tap* booked at an Air Force dance).

- **Consistency.** Keep the rules of your comic world fixed. Foreigners who know nothing of American culture can't suddenly get a McDonald's joke. Farce must be farce throughout; you can't start as realistic romantic comedy and then throw pies. Audiences build a gestalt of your world fast and feel violated when its rules break.
- **Sufficient information.** A joke is a puzzle, and an audience without the clues feels stupid and takes it out on you. Build the clue into the joke: "I'm not saying that California Jews aren't religious, but their technical name is Presbyterian."
- **Avoiding an adversarial relationship.** Vorhaus says he's Jewish before he tells that joke, which keeps him "an okay guy." Track what the audience will tolerate as tastes shift: drug humor was everywhere in early-1980s sitcoms and a quick ticket to the parking lot by the early '90s. "You don't need to pander to your audience, but you don't want to alienate them, either."
- **Hope versus expectation.** All comedy *creates an expectation*, and "meeting an audience's expectation is about the single most useful thing a comic creator can do to win an audience's allegiance. Violating that expectation... is the kiss o' death." Viewers of *Married... with Children* hope Al Bundy wins and expect him to lose: the show "can defeat their hope, it must not defeat their expectation," because hope is our picture of how things should be and expectation our picture of how they really are, and violating it tells people they're wrong. Shock and insult comics meet the expectation of bad behavior, so their alienation wins approval. Best of all is to meet and defeat the expectation at once: Al loses, but in a completely unforeseen way.
- **Frames of reference.** Comedy is a popular art form, so assimilate the audience's frame of reference: what it knows and what it accepts. Knowledge goes stale, and topics too new leave you alone ahead of the curve (the latest *New England Journal of Medicine*), so strike a balance. You can't know an audience's frame absolutely: "guess and conjecture, and above all test," and be ready to change the material. On the page, be stricter still: "Go for the cheap topical reference and you're only planting the seeds of your own destruction."
- **Offense.** "It's okay to offend *part* of your audience if you connect with another part"; offend too many and amuse too few, and you have no audience. "It's okay if some people really hate your stuff." Avoid "the vast, bland middle ground where your humor is safe, innocuous, offensive to no one—and thus compelling to no one." Turn offense to advantage with a common enemy, *the Saddam Hussein effect*: the comic who savages a heckler bonds the rest of the room ("us versus him"), and an essay against insurance salesmen ("As if life insurance worked...") unites an audience of their victims. "Just don't try publishing that essay in *Insurance Salesmen's Quarterly*." In the end, delight the audience of one: if you genuinely find it funny, a fixed percentage of the world will too.

## Practice

- **Your comic vocabulary.** Save the comic flotsam: jokes cut for the story, notions that can't bear development, lines ahead of their audience. Vorhaus's file, "1001 Clever Things I Thought Up Myself or Stole From a Few Close, Personal Friends," holds napkin jokes ("Coffee is better than sex because you can have more coffee right away"), aphorisms, slogans, oxymorons, funny names and ear tickles. Review it when stuck; it's "both an inventory and a stimulus." Add five or ten new jokes or jokoids every day.
- **The Wade Boggs paradigm.** The best pure hitter in baseball took batting practice every day; we're not that good, so we need it more. If you're not as funny as you want to be, "perhaps you're not *working hard enough*." Practice means doing the thing: write, draw, perform, take lots and lots of photographs. "Even a .300 hitter makes an out twice as often as he gets a hit. Embrace failure and make practice part of your routine."
- **Do something new.** Doing the unexpected thing creates funny moments. Make the creative process itself fresh: break old habits, even ones that work. "Write in bed."
- **Leaplets.** "We grow not in leaps but in leaplets. Moving forward slowly is moving forward just the same."
- **TALENT + DRIVE + TIME = SUCCESS.** Everyone has talent, so ask whether you have enough drive: "hard work, practice, patience, perseverance, and relentless pursuit of your goals." You have twenty-four hours a day, but time is non-renewable. Focus on *journey, not destination*: every step moves you "farther from the beginning," a success you can count every day. Practice patience and impatience at once, taking the long view for patience and the short view for drive: "put myself on the spot and let myself off the hook at the same time." Some days the river runs dry; those are good days to go to the movies.
- **Revelation fades.** The rush of a new tool or book always fades, so keep it alive with new inspiration, and "above all, *create*." Use the tools that come easily, and come back later to the awkward ones: weak muscles need their workout too.
- **Heart.** Art without heart is an "artoid," the velvet Elvis, "not art but an incredible simulation." Comedy is "a powerful, often subversive, force for change"; you choose whether it's constructive or destructive.
