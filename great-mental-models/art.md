# Art

Models from the arts. Magritte: "Art evokes the mystery without which the world would not exist." Art is where people work hardest at how something is experienced, so these models apply wherever you communicate, persuade, organize attention or tell a story about events, including the story you tell yourself. Each entry gives the model, its moves, its limits and its cases.

## Audience: "Who is it for?"

**The model.** The interaction between what we do and how it is experienced: a mutual exchange (Iris Murdoch has art gathering the personality of creator and reader into a "unified significance"). The audience is both the group the artist creates for and the group that chooses to consume, and the art we like may simply be made for people like us. Anticipating the audience shapes development (in theater, will they be able to describe what they saw?). Jatinder Verma's question: how do you wake this person up, at "that dangerous moment of the unexpected"? Kurt Vonnegut: write to please just one person.

**How audiences work.**
- Size and diversity matter, as does solo versus shared experience. No two people experience a work the same way, nor does one person twice (a childhood book reread); Heraclitus: "You could not step twice into the same river." Public art is futile without its power source, a crowd (Peter Schjeldahl), and reactions are contagious (a clap, a laugh). Philip Ball: music creates social cohesion, and "Music is not a series of acoustic facts": the listener is active.
- Audiences shape and are shaped ("we transform what we look at"); feedback through responses and buying habits makes it a dialogue in both directions.
- Two ways to categorize an audience: **demographics** (who they are) and **preferences** (what they like). John Updike imagined his book found battered on a library shelf by a teenage boy east of Kansas.
- "Who will pay for my creation?" Without an audience and money, art is economically prohibitive: the nineteenth-century "well-made play" was built to fill seats and tills (Neil Grant), and patrons bought influence (flattering portraits, expensive paint) when, without copyright, association with the work was paramount.
- "Creating for someone influences what you create. Performing to someone can make us better": being watched helps focus.
- **Direct audiences** are permissionless; **indirect audiences** require a filter (people, technology, culture) that can amplify or cancel you. Sara Maitland: to tell stories you need to hear them, and you need an audience.

**Limit: the paradox.** The more you focus on the audience, the more you risk losing your authentic voice and producing something bland and generic. Some artists study the audience to cater to every detail, which taken too far is pandering; respect without pandering is a tightrope. You can fake likes, followers and reviews, but not genuine engagement. "Never forget your audience, but never let them dictate your creation"; the audience is your anchor, a silent collaborator and the ultimate judge, a cocreator rather than a passive receiver (a sunset painting can bring peace or melancholy).

**Case: influencing your audience (Clarence Darrow).** Designing for a specific reaction, even provocation, requires knowing your audience well enough to anticipate it.
- *Leopold and Loeb.* Richard Loeb and Nathan Leopold Jr., eighteen and nineteen, had confessed to killing fourteen-year-old Bobby Franks; the goal was to avoid the death penalty, and Darrow genuinely believed his argument. He chose the judge, John Caverly, over a jury by pleading guilty and focusing on sentencing. He "spoke to three distinct audiences" (John A. Farrell): the judge, with a compelling legal reason; America, on the evil of capital punishment; and Chicago, since the judge was a politician and public opinion had to move. He spoke for hours, not overly technical, on values and emotion, until women in the room, including Caverly's wife and sister, wept. And he gave Caverly an out: no one so young had been executed in Illinois. "Precedent was the key"; the sentences were life. Darrow believed outcomes rested on likability.
- *The Scopes trial.* The jury came from around Dayton, Tennessee, so he wooed the locals, learned the town and kept his delivery accessible, while telegraph keys carried it to millions. He got William Jennings Bryan on the stand to admit the Bible isn't all literal: a legal defeat that shaped public opinion and made the trial a landmark.
- *Ossian and Henry Sweet* and nine others in Detroit, charged after defending their home: the jury already believed in self-defense in the home for white people, so Darrow's task was to make them accept the same rights for Black people. He spoke for almost seven hours, and the defendants were found not guilty.
- The lens: we are often performing and someone is always watching. Your audience is at least one person you're trying to communicate with, through words, tone, body language and medium. "Thinking about how we reach, persuade, and build our audience is a powerful form of leverage."

## Genre: "Form shapes expectation."

**The model.** The benefits and pitfalls of grouping things together. Noticing similarities helps you make connections, but classifications that are too rigid miss new combinations; there's a sweet spot. Genre is a style or category of works, named by critics (film noir, jazz, sci-fi, still life), scholars (biography, editorial) and Aristotle (drama, lyric poetry); the novel is a genre of writing, the mystery a genre of story. Catharine Abell notes we classify by setting, content, medium, effects, tone, budget and origins, inconsistently.

**Facets.**
- **Genres change.** Todorov and Berrong: "A new genre is always the transformation of one or several old genres: by inversion, by displacement, by combination" (space opera, art deco). Genres are shaped by social forces and expectations (the novel rose in the eighteenth century with a leisured, moneyed class), so you can learn about a society from its genres (blues, Kabuki): the presence or absence of a genre reveals a central ideology.
- **Humans enjoy categories.** Chunking (as this series of models does) helps; Nolan Gasser notes we need genre labels to decide on a concert or discuss taste, and *The Age of Innocence* and *Pride and Prejudice* sit together as novels of manners, *Dawn of the Dead* and *Get Out* as horror. Genre narrows unlimited choice ("I feel like watching a comedy tonight").
- **Context helps and limits.** Abell: "we evaluate works according to how well they perform the purposes for which they are produced." Genre signals come from inside the work (a spaceship in the first scene) and outside it (the bookstore aisle sign); Trudier Harris calls it an umbrella that defines the terms of engagement, and Gasser a code for solidarity or division.
- "Genre is part of the contract between artist and audience." Neil Gaiman: "a set of assumptions, a loose contract between the creator and the audience," with points a reader would feel cheated without; it's "something to play to and play against."
- **Do conventions stifle?** There are many genres, with room inside each. Adorno: a work subordinate to its genre doesn't do it justice; it's more fruitful in conflict with it. Combining genres (jazzy rock, historical horror, punk ballet) still needs fresh conventions.
- **A label says what something is not.** Derrida: once a limit is drawn, norms and interdictions follow, and "The norm becomes visible—lives—only by its transgressions," which can found a new genre. The frisson of mixing requires identifying the genres; appreciate a genre to enjoy its thwarted expectations.

**Limit.** "Stick too closely to the conventions and your work may be dismissed as formulaic. Stray too far and you risk losing your audience." Unmet expectations make people feel wronged, and expectations shape what we see and miss: we see only what we expect. Genres evolve (rock went from blues and country in the 1950s to psychedelic, punk and grunge), and the transgressive becomes mainstream; art transcends genre by using convention as a springboard.

**Cases.**
- *Every living thing (biological classification).* To know what you are, know what you are not. At what point do microbes become us? Some ants look alike but reproduce differently. Rob Dunn: "boundaries between organisms are at best fuzzy ... between species more so." Naming brings things into collective consciousness; every culture names and groups species and builds stories, early on by harm, and you can't build a relationship with a maple or a wasp without classifying it. The lesson: classification is useful only as a **common language** (for marketing a comedy, for species names). Linnaeus, in the early eighteenth century, gave us the hierarchical *Homo sapiens* and *Canis lupus*, but organisms relate in multiple ways: hierarchy helps you guess which tree gives syrup, relational grouping (symbionts) helps with the bacteria in your body. "Classifications are only as good as their usefulness. Precision is subservient to function." A librarian's granularity (urban versus contemporary fantasy) finds historical horror like *Dracula*, but not books with elderly characters or Ukrainian literature. Dunn: no species definition is right, some are more practical. Don't be too rigid with your categories: "Classify for function, but don't demand only one function."
- *Artists and soldiers (DARPA).* Genre for people means roles, not pigeonholes. The instinct is to cross-pollinate (*Pride and Prejudice and Zombies*), but some things work best kept apart. Vannevar Bush, an advisor to President Franklin Roosevelt, chaired the Office of Scientific Research and Development from 1941 to explore "the bizarre," and it evolved into DARPA, against a military hostile to it for usurping power and budget. Safi Bahcall: as with ice and water, one molecule can't yell to its neighbors to loosen up; what matters are "the unique conditions under which two phases can coexist." Mingling would quash ideas: the "artists" (high-risk, early-stage ideas) need to be sheltered from the "soldiers" (the already successful, steady-growth part). Artists develop ideas; soldiers shape them for real-world application. Six Sigma efficiency risks stunting ideas, because innovation is rarely efficient (false starts, iterations) and looks foolish to soldiers until ideas filter out. DARPA today is a loose collection of small startups, with no career ladder and about a hundred program managers with extraordinary autonomy and visibility; it produced ARPANET and the Shakey robot. Treat both genres as equally important, neither better: recognize their strengths and insulate their weaknesses.

## Contrast: "Difference defines."

**The model.** Juxtaposition: placing two things together to show their difference, because "you can see what something is more clearly in the context of what it is not." A pickpocket bumps your shoulder (a high-contrast touch) while lifting your watch. Contrast is the arrangement of opposing elements, effects or content; it stimulates, captures attention and produces emphasis (a red apple on white and brown).
- Visual: light and dark, smooth and rough, large and small (Matisse's clashing color extremes; Julian Bell on Utamaro's "interfaces of patterned and plain, of covered and revealed"; Cindy Sherman's *Untitled*, where beauty disintegrates into the ersatz). A Nigerian king's bust from about 1200 CE pairs an outer likeness with an abstract cylinder for the inner spiritual man.
- Music: sound and silence, pace, intensity, tone.
- Literature: protagonist and antagonist; virtues show beside their opposites (Holmes beside Watson and Lestrade; Elizabeth Bennet beside Caroline Bingley).
- Theater: lighting, from early-nineteenth-century gaslights to limelight (a calcium flare), hence "in the limelight."
- Foreground and background: where is attention directed, where is the emphasis? Backgrounds are designed to make the foreground clearer. "We cannot see everything with perfect clarity all at once"; we understand things more clearly in juxtaposition.

Brains are wired to notice change and tune out the monotonous, so quiet makes the loud explosive, darkness helps us understand light and winter makes us appreciate spring.

**Cases.**
- *Contrast as context (data visualization).* The difference between a pretty picture and an informative one is context created by contrast, and the contrasted things needn't be opposites. Edward Tufte: "Compared with what?" is the fundamental analytical act, so displays should show comparisons. A hundred car accidents tells you little without comparison across region and time; John Snow's 1854 cholera map of Soho stacked deaths as bars that clustered near the Broad Street pump. Encourage the eye to compare at several levels of detail; ask first what content-reasoning tasks the display should help with; "data-rich designs give context and credibility." Avoid clutter: making something a different color isn't the contrast meant, and the goal is clarity "without sacrificing truth for beauty," with ink spent on data. "'Show comparisons' is the design principle." A move: the hot business ideas that attract the best and brightest are seldom the most attractive to enter, since your talent is compared with your rivals'. "In a room full of sneezers, it's hard to stand out"; unpopular fields offer a better chance.
- *Contrast in the universe (entropy).* Contrast needn't be a direct opposite, only enough difference to change the landscape. By the second law, a fish tank partitioned hot and cold goes lukewarm. Peter Atkins: a sneeze in a quiet library (a low-temperature, low-entropy setting) produces a big increase in entropy; on a busy street, little. To stand out, change the "temperature": raise it (a chicken costume on a busy street, a sale sign, a billboard) or lower it (a spa or park amid overstimulation). Sometimes leave it alone (stay quiet in the library, out of norms and respect). "Wisdom comes in assessing any situation and knowing whether contrast—raising or lowering the temperature of the situation—might lead to a breakthrough."

## Framing: "Context shapes meaning."

**The model.** We never see everything, and framing helps us identify the constructed frames around information: "The frame decides what we see and miss." Ansel Adams: "A good photograph is knowing where to stand." Framing is fundamental to all art, which is selective (a book, a documentary's edits, a portrait's boundaries) and conveys intent, story and focus. Frames are the mental constructs we use to make sense of the world, and mental models are themselves a set of frames. Framing compresses something complicated into what you want people to see (Farnam Street's tagline: "Mastering the best of what other people have already figured out"). "Art is compression": a close-up, a spotlight, a gilded edge, a melody against its embellishments.

**How frames work.**
- An art form's frame is a set of expectations (an aside to the audience, or candy wrappers in the theater). Susanne K. Langer on bunraku: the audience ignores the visible puppeteers. Frames shape how to approach a work (don't talk during the symphony; suspend disbelief), and the artist must put into the frame what's needed.
- Art expands frames: Camille Paglia on Mondrian discarding the frame. Erving Goffman found "considerable resistance to changing their framework of frameworks," so we may dislike art because it's outside our frame, not because it's bad; he also found audiences have a great capacity to "adjust and calibrate," and social sensibilities change (theatergoers once threw objects).
- What's in and out of the frame shapes historical narrative: Jacques-Louis David's *The Death of Marat* made him more muscular and erased his blisters. Painters can position the viewer: Caspar David Friedrich's figures turn their backs to direct our gaze; Manet's *At the Café* puts us behind the bar.
- The same options presented differently change choices ("Is it a muffin or a cake?"); marketing frames a car as status, adventure or family, a watch as punctuality or prestige. Framing works subtly, emotionally, subconsciously, a "silent partner in every communication" that can illuminate or obscure, empower or limit.

**Moves.**
- Consider the frame of the person presenting the information.
- Ask what has been left out of the frame: "it's always suspicious when all of the information that's been presented perfectly supports the position."
- Consider your own frames, and how to change them.

**Cases.**
- *History as frame (Nur Jahan).* Historical records are frames: they exclude the irrelevant, the inaccurate, the incendiary and the unknown, and they compress time. "When people frame something, they're making a statement not about the objective truth but about themselves." Nur Jahan (Ruby Lal's *Empress*), the twentieth and favorite wife of the Mughal emperor Jahangir, married in 1611, issued orders, built gardens and buildings, and struck coins in her name. Contemporaries called her "cunning and conniving"; Europeans, "manipulative and mysterious," "haughty and stubborn," with only the emperor's love to explain her power ("a gold-digger and a schemer"). Nineteenth-century British orientalism, Indian films and comics reduced her to romance; "she is not discussed as a leader," and academics leaned on the love story. The frames leave out her anger, playfulness and ambition. Her birth is the one certainty, outside Kandahar in the winter of 1577 on the road to India, yet by the eighteenth century three embellished versions existed, all by men, revealing their tellers and enlarging the contents to direct focus (mysticism, destiny). Shah Jahan's official historians wrote her out, but her coins (she was the only Mughal woman to issue them), formal paintings and monument inscriptions survive. "We are always interacting with framed information."
- *Updating our frames (Walter Cronkite).* Frames emerge from experience, inherited or learned, and we resist changing them because we must admit we were wrong; it's "both difficult and brave to process and share our changing frames." Cronkite of the *CBS Evening News* had been a World War II correspondent for United Press, where reporters helped the military win. His Vietnam coverage was balanced (White House statements alongside the US marines' destruction of Cam Ne; an interview with Ho Chi Minh): a "cautious hawk" (Douglas Brinkley) in the first widely televised US war. On his first visit in 1965 the reality differed, and he sought varied sources, but until early 1968 he took no public position. After the Tet offensive he returned to "be your own eyewitness" with a "mind wide open," asking whether the government line was a lie (Mark Bowden): a betrayal he'd correct even if it meant abandoning neutrality. He wore a helmet and flak jacket, flew out with body bags, and found morale in shambles and officers misunderstanding the North Vietnamese. In "Report from Vietnam" on February 27, 1968, he called the war "mired in stalemate" and urged negotiating "not as victors, but as an honorable people." His opponents were hard to dismiss as "hippies"; it was a tipping point and a blow to the Johnson administration, and "stalemate," a middling position, moved the antiwar view into the mainstream. Polls calling the war a mistake ran 25 percent in March 1966, 53 percent in August 1968 and 60 percent in 1973; in 1965, 90 percent told Gallup they'd never felt the urge to demonstrate, while by 1990 a quarter wished they'd protested harder. He didn't let his past blind him to his present. "How something is compressed and positioned can make the difference between ten people seeing it and one million."

## Rhythm: "Pace yourself."

**The model.** The pattern and pace at which things happen; rhythm synchronizes us and gives purpose and direction. The model asks what we can change and accomplish by modifying a rhythm.
- In music it's the element of time: recurring strong and weak notes and rests; Sara Adhitya calls it the "relation between repetition and difference." It sets when, how long and at what intensity; if musical movement is a wave, it's "rhythm that determines the shape of the wave."
- Pleasure lies in anticipation, uncertain payoffs at low stakes, meeting and thwarting expectations (a tempo change, layered rhythms). "Too much predictability is boring; too little is disorienting." Philip Ball: an "asymmetry of events" avoids monotony (human speech versus a robot's). John Powell: we're equally surprised by increases or reductions in loudness, or sudden silence; a change in pattern triggers attention.
- It isn't universal (some music lacks a steady pulse) and varies by culture, and we prefer what we already understand, what the ear is attuned to. **Polyrhythm** layers rhythms (drums-only African music).
- Heart rate entrains to music, so the brain experiences the matching emotion. Speed is the clearest mood indicator (fast for happiness, fear or anger; slow for tenderness or sadness), though not universally; David Byrne notes happy rhythms override melancholy melodies in flamenco and salsa.
- Rhythm runs through poetry, dance and prose (Virginia Woolf: "All writing is nothing but putting words on the backs of rhythm"), through language's cadence and meter, and through routines, habits and cycles of work and rest, activity and reflection, giving structure, meaning and shared togetherness. Regularity and variation together (jazz syncopation, classical shifts) make it work.

**Cases.**
- *Daily rhythms.* Adhitya: the rhythms with most impact are those repeated most often, the everyday ones: heartbeat, breath, circadian rhythm, seasons, sunrise and sunset, moon and tides, the workday, transportation, a city's activity. Biological clocks are internal; sunlight entrains them but doesn't tell us when to get up. Foster and Kreitzman: behavior is "anticipatory of regular and predictable daily environmental changes ... rather than reactive," with clocks that run without external calibration, which makes evolutionary sense as preparation for optimal conditions. Humans create their own conditions (artificial light, air conditioning), and it may not be good for us to ignore natural rhythms: "success still comes, just as it did for the ancient Egyptians, from anticipating the future and organizing to meet it." Created rhythms (traffic, lawnmowers, commutes, store hours) make urban life "a complex, polyrhythmic composition" whose sound informs how we navigate. Jan Gehl found uniform, inactive facades make people hurry, while varied, active ones make them linger and socialize; design can speak to internal rhythms to create the urban experience you want, because the rhythm we follow affects how we feel. Octavio Paz: rhythm provokes expectation and yearning, and interruption is a shock.
- *Marching in time (military drill).* A rhythm experienced by many at once needs no training, which makes it a tool for group cohesion (and efficiency, in rowing and walking): moving to a beat lets you anticipate others and organize. The Canadian Armed Forces' drill manual: "drill is the basis of all teamwork," building operational effectiveness (march and maneuver as one), discipline, alertness, precision, pride, steadiness and cohesion. Its standard pause is two beats of quick time, and **cadence** is "the number of beats to the minute," kept by drums or metronome. Drill lets forces move, mass and maneuver and react automatically, because "In order to rely on people, you need to trust them. In order to trust others, you need to be able to predict them." It builds instinct and esprit de corps, de-emphasizing the individual for the unit. The beat is neither arbitrary nor indefinite: it runs for a set time to coordinate specific movements, then remains a structure to return to when there's little time for analysis, so others can anticipate your movements and work with you confidently.

## Melody: "Tones tell tales."

**The model.** A tool for identifying the connections that tie elements together and communicate the whole picture. Melody is the most memorable part of music: "Melody is like the first principles of a piece of music: you can change many other aspects, but if the melody is the same, the music is still recognizable." Nolan Gasser compares it to a human face.
- It's a succession of notes arranged into phrases, with pauses between, sometimes several repeating melodies; a horizontal presentation of pitch. Removable notes are embellishments.
- Listeners have an astonishing ability to spot the intended tune and know where a melody won't go (Powell). Its value comes from the relationships among elements and from context and expectation, a balance of familiarity and surprise (Ball). The process is unconscious pattern seeking, chunking, context from prior notes, expectation and comparison with memory, using four grouping skills: **similarity, proximity, good continuation, common fate** (and grouping by an instrument's timbre). Powell: "A tune [melody] is always on its way somewhere." Listening is active.
- Emotional impact makes a melody memorable (Byrne writes lyrics to match melodic lines), and songs get tied to feelings and moments. Melodies are culturally influenced; Gino Stefani: the popular ones are suited for appropriation in more ways, for more purposes, which gives them staying power. They're shared among different groups, and Rousseau saw them as tools of political organization: something in common without everyone being the same. Stefani: you fight a haunting melody with another melody.
- A great melody has a shape, contour and arc, with inevitability yet freshness, and works like language (rising for hope, falling for sadness, leaps adventurous, steps intimate). The best balance the familiar and the new, giving unity and coherence amid noise.

**Octaves everywhere** (sidebar). John Newlands's 1865 Law of Octaves held that every eighth element repeats the properties of the first. He was ridiculed, yet it predicted missing elements, and he was later recognized as the discoverer of the Periodic Law.

**Cases.**
- *How many Batmans are there? (comics).* We like familiar things (cover songs at the local bar). Iconic characters are like melodies: Spider-Man played by Tobey Maguire, Andrew Garfield or Tom Holland keeps his basic melody. DC came first (Superman, Batman); Marvel took the sales lead in the 1960s (Spider-Man, Captain America). With a new comic every few weeks (DC released 343 in 1962), the archives were searched: the Flash, canceled in 1949, was revived in 1959, as was the Justice League, and in 1963 Stan Lee and Jack Kirby teamed up existing heroes to meet a deadline (Reed Tucker). Tight turnarounds breed **availability bias**: Steve Gerber's Wundarr borrowed from Superman. You keep the elements that make Wonder Woman: "The melody must be maintained." The 1970s brought yearly "event" issues, but "when everything is an event, nothing is"; by 1990 Marvel ran a fourth Spider-Man title, "#1s" multiplied (Wolverine restarted three times since 2010) and #1 lost its currency. *Watchmen* (1986) was the exception, playing a longer game. "When you are under a time crunch, you are much more likely to stick with what you know."
- *Common elements in uncommon groups (the Silk Road).* Melody is the path of a song, the common element linking its parts; metaphorically, look for common elements in groups that seem to have little in common. The Silk Road ran from the Pacific to the Mediterranean, with trade as early as 3000 BCE and, by the second millennium BCE, a chain of links almost 5,000 miles long, "the world's central nervous system" (Peter Frankopan). Silk was "an international currency as well as a luxury product," a recognizable commodity. Ideas traveled too (zero; Judaism, Christianity, Islam and Buddhism; siege warfare, transport, agriculture, ceramics, metals), in both directions with no primacy, through hubs like Kandahar and Merv. The constants amid diversity, silk and spices at remote outposts (Indian spices in Mainz around 700 CE; silk in Viking graves), were a melody that oriented travelers and gave context (a silk wearer was likely powerful). By about 1000 CE merchants were secure regardless of faith or war, since trade requires working together. It was the "largest single network of exchanges on earth before the sixteenth century," with no homogeneity: before the Mongol empire's end in 1368 few people traveled its length and accurate geographical and cultural knowledge didn't travel (David Christian), but goods and ideas did. Christian finds an "underlying unity" in Afro-Eurasia: common recognizable elements under local particularities (silk, silver, accounting, paper) make working together easier.

## Representation: "Influence through presentation."

**The model.** Look at the purpose behind something and beyond its surface: what might we understand if we dig into why things appear the way they do? Visual art represents things, so ask why this thing, and why this way out of a thousand. "Representation involves choice," and choices reveal purpose: cave paintings to influence hunts, tomb art for prosperity in the afterlife, church paintings for Bible stories, Chinese paintings for meditation, portraits for posterity, historical scenes for perspective, and work that shows personality, offers commentary or expands art.
- Representation isn't always literal. For the Egyptians "everything had to be represented from its most characteristic angle," showing what they knew existed rather than what they saw; they followed rules and weren't bad artists, and the rules tell you what they valued. Buddhist Chinese landscapes use few strokes ("the awe he must have felt"); Muslim artists barred from depicting figures turned to shape and color patterns; the Impressionists painted light, movement and impression, responding to "a temporary pattern of stimuli to the retina" (Julian Bell). Later Egyptian art was "hooked on the tension between nature and the ideal"; Lysippos showed people "not as they are, but as they appear."
- Visual constraints shape it: Gu Hongzhong's *Night Revels of Han Xizai* (about 1070) recedes at 45 degrees for smooth reading.
- Who paid shapes it: governments, religious institutions, patrons, galleries, the public. A thousand years of Virgin Mary paintings exist because the Catholic Church had the money and the walls. The first Qin emperor (died 210 BCE) left more than 6,000 individualized terra-cotta warriors at Xi'an, "representing facts faithfully on an industrial scale."
- Commonality of culture is the "mutually intelligible use of various representational systems" (Whitney Davis), and problems arise when we judge others' representations as products of our own system. The unfamiliar gets dismissed as "not art." Gombrich: "We are all inclined to judge pictures by what we know rather than what we see." Rothko expanded representation with few forms.

**The move.** "Liking (or disliking) something and understanding something are two different activities." Look at what is represented and how; the context offers insight into the culture behind it, a gateway to the artist's world.

**Limit.** Representation is mental shorthand, something standing in for something else (word for object, map for territory, note for sound), since we can't hold all of reality. But "representation is not neutral. Every representation is an interpretation" (an emoji versus lived experience) and always a kind of distortion: good representation reveals hidden truths, bad representation misleads and reinforces stereotypes. And it shapes reality: art shifts attitudes, narratives sway elections and models guide policy, a feedback loop between map and territory.

**Cases.**
- *The value of open-source intelligence.* Most intelligence comes from open sources, **OSINT**: freely public, unclassified material (newspapers, radio, manuals, social media). H. Akin Ünver defines intelligence as "the methodical collection of high-value information in a way that yields comparative advantage to decision makers"; early CIA managers believed 80 percent came from foreign publications, radio and people with general knowledge (Kathy Peiss). The value is in analysis, which needs foreknowledge of what's "out there" to put clues in context. Through the representation lens, what is represented gives clues to the surrounding context: "Evaluating what an enemy puts out into the world is a great way of understanding them." Combine sources by keyword and category and juxtapose them to reveal trends. In World War II, periodicals, manuals and directories revealed troop strength, weaponry and production; society pages revealed regiment locations; gossip columns hinted at scandals. Richard Hill, a music librarian, surveyed concert reviews, theater absences, opera changes and refund notices in the *Deutsche Allgemeine Zeitung* to infer the timing, location and extent of bomb damage in Berlin, reading not the cancellations but what they represented. OSINT gives the broad context that narrow clandestine intelligence can't ("strategic relativity"). Analysts "do not stop at the representation but go deeper by asking, what does that representation tell me about my adversary?"
- *Powerful women (the chess queen).* How things are and how they're represented are symbiotic. The earliest sets, in India around the sixth century, had a weak vizier beside the king, moving one diagonal square; today the queen is the most powerful piece. Her rise correlates with the rise of female power in European monarchies, and was unlikely without a corresponding reality in society. Marilyn Yalom: the queen appeared after Arabs brought chess to southern Europe in the eighth century, replaced the vizier around 1000 and by 1200 had spread from Italy to Norway. The board reflected feudal structures, was played across classes and by men and women together, a "perfect representation of a social order in which everyone was expected to know his or her exact place." Christian monogamy made the king incomplete without a queen, and women inherited regencies; multiple queens via pawn promotion were at first unacceptable, since she was the king's only permissible conjugal mate. She kept the vizier's moves at first and gained full power by the end of the fifteenth century, alongside reigning queens such as Urraca of León-Castile (1109–1126), Eleanor of Aquitaine (queen of France 1137–1152 and of England 1154–1189), Margaret of Denmark (Denmark, Norway and Sweden, 1387–1412) and Isabella I of Castile, whose wars and exploration left a mixed legacy (the exploitation of the Americas, the Spanish Inquisition). A 1497 Spanish book called the chess queen the most potent piece, and Valencian players may have modeled her on Isabella. The piece also drew vitriol, used as an analogy to attack all women. Games, like art, represent norms, ideas and ideals, who we are or want to be; Yalom calls the chess queen the "quintessential metaphor for female power in the Western World."

## Supporting Idea: Balance

Victor Hugo: equilibrium is good, harmony is better. Balance is the arrangement of elements that feels beautiful, stable and harmonious, "when things come together just right."
- In visual art, line, texture, color, space and form combine into visual stability, which is harmony; there's no formula, and sketches show ongoing experimentation (Gombrich on Raphael finding "the right balance between the figures"). A 2D work needs space to be observable; a 3D work must physically stand as well as balance visually.
- Beyond art: a balanced diet, work-life balance, a balanced view; left and right, up and down, foreground and background. Balance compromises competing goals; Gombrich: "suggestion of space and the faithful imitation of reality must not be allowed to destroy the balance of the composition."
- Humans prefer symmetry: facial symmetry signaled health and genetic fitness, symmetrical text (*The New Yorker*'s columns) is easier to process, and flowers and shells show it, as bilateral symmetry has for at least five hundred million years. But balance needn't be perfect symmetry: asymmetry can be balanced by shading, or lines that offset clusters.
- It isn't universal (culture, preference); Mondrian's *Tableau I* asks the eye to judge relations and balances, and Schjeldahl finds stability "poised at a breaking point." Gombrich likens it to arranging flowers, balancing without being able to say what harmony is. The more you learn, the easier it is to know when something is as good as it can be. "Life is the pursuit of balance." Barbara Kingsolver: "we are our injuries, as much as we are our successes."

## Plot: "Sequence shapes story."

**The model.** A story's road map, a chain of connected events. Elizabeth Bowen: "Plot is the knowing of destination." Organizing events into a plot helps us make sense of them, with two red flags: being swayed by the better of two plots, and letting the stories we tell ourselves blind us to new information.
- Causality lends believability (Gustav Freytag), and plot is "heavily dependent on the circulation of information" (Marie-Laure Ryan). Freytag's five elements: **exposition, rising action** (internal or external conflict), **climax, falling action, resolution**. Nonfiction has its own arc: introduction, background, new information, putting it together, conclusion, the aha moment.
- E. M. Forster: "The king died and then the queen died" is a story; "...died of grief" is a plot. Conflict is the "primary source of narrative interest": person against person, nature, society or self, a character wanting something and facing obstacles. Plot is a skeleton that needs characters, setting, emotions, desires and details; Aristotle held it the core of drama, above character. Plot is a crucible for character: if the character is unchanged, the story went nowhere.
- A plot doesn't mirror reality; it's something special worth telling. Some resolution is necessary (audiences dislike the *deus ex machina*), plot holes break it, and deliberate subversion jars.
- There are two plots: the author's storyline and the characters' own plots (their goals, plans and schemes, in Ryan's terms). Each choice closes doors, and earlier choices constrain later ones.

**The move.** Plot follows causality and life doesn't, so "plot is the most useful lens to help counteract narrative bias": are you assuming a relationship between events that doesn't exist? Conversely, know when you need to craft a better story. "The most powerful story in the world is the one you tell yourself about the obstacles and challenges in front of you. A positive story doesn't always ensure success, but a negative one almost guarantees failure." Stories are hard to change once rooted: "Change the story to change the results."

**Chekhov's gun and red herrings** (sidebar). Stephen King's version: a gun on the mantel in Act I must go off in Act III, and the reverse is true. In a story everything serves a purpose, and foreshadowing is a promise to the audience. A *red herring* deliberately misleads, and a plot twist breaks the previous chain of causality. The limit: in real life not everything present matters; there are coincidences and props that mean nothing. "Life is not a novel."

**Cases.**
- *Who told the better story? (Kepler's mother).* We're narrative creatures: a story about ice density is more memorable than a presentation of it. "Although facts matter, the story you tell with them matters even more. Often, whoever can tell the better story is the one who wins the audience." In 1615 Katharina Kepler, mother of the astronomer Johannes Kepler (also an optics scholar, a devout Lutheran and a defender of Copernicus), was accused of witchcraft and jailed for more than a year. The forty-nine accusations followed a simple cause and effect: "Something bad ... happened, and Katharina was in the vicinity..., so she must have caused it." In Leonberg, in the duchy of Württemberg, witchcraft explained misfortune, though the Lutheran leadership was cautious (Ulinka Rublack). The accuser was Ursula Reinbold, the depositions contradicted one another, and skeptics applied common-sense causal criteria. The case would be decided by law professors in Tübingen, and they were Kepler's audience. He brought mathematics and logic, experience with patrons and with resolving rival hypotheses; he questioned his mother about the people of Leonberg, put detailed questions to each witness and used legal reasoning to discredit every one (too young, inconsistent, linked to Reinbold's rumors), replacing witchcraft's cause and effect with contemporary science's for all forty-nine (natural disease, bias, family quarrels, mishaps). Dozens of pages against a one-page answer: she was shown the executioner's instruments but not tortured, and released into her family's care. He saved her by telling a better story to the audience that controlled her fate.
- *Stories we tell ourselves (pain relief).* Technological change comes with narrative change. Until the mid-nineteenth century people gritted their teeth (with drink or willow bark) through polio, smallpox, amputations and rotten teeth, and early painkillers were met with indifference and used recreationally for decades. Joseph Priestley produced nitrous oxide in 1772, and Humphry Davy noted its pain-killing effect in 1800 in a throwaway remark; it stayed recreational until the mid- to late nineteenth century, about a hundred years before medicine adopted it, and ether and opiates followed the same path while patients declined them (Joanna Bourke). Through the lens of plot, the social narrative had no expectation of a life free of suffering: (1) attitudes to pain tracked the value placed on groups, and denying pain dehumanized soldiers, women, children, the elderly, immigrants, the working classes and slaves (a 1939 author claimed children feel no pain in minor surgery); (2) the familiar sequence felt normal, so surgeons disliked silent, unmoving patients, and patients were disturbed by unconsciousness, wanted control and distrusted the "unnatural" and its side effects; (3) bearing pain was positive, a hallmark of self-control and bravery and a religious virtue securing heaven ("If I don't experience pain stoically anymore, am I still a good person?"). Narratives change: nitrous oxide is now routine at the dentist. Then the pendulum swung too far, to the belief that no one should feel pain, though pain is a biological signal. In the 1980s the WHO advocated aggressive opioid use for anyone with "pain," US agencies tied reimbursement to patients' perception of pain control, and addiction, illegal opioids, overdoses and crime followed, on flimsy evidence and pharmaceutical lies about addictiveness, against the backdrop of a belief that pain is inhumane. There's no real evidence of long-term benefit for chronic pain; opioids slow wound healing and mask severity, and post-surgery pain should decrease, a signal they hide. The lesson: "change in how we do something quite often requires a narrative change."

## Character: "Heroes and villains."

**The model.** Who we identify with influences how we perceive events, and the model helps you identify who is telling the story. Information is crafted and shaped by its providers; understand how they relate to the larger story to see how they've interpreted and packaged it. We can become very connected to characters, giving unreal things immense power.
- A character is a being with agency: personality, values, quirks, a story. The protagonist (who needn't be good) faces an antagonist or obstacles: a villain, a norm-enforcing group, internal limitations, all within a web of relationships.
- The gap between what characters want and what's happening drives conflict; Ryan: we want them to appear "autonomous agents ... rather than puppets of authorial whimsy." Vonnegut: make every character want something, even a glass of water.
- Characters are dynamic or static; conflict and crisis develop them, against another character or society, against themselves, or against nature (physical, psychological, spiritual). "Obstacles reveal character," and the journey changes them (Scrooge).
- **Stock characters** have no depth: generalizations and props whose motivations are taken for granted. **Direct characterization** tells (rude and entitled); **indirect** shows (shouting at the waitstaff).
- For Aristotle plot came first, but for audiences characters often seem most important and vivid, and engaging characters can rescue weak plots. Negative characters may resonate more and be more memorable. A great character is a bundle of traits and often a paradox; "character is destiny."
- Understanding character lets you step into others' shoes, predict and empathize. Character isn't set in stone: "Easy choices in the moment almost always makes the future harder. Harder choices in the moment often makes the future easier," and "No single choice satisfies the pursuit, only repeated steps in the right direction."

**Archetypes** (sidebar). Archetypes recur across history and cultures (Jung tied them to the collective unconscious, surfacing in dreams, religion and art); the Greek root means "original pattern." They resonate and are hard to avoid, but books whose archetypal characters never evolve aren't stories but polemics or morality lessons disguised as stories.

**Cases.**
- *Cults of personality (Sun Yat-sen).* Secondary characters have little inner life and no arc, yet they're necessary, and their contrast enriches the main character. Regimes consolidate power through storytelling (Nazi Germany, Stalin's Russia): the leader as protagonist, secondary characters to highlight him. A cult of personality is an "idealized, even god-like, public image ... shaped and molded through constant propaganda and media exposure" (Wright and Lauer), and cults also form around historical figures to legitimize current leaders. Sun Yat-sen, born 1866, aligned with many, including the Soviet Communists. He didn't lead the transition after the imperial collapse of 1911; the Nationalists took over in 1928 and Mao's Communists in 1949. He was complex: he raised awareness, had opponents killed, and by Jung Chang's account was a womanizer who used his wife as bait. After his death in 1925 his brother-in-law Chiang Kai-shek aligned with his legacy, building a "Lenin-style cult" around "the Father of China," "the greatest man in the 5,000-year history of China," with statues, weekly commemorations and a mausoleum of 30 million square meters against 1.7 million for the last emperor's. It lent legitimacy: heroes inspire and are trusted, but are stripped of complexity into superheroes, and Chiang shared in the heroics. The Communists gave Sun a supporting role: for the ninetieth anniversary in 1956, his widow Ching-ling wrote in the *People's Daily* that he was China's Lenin and the Communist Party had "took over his mission," the Nationalists an aberration; Key Ray Chong and Fang-fu Luan (1988) found the party using Sun as a means to its ends in crises. Sun became like a stock character.
- *Emotional engagement with nonliving things (Barbie).* We're devastated by fictional deaths, attack authors and stop buying; caring keeps us coming back. Barbie, since 1959, is either a playmate or an unattainable vision of womanhood. She was marketed as a character: Barbara Millicent Roberts of Willows, Wisconsin, daughter of George and Margaret, student at Manhattan International High School (Tanya Lee Stone), with Ken, friends, family and more than 120 careers, so people talk about her by name. She's a friend for acting out dreams (vet, novelist, teacher), an "empty container to be filled at will." Germaine Greer noted her non-functional body, feet too tiny to stand on, which is true except that she was never human: we critique her because we see her as a character. Ethnic Barbies kept the same form, though there's no one way to represent women; Ann Treneman: a real Barbie would have a nervous breakdown. Stone: "she is not just a doll," and her first TV commercial planted the illusion that she was "real!" The lens: when we turn something into a character, as we naturally do, we imbue it with a realness that engages our emotions, and that investment doesn't go away easily.

## Setting: "Where the action happens."

**The model.** Where something happens influences what happens. Environment changes behavior as much as behavior changes environment, yet too often we think we can move the action to a new location and get the same result. Carmen Maria Machado: setting isn't inert; point of view activates it.
- Setting is place and time, the context for events and decisions, carrying hints and feelings (dark and stormy versus sunny). Nonfiction, even a collection of facts like physics, has a setting: consider the time and place information came from rather than assuming it applies everywhere and forever.
- Settings nest (Berlin in 1975, then the guardhouse at Checkpoint Charlie, then an apartment over the Tiergarten) and must be consistent (no horse-drawn carriages). Setting determines elements of plot and character (no cell phones or internet in 1975).
- It's subjective: description from a narrator or character tells you about them, and "a single physical setting prompts vastly different reactions from different inhabitants." It's fluid, interacting with character and plot (a graveyard). Settings carry archetypes and stock elements (a city is fast-paced; a Western works in the American West of 1920 or on Tatooine, not in downtown Beijing).
- Settings are valuable constraints that narrow choices, in performance too: "You are always constrained by the space you are in." David Byrne: "we work backward ... creating work that fits the venue available to us." African drum music played outdoors keeps its intricate rhythms from being mashed together as they would be in a gym or cathedral ("music perfectly fits the place"); symphonic music grew into large halls with texture and sonic shock and awe; around 1900 audiences began to sit and watch, which allowed extreme dynamics and quiet passages; the 1960s arenas and stadiums, with terrible acoustics, gave us arena rock.
- Setting is an active participant that enables or hinders, reveals or conceals: "not just where the story happens ... it's why the story happens." Social, cultural and historical context defines what is possible and permissible (medieval Europe versus modern Tokyo; a gossipy village versus an anonymous city). And it runs both ways: characters act on their setting, a reciprocal relationship.

**The move.** "Where you do something influences what you can do": look at what's around you, because your choices and options are confined by where you are. "Setting is the silent force that influences our fate," so "to change your behavior, change your environment. If you don't, it will change you."

**Cases.**
- *Work with what you've got (cuisine).* Not everything is available everywhere, and place shapes content. Cuisines were shaped by local growing conditions, water, daylight, soil and animals, by clay or copper pots, open pits or ash ovens, and by the fuel available hundreds of years ago. Wealthy sixteenth-century English homes roasted meat by an open fire, turned for hours and carved with special knives, because the "English were abundantly well-endowed with firewood": "The roast beef of England reflected a densely wooded landscape" and grass (Bee Wilson). The knock-on effects: carving became part of dining, with many knives (for stag or oxen, for game birds, spatulas for serving, credence knives for crumbs), and the Sunday roast survives. The Ottoman Empire's sherbet: "After water, the principal beverage was sherbet" (Priscilla Mary Isin), adjusted to weather, place and drinker, chilled with ice in summer and served hot and spiced in winter. Snow collected in winter filled shops "as numerous as butchers," and since it melted quickly it was cheap enough for the poor, which made sherbet accessible and woven into religious feasts, weddings, new motherhood and welcoming travelers. Its flavors were limited only by availability and preservation (pomegranate, lemon, violet, tamarind, mulberry and more); the poor drank chilled water with honey, the sultan coconut, rosewater, musk and ambergris, and syrups, pastes and tablets were prepared in season. It spread to Europe and adapted: Italy's pistachio, strawberry and fennel became *sorbetto*, and in mid-seventeenth-century England sugar replaced honey and baking soda added fizz. The lesson: "our setting impacts us far more than we realize"; constraints define the range of choices, and shared things adapt to local constraints.
- *The first zero.* Every story takes place somewhere, and a setting has rules even when made up. Art is made and consumed in a setting (stage dialogue versus film dialogue); logical inconsistencies make people tune out (a Tokyo chase can't disobey physics), and art struggles outside its intended setting (a painting in dim light, a symphony read off the score). "No art can be separated from its setting." Zero is ubiquitous now but grew out of a specific setting, and it isn't intuitive (you never see zero deer). Very few cultures invented it; the Mayans had a representation for nothing but it wasn't part of their number system and never spread. Pythagoras's theorem and Euclid's *Elements* did without it; Tobias Dantzig: "The concrete mind of the ancient Greeks could not conceive the void as a number, let alone endow the void with a symbol." You must understand the void before you can name it. Amir Aczel: zero grants "the ability of the numbers to cycle so that the same signs could be used over and over again to mean different things," as a placeholder (65 versus 605 versus 6,050); it was thought invented for commerce, but predates it. The oldest known zero, a dot rather than a circle, is in an Old Khmer inscription (stone K-127) from the temple of Trapang Pei at Sambor on the Mekong, found in 1891 by a French archaeologist and translated by George Coedès. Aczel argued its invention depended on a place where people already appreciated emptiness. Western culture is binary (good and evil, mind and body; A cannot be not-A), while early Buddhist logic's *catuskoti* allows four possibilities: is, is not, both, neither (an average dinner is neither good nor bad; a child leaving home is both great and devastating). Thich Nhat Hanh: "Emptiness is the Middle Way between existent and nonexistent"; for Aczel, existence is 1, non-existence -1, emptiness 0. A monk counting breaths reaches the void when he stops, which is zero: "It came from Buddhist meditation," in Cambodia around the sixth century CE. "Innovations are extensions of the world in which they originate." Older zeros have since been found in Indonesia, in the Srivijaya empire, also Buddhist: "the oldest thing found is only the oldest until the next oldest one is found."

## Supporting Idea: Subtext

Meaning on two levels: the **text**, given directly, and the **subtext**, conveyed without being stated. We pick up subtext intuitively, and it's common in speech ("What time does this meeting end?" can mean "Is it rude for me to leave now?"). It enriches the experience with multiple levels; it's usually deliberate, but can emerge unnoticed by the artist or be projected by the audience, and characters may or may not be aware of it. It's a sign of mastery, in the spirit of "Show, don't tell."
- Paul Nash, an official British war artist in World War I, painted landscapes with little war iconography, leaving people, planes and weapons out, so that the subtext was war's impact on people. "To convey something in a subtle fashion can have far more impact than stating it outright": the audience works harder and engages more, and characters become more believable (Orwell's *Animal Farm*).
- Under oppression, subtext carries messages that would otherwise be censored, with no actual critique present. Arthur Miller's *The Crucible* used the Salem witch trials to mirror 1950s McCarthyism, the same paranoia and hysteria, where direct criticism would have had him labeled a communist. "Because subtext is implied and subjective, an oppressive regime may not be able to take it as proof of dissent."

## Performance: "Break a leg."

**The model.** The live interaction between performers and their environment is unique; no two performances are alike. As a model, it asks you to factor in how engaging with the world subtly yet profoundly influences actions. Marina Abramović: "The essence of performance is that the audience and the performer make the piece together."
- Theater uses physical things to tell a story and requires three elements: **actor, space, and audience**. Story isn't required: Greek tragedy (*Oedipus Rex*, *Antigone*) instructed on virtues and flaws; Noh drama has "often little or no plot; conflict ... is absent"; Antonin Artaud's Theater of Cruelty (1940s) made words less important than action, rhythm, sound and gesture.
- Improvisation: sixteenth-century Italian commedia dell'arte had no script, only "an agreed framework of scenario"; in seventeenth-century England and Spain, "pit" audiences mocked and launched missiles at the performers, forcing improvised changes.
- The space offers limits and opportunities, including unconventional spaces (theater in a restaurant among its regular patrons). Audience engagement varies: today dark and hushed; in sixteenth-century China hours or days of eating and drinking; in Kabuki, attention on the set pieces; in eighteenth-century France the audience was banished from the stage; Bertolt Brecht (1920s to 1940s) sought alienation and a "new objectivity."
- Audience and space change with each show (Declan Donnellan: the opening-night play differs from the closing), a dynamic component with the possibility of the unexpected that a movie lacks; Jatinder Verma: mistakes become part of the performance. Abramović: the unknown is embedded, since you can't predict the audience. A recording of a live concert disappoints, with its auditory, visual and social dimensions and the sound bouncing off the walls all reduced (Byrne).
- "The peculiar chemistry engendered by live performance lends it a power," which is why theater has fallen foul of authority: fourteenth-century English Robin Hood plays were "suppressed as dangerously subversive." "The greater the reach, the greater the influence, the larger the potential threat."
- Performance is the art of the ephemeral: presence, immediacy and intimacy, "an event to be lived" rather than a thing to be consumed. It's also absence, the gaps between intention and reception, and contingency, "meaning is always in the making": "a collaboration with chance, a dance with the unknown," with the audience as active participants and cocreators (fans transmitting energy to a team).

**The move.** "We tend to think of the creation of something as its end... The model of performance reminds us that things continue to develop when they interact with the world." Robert Lepage: "The writing starts when you perform."

**Cases.**
- *Performance for social change (John Paul II).* In 1979 Karol Wojtyla, a former actor, was elected Pope John Paul II, the first non-Italian pope in 455 years and the first Polish one, amid a Cold War stalemate. Soviet communism was atheist, and religion disperses power; it was never eliminated, especially in Poland, where John Lewis Gaddis saw ties between the individual morality of Poles and the universal morality of the Church. His first foreign trip was to Poland, the first papal visit to a communist country: nine days, with settings chosen deliberately, in which "a single individual, through a series of dramatic performances, was changing the course of history" (Gaddis). He kissed the ground; he said the opening mass in Warsaw's Victory Square, a symbol of withstanding World War II's totalitarianism and now the Cold War's; he visited shrines and monuments in "forty public, highly theatrical appearances in nine days" (John Cornwell). More than a third of Poles saw him live, and his last mass, on the Krakow Commons, drew two to three million, the largest outdoor gathering to date. In 1983 he was more explicit about freedom ("The sovereignty of the state is deeply linked to its ability to promote freedom of the nation") and adjusted his words to each setting: in Poznan, where communists had suppressed workers three decades earlier, he first spoke the forbidden word *Solidarity*. "Setting influences performance." He met the imprisoned Lech Walesa, sharing the stage, which showed, as a priest put it, "the movement was alive and that the story was by no means over." In 1987 more than a million gathered near Gdansk, site of the 1980 shipyard strike that launched Solidarity, to hear him on workers' right to make decisions. Language, symbols and allusions carried common cultural meanings and settings reinforced his points: the same message (religious freedom), never delivered identically. At Jasna Góra, Cornwell judged, he turned a brooding insurgency into a peaceful but determined transformation of consciousness; for Gaddis, the kiss of the ground on June 2, 1979 began the process that ended communism in Poland and Europe. It shows the power of harnessing the dynamic interaction of actor, space and audience.
- *Performance as tool (Willie Sutton).* Sutton studied audiences and settings and decided what part to play, and each performance robbed a bank. Between the 1920s and 1950s, between prison terms and escapes, he robbed more than one hundred banks, mostly in New York, stealing millions without killing anyone, blowing a safe or taking hostages. "I studied a bank carefully before I robbed it... I rehearsed my men thoroughly in their parts." He played whatever role the audience expected: Western Union messenger, postman, policeman, window cleaner with a squeegee, once the bank manager. He learned makeup from dating an actress and rented uniforms ("the right uniform was an open sesame that would unlock any door"), since guards saw the uniform, not the face, and he committed fully (as a policeman he lectured people on obeying the law; he "*became* a dedicated member of the US Postal Service"). "I was beginning to think of this as a drama, with myself as director and main actor." He adjusted and improvised (appealing to a manager's responsibility for his staff), "considered casing a bank as important as actually robbing it," was in and out in minutes and was never caught during a robbery; police said "Sutton's equal never lived." Great performances look effortless but rest on great effort: understand the limits and opportunities of the space, sense who the audience is and what they want, and ask how the actors can use the space to get them to buy into the illusion. Not everything can be controlled, so preparation is what lets improvisation maintain the illusion. Sutton, at fifty-one: "I've spent most of my adult life in prison or in hiding, and I haven't a penny." He studied law in prison, argued his way out on time served, and "Willie the Actor" died in 1980.
