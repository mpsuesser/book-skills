# Physics and chemistry

Models from the physical sciences. In the authors' framing, physics models work mostly as metaphors (inertia explains why erroneous beliefs persist), and chemistry models as accounts of any process of creation and change. Each entry gives the model, its moves, its limits and its cases.

## Relativity: "Put it into perspective."

**The model.** There is more than one way to see everything. That does not make all perspectives equally valid; it means we may not have the most complete view. Steven Strogatz called relativity "founded on empathy" in a rigorous sense: imagine how things look to someone moving differently.

- *Galileo* (1630s): below deck on a ship moving steadily, a dropped ball falls straight down; to a fish or someone on the beach, it also moves sideways. Observers moving at constant speed and direction get the same results from mechanical experiments. Earth itself moves around the Sun at about 67,000 miles per hour, and we don't feel it. The move: "Now what does a fish see?" Imagine the scientist, the fish and the bird, and their accounts differ. Imagine the situation "through the eyes of all the participants": it reveals blind spots and builds empathy.
- *Einstein*: the speed of light is fixed for any observer moving at constant velocity, so there is no fixed frame of reference, and observers in relative motion experience time differently. Bob on the ground sees two lightning strikes at once; Alice at the middle of a passing train sees the front strike first. Both are right. "Your perspective informs your experience." There is an objective reality, but no one perceives it whole without work.

**Perspective-taking** is the ability to perceive a situation from another point of view, physical (what they can see) or conceptual (what they feel and believe). It develops in childhood, and we are often lazy about developing it further.

**Subjectivity of perspective.** Eyewitness accounts are shaped by physical factors (vision, light, how long they looked), psychological ones (mood, hurry, an argument that morning, an incentive to take a side) and biases (about ethnicity, a predisposition to help police). Memory is not a recording: it suffers misattribution and suggestibility (leading questions, emotive words), is distorted by what we know now, and gets rewritten to fit our beliefs, while we stay committed to our original perception. In *Rashomon*, the bandit, the wife, the dead samurai (through a medium) and a woodcutter give different accounts of a murder, and the film never supplies the truth.

**Moves.**
- "When you see someone doing something that doesn't make sense to you, ask yourself what the world would have to look like to you for those actions to make sense."
- **Consider the frame.** How others frame things is their perspective. Given an opinion, report, article or plan, ask: "Who is involved in this information, and what do you know about their vantage point?" Editors and peer review combine outside views into a better product.
- **Shift distance or time** when stuck: zoom in for detail or out for fewer details (we advise friends well because we're not caught in their irrelevant details), or extend the timeline weeks, months or years ahead.
- **Travel far outside your normal experience.** Einstein's train metaphor: we forget we're on our own train. Rifa'a Rafi' al-Tahtawi spent five years in Paris in the late 1820s, wrote a report-like account (*An Imam in Paris*), saw his own culture anew, and carried what he learned back to Egypt.
- Ask others what they see that you can't, apply judgment, and update.

**Limit.** "Relativity is not the same as relativism—the idea that all perspectives are equally valid." Asimov: thinking a flat Earth and a spherical Earth equally wrong is "wronger than both of them put together."

**Case: the Minh Duc Hong trial.** On July 4, 2000, in Ocean Shores, Washington, Chris Kinison, who had waved a Confederate flag, shouted slurs and assaulted Hong's brother, was killed in a fight with Hong. About a dozen witnesses gave conflicting accounts; when their positions were mapped, some had described things they could not physically have seen. Local-versus-outsider and racial biases, drinking and fear all colored testimony. The trial ended in a mistrial, deadlocked 11 to 1 for acquittal. "What you see is never all there is."

## Reciprocity: "Give and take."

**The model.** Newton's third law: "For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction." When you act on things, they act on you; the harder you push others, the harder they push back. Forces come in pairs. Jet propulsion (and the octopus and squid) moves forward only if the push is stronger than friction and weight. A tackler feels the tackle too, so use the least force needed: "the more force you apply to others, the more damage you do to yourself." The model explains win-win deals, "going positive and going first," why firms forbid gifts, and why giving is as valuable as receiving. The authors' rewrite of the Golden Rule: "Do unto others knowing that something will be done unto you." Confucius named reciprocity as the one word to live by: "What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others."

**Two types.** *Direct* ("I help you and you help me") and *indirect* (paying it forward, or building reputation). In the physical world reciprocity works every time (punch a wall); in the biological world, more often than not. We evolved to help those who helped us. *Quid pro quo* ("something for something"); the Latin *do ut des*, "I give, so that you may give."

**What we give.** The link between good deeds and a good life is imperfect but documented: volunteering predicts better mental and physical health, life satisfaction, self-esteem and happiness, and lower depression, distress and mortality. "When we give, we get." Norman Bethune, a Canadian surgeon, ran a free clinic in 1930s Montreal, created the first mobile blood-transfusion unit in Spain (1936) and led Communist medical forces in China (1938), dying at 49 of septicemia from a cut finger. Mao eulogized him; China still teaches his story in primary schools, while Cold War Canada wrote him out.

**Tit for tat** (sidebar). In iterated games of cooperation and defection, the strongest strategy (codified by Anatol Rapoport) begins by cooperating, then copies the other side's last move. With people you can't fully trust, reciprocate their choices. *Tit for tat with forgiveness*, occasionally cooperating after a defection, beats the straight version because it escapes cycles of mutual defection. Peter Kaufman: "go positive and go first." People make mistakes; absent malice, forgive.

**Loss aversion** (sidebar). Daniel Kahneman: "losses loom larger than gains"; people will risk losing $100 only for every $250 of potential gain. That makes approaching people who might reject us feel risky. The trick is to start "looking at outcomes in the aggregate instead of focusing on each unique situation." Smile at strangers for a week and list the outcomes: we remember the scowls and forget the smiles, and the occasional loss is more than repaid.

**Schadenfreude** (sidebar, "damage-joy," Tiffany Watt Smith). It is strongest when the misfortune seems earned, and is tied to aggression (toward an out-group), rivalry ("Status is always relative") and justice (a comeuppance).

**Case: the Eternal Treaty** (about 1250 BCE). Fifteen years after their great showdown, Egypt's Ramesses and the Hittite Hattusili signed the first known peace treaty, with mutual military support. Ramesses wanted monuments and trade and had the Libyans to worry about; Hattusili had usurped the throne from his nephew, needed legitimacy and faced Assyria, which then did not invade. "Reciprocity based on self-interest is still reciprocity." "The more people you help, the more people you will have willing to help you."

**The move.** Give cynicism and curtness and you get them back; give opportunity and the benefit of the doubt and, more often than not, you get those. "Become what you want to see in the world, and the world will give it back to you." The biggest misperception is waiting for others to go first, rather than "going positive and going first without expectation." "The best way to achieve success is to deserve success."

## Thermodynamics: "Reduce chaos and find order."

**The model.** "All work requires energy, and all systems are headed toward equilibrium."
- **First law:** energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or changed (conservation of energy). It is exchanged as *heat* (thermal interaction) or *work* (any other process). Work can be completely converted to heat; heat cannot be completely converted to work.
- **Second law:** the *entropy* (disorder; energy no longer able to do work) of an isolated system always increases, toward maximum entropy: thermal equilibrium, with no net heat flow. "We need to expend energy to create order. Without the deployment of energy, all things move away from order."
- **Third law:** as temperature approaches absolute zero, entropy approaches a constant.
- **Zeroth law:** two objects each in thermal equilibrium with a third are in equilibrium with each other.

Applied: we drift into equilibrium with the people around us, so choose them wisely; order costs energy to keep, so expect things to fall apart and focus on prevention. Peter Atkins: "A cold sink is essential" (a power plant's cooling towers matter more than its turbines). Geerat Vermeij: an economy's energy state (its temperature) sets what its members can do and how fast, linking energy and time, the two components of power.

**Putting up walls.** Expose two systems of different temperature to each other and they reach the same temperature. To encourage equilibrium, maximize exposure; to slow it, insulate (a thermos slows cooling but cannot stop it). Helen Czerski: the physical world "only ever has one destination: equilibrium." Social versions of the three modes of transfer: *radiation* (radio and TV carry ideas across borders), *convection* (teacher and student exchanges), *conduction* (brands and foreign aid carry values). Walls rarely work, because "contrast is hard to maintain," and borders are places of exchange (trade, marriage). Ken Wilber: a boundary line is also a potential battle line.
- *Hadrian's Wall* was too long to stop an army; it controlled movement, slowed aggression and anchored diplomacy. Its success rested "less on the fortifications and barriers than on the soldiers who manned them" (Adrian Goldsworthy), true of all walls. It slowed the cooling of "hot" Roman culture, and the Romans eventually judged better insulation too costly.
- *The Great Wall* was many walls over 2,000 years: expensive to maintain and supply, built with forced labor, guarded by bribable soldiers, and circumvented because walls end somewhere. The Ming attitude was "define, enclose, and exclude." Invaders repeatedly adopted Chinese ways and became Chinese; King Wuling of Zhao (307 BC) adopted the nomads' tunics and mounted archers.
- *The Berlin Wall* aimed to stop ideas as well as people (some 2.5 million, about 15% of East Germans, mostly young and qualified, fled 1945 to 1961). It never fully worked, fed the ideology it was meant to keep out, and fell in November 1989. Holding a temperature difference takes constant energy.

**The problem of equilibrium** (sidebar). Nietzsche observed that a party needs its opposition to stay strong. True equilibrium means no change, growth or movement. "It is contrast that drives development."

**We don't like disorder.** There are more ways for things to be mixed than sorted (Murray Gell-Mann's coins; peanut butter and jelly), so a closed system with order moves toward disorder. Life keeps its structure by consuming outside energy and raising the entropy around it. In the game of broken telephone, "Today is Wednesday" becomes "I like scary movies": there are more ways to change than to stay the same. Structures (laws, religions, social norms, customs, stories) nudge disorder into order. Fairy tales follow equilibrium, disequilibrium, new equilibrium; John Yorke: "Every act of perception is an attempt to impose order." The archetype runs: home threatened, a flawed protagonist, a journey for the cure, a literal or metaphorical death, rebirth, home saved (the hero's journey). Their predictability is itself order; "the forest is where you are when your surroundings are not mastered." Cinderella recurs as "Yeh-Shen" in China, "Cendrillon" in France, "The Turkey Girl" among the Zuni.

"Entropy is the universe's tax on time." The struggle between order and disorder is the source of change and progress, in pockets of temporary order: a clean room, a planet, a civilization.

## Inertia: "Change requires force."

**The model.** Inertia is an object's resistance to a change in its state of motion. Newton's first law: an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps the same speed and direction, "unless acted upon by an unbalanced force." "Left to themselves, systems resist change. So too do people." Starting is hard, and so is stopping. Keeping things as they are takes almost no effort and little uncertainty, which is part of why we reject the new. Starting costs more than continuing, and multitasking is a myth (each switch costs energy and time). Hence car-reliant cities, people staying in jobs they hate, avoiding views unlike theirs, keeping their parents' religion. Einstein: "Nothing happens until something moves."

**Momentum** (sidebar): p = mv. A train must brake well in advance; double the speed or the mass and you double the momentum. Outside physics, momentum is progress in a direction that would take a weighty outside force to stop. **Escape velocity** (sidebar): about 7 miles per second at Earth's surface; once reached, no further propulsion is needed. It links to activation energy: "How much effort do we need not only to overcome resistance, but to set an object on a new path?"

**Mass as duration.** For habits, "you can often think of mass as duration": the effort to change a habit, product or idea grows with how long it has been around. "Once we consider a story true, it takes a great deal of force to change it. Data alone is rarely enough." Urban legends persist.

**Case: lead vs absinthe.** Vitruvius warned some 2,000 years ago that water should not run in lead pipes. Lead stayed in makeup, paint, printing, wine, face cream and gasoline; Alice Hamilton, a leading expert in industrial toxicology after her 1910 Illinois survey of industrial illness, campaigned against leaded fuel from the 1920s, yet it was not banned in the US until the 1980s, and leaded paint is still used. Absinthe, popular from the 1860s, was banned in Switzerland three years after the 1905 "absinthe murder" and in France in 1914, though later tests found it no worse than other alcohol of the same strength. "Mass matters": lead was woven into manufacturing (with incentives against the costs of containment and retooling), while absinthe stood alone.

**Inertia of belief.** Beliefs become habits; Rafe Sagarin noted belief systems carry "enormous evolutionary inertia." Inertia blinds us to opportunities and new information (the history of invention is a history of dismissals: radio, telephone, cars, airplanes, laptops). But it also spares us relearning everything, and values with strong inertia help us persevere. "Strong beliefs can stay strong while being flexible": refine them continually. Lise Meitner held her beliefs that way. The first woman admitted to physics lectures at the University of Vienna, she started in Berlin as Max Planck's unpaid assistant, codiscovered protactinium with Otto Hahn (who always took first authorship), became Prussia's first woman professor in 1919, fled to Sweden in 1938, and with her nephew Otto Frisch explained nuclear fission. Hahn alone won the 1944 Nobel; Meitner was nominated 29 times and never won. She worked until 81.

**Moves.**
- "The path of least resistance is always the status quo." Inertia is "the guardian of the status quo."
- **Small steps** reduce the force needed (one push-up, not fifty; floss one tooth). "The bigger the mass—in this case the gap between where you are and where you want to be—the more effort required."
- **Make inertia work for you.** Pick the right direction and build momentum: the *flywheel*, where success breeds success and small wins compound. "It takes a sustained force in the right direction to build momentum in a new direction."
- Successful companies fight the inertia of their own success (size, complexity, entrenched interests); startups exploit their lack of it.

## Friction and Viscosity: "Movement is a battle."

**The model.** *Friction* is the force you must overcome to achieve an outcome. You can't eliminate it, but you can minimize it, and shaping the environment to reduce opposing forces is key to productivity. Physically, friction opposes the motion of surfaces in contact and produces heat and sound. "There are no frictionless surfaces, only surfaces with less or more resistance." *Kinetic* friction acts on sliding things (a pushed book stops); *static* friction keeps still things from starting. *Viscosity* is a "measure of how hard it is for one layer of fluid to slide over another layer." For small particles, viscosity dominates gravity and inertia (plankton stop the instant they stop swimming); a whale barely notices it and coasts on inertia.

Two lessons: (1) what is easy in one environment can be hard in another (peace and war); (2) which forces matter depends on the scale you operate at.

**Surface tension** (sidebar) matters where two fluids touch. Agnes Pockels, barred from German universities in the late nineteenth century, studied from her brother's textbooks and built a trough to measure surface tension with oils and soaps; her work became the base for Nobel-winning research by others. She succeeded in a high-viscosity environment.

**Slowing the flow: Chernobyl.** Information flow depends less on content than on the medium it moves through. The 1980s Soviet Union was a high-viscosity environment for communication (a goldfish swimming through honey): censorship, criminalized sharing, domestic spying, no power on the ground. Earlier accidents at Leningrad were illegal to discuss, so their lessons never reached Chernobyl's design. When the 1986 turbine test went wrong, intercity phones were cut, workers were forbidden to tell relatives, and concern for image sent thousands into a May Day parade in Kiev. Leaders bullied subordinates and demanded unrealistic quotas, so problems were pushed up the chain and corners cut. The medium had "high viscosity for true information, whereas false information faced a low-viscosity medium," so falsehood spread further. Pripyat's residents held weddings while they waited; evacuation took days. It backfired: the sense of betrayal fueled Ukrainian independence. "If people get an inkling something is hidden from them, they'll push as hard as possible to find it."

**Trickle-up innovation: Toyota.** The forces acting on an executive differ from those on a frontline worker; to get frontline innovation, attend to what eases or blocks movement in *their* environment. Ford's mass production treated workers as "interchangeable parts" and left problems to specialists at the end of the line, the costliest place to fix them. At Toyota in the 1940s, Taiichi Ohno targeted frontline friction instead of raising quotas: workers took over minor repairs and quality checks, an *Andon cord* above every station let any worker stop the line while the team swarmed the problem, and time was set aside for improvement ideas. The line almost never stopped, "the same problem never occurs twice," and quality, efficiency and morale rose. *Lean* production hands the most tasks to the workers who add value and traces every defect to its ultimate cause. Executive vision matters less at the working level than an environment that doesn't feel like pushing a cement wall. "If you want to change a situation, you must appreciate the forces that are strongest in that particular environment."

**Moves.**
- "To overcome resistance, we often default to using more force when simply reducing the friction or viscosity will do. However, doing both is more effective than either in isolation." "Reducing resistance is often easier than adding force."
- Friction is double-edged: the enemy of efficiency, and the source of traction. In relationships it is conflict from differing goals and beliefs, which wears but also lets you connect and influence. Viscosity costs pumping energy, and makes paint and lubricants work.
- Friction is a weapon: competitors can be slowed by adding resistance (regulation, bureaucracy).

## Velocity: "Direction over speed."

**The model.** Speed is distance over time; *velocity* is displacement over time, so it has a direction. "Velocity moves you toward a goal. Speed does not." Running in circles is fast and goes nowhere. "Progress matters more than activity": measure how far you've moved from where you started. Constant velocity in the right direction is usually most effective; too many direction changes and you're back in circles. Seneca: if a man does not know what port he is steering for, no wind is favorable.

**Kinetic and potential energy** (sidebar). Kinetic energy is the energy of motion, a function of velocity and relative to other objects; potential energy is stored by position (gravitational or elastic), independent of others' movement.

**Case: Napoleon in Italy and Russia.** Napoleon held that an army's strength, like mechanical power, is mass times velocity. Given his first command at 26, with an army neither strongest nor best trained, he kept moving so opponents could not regroup or build obstacles (one division covered about 50 miles in 36 hours; three battles in four days across 56 miles). What enabled it: deep prior study of Italy's history and geography, willingness to try others' ideas, mastery of the territory that gave him flexibility, routes chosen for constant velocity, troops who were "his men" (he gave them victory and spoke to them as equals, making velocity a group goal), and clear, simple instructions (decoding complex orders slows movement). He controlled what he could (fewer camp followers, no tents, little baggage) and accepted what he couldn't (weather). But impatience at Cosseria cost 600 to 1,000 casualties. And in Russia in 1812, over some 1,550 miles: "because direction is paramount, size sometimes compromises our velocity. The bigger we are, the harder it is to adjust our direction." He had given up so much for speed that he had nothing left to adjust with, and reached Moscow with about 90,000 of at least 400,000 men. His goal had been to force Russia to accept French superiority, not to reach Moscow; much speed, little real territory.

**Moves.**
- Define the full destination. Debt-free by forty, yes, but also with relationships, health and no crimes: "Figuring out how to improve your velocity must take into account the full scope of what you want the arrival at your destination to look like. Better to go in the right direction slowly than in the wrong direction with speed."
- Set a direction, improve tactics, adjust to new information. "Sometimes progress comes from more force and sometimes progress comes from removing friction." Work harder, and eliminate whatever doesn't serve the goal.

**Case: Mae West** moved from performer to writer, producer and star of her own scripts, turning down any role that was a step backward and writing whenever she wasn't onstage. Working outside the studio system, by 1935 she earned more than any other woman in the world. A car speeding in circles goes nowhere; a slow walk in a straight line crosses continents. "Velocity is the great differentiator."

## Leverage: "Enough to move the world."

**The model.** "Achieving results significantly greater than the force you put in." Written language leverages past learning; standardization gives firms leverage over workers (they're easier to replace); once you have judgment, leverage it to reduce effort. Hard work alone is not the path to wealth. Three classes of lever, by the order of parts: force, fulcrum, weight (crowbar); fulcrum, weight, force (wheelbarrow); fulcrum, force, weight (baseball bat: more energy in, but the weight moves farther). Archimedes: a long enough lever could lift the earth. The weight moved and the weight moving are in inverse ratio to their distances from the fulcrum. When everyone had only sticks, productivity barely varied; tools leverage small individual differences, so "if technology leverages variation in individual performance, then we can expect the gap between the most productive and least productive people in a society to increase over time."

**Leverage between people.** The levers are items or ideas with shared value. "Leverage itself is amoral"; it is about influence, not manipulation. In negotiation, apply pressure in the right place; "the best way to have leverage in any deal is to not need the deal at all." Too much pressure breaks the lever or ends the win-win; too little misses the goal. Roger Volkema's two principles: leverage rests on *perceptions*, and it is a *social or relational construct* (everyone must perceive roughly the same value, and it vanishes if the other party walks away). It isn't binary: the same leverage can get you X, or X, Y and Z.

**Three questions.** "In a situation of true leverage, the lever, not the application of force, does most of the work."
1. How do I know when I have it?
2. Where and when should I apply it?
3. How do I keep it?
Conquerors extract ongoing leverage (land, reparations, marriages) and often sow the seeds of their own destruction.

**Case: Eleanor of Aquitaine.** Aquitaine was rich, and its custom let women inherit and keep property in marriage: it was her lever. She exited her marriage to Louis VII of France, who could not control the duchy's dozen counties, and married Henry II of England, who acted without consulting her kin. "Ownership and control are different things." She made her son Richard Duke of Aquitaine, backed him against his father, lived in the duchy, cultivated its nobles, church and people, and in her seventies toured it issuing charters to win subjects for John. "Land can only be used as leverage if the people living in it are supportive of your leadership. Leading requires followers." Lessons: (1) don't underestimate your leverage; (2) keep others wanting what you have, since all parties must perceive its value; (3) know when you can use it and when you can't (through fifteen years as Henry's prisoner she never squandered Aquitaine trying to escape).

**The dark side.** Having leverage doesn't oblige you to use it, and leverage used to the maximum all the time, or entrenched systematically, becomes tyranny. In West Virginia coal company towns, miners owned no houses, could be evicted without notice, were paid in "coal scrip" spendable only at the company store (whose monopoly prices canceled wage gains), and lived under company control of mail, ballots, courts and juries; a 1913 congressman said the company owned both parties. The counter was collective action (strikes, unions) aggregating small powers into counter-leverage, or individual exits (moving away, cash from gardens or homemade alcohol). "The leverage you have may not always be the leverage you want, but chances are, if you look, you will find you have some somewhere."

**Moves and limits.** Leverage is "the force multiplier of the world," behind nonlinear outcomes (an author's millions of copies, a CEO directing thousands). Personally, find the key habits, skills and relationships, "the critical few rather than the trivial many." Leverage cuts both ways (borrowing too much against a house); "good ideas taken too far often cause unanticipated consequences," and exploited people never give loyalty or their best work. Weigh second- and third-order effects. "Leverage is a tool, not a toy."

## Activation Energy: "Get to the end."

**The model.** In chemistry, the minimum energy needed to start a reaction: molecules must collide hard enough to break bonds so new ones form (heat raises velocity and so collision frequency). Rub a match gently and nothing happens; strike it and you cross the threshold. The greater the activation energy, the slower the reaction. Past the *transition state* (high-energy, unstable), molecules proceed with no further input. A high threshold is also protection: propane doesn't burn at room temperature, and hydrogen and oxygen in the air don't become water. Svante Arrhenius's equation (sidebar) relates temperature to reaction rate, which is why cool food lasts longer. Paul Graham allowed that it's fine to fool yourself a little to find the activation energy for a new project.

"We know the amount of activation energy is correct when enough new connections form that it becomes impossible to revert to the way we were."

**Moves.**
- **Plan for the whole fire.** One sheet of newspaper burns about ten seconds and leaves the logs barely charred. You need enough paper and kindling, a match in several places, and ongoing adjustment of logs and air. "You need to plan for not only the initial flame, but all the energy required to get and sustain the fire you want."
- **Finish the reaction.** New bonds must form or the old ones return; the energy must carry re-formation so far that reverting is hard or impossible. "Successful reactions demand a new way of doing things." "The buildup to doing something is part of the activation energy required—this buildup includes everything on the camel, not just the straw." Breaking a habit or addiction usually takes more energy than you think, then gets easier to maintain; identify the triggers in your internal and external environment and change them, or the old bonds re-form. "Change that doesn't last is easy." People who fail repeatedly rarely ask whether enough effort went into forming the new structure, and conclude they're doomed.
- "Everything you need to do, right to the end of your goals, is the real activation energy required to finish the reaction." It is "a necessary upfront cost, not a permanent obstacle. Once things are moving, momentum takes over."

**Cases.**
- *Revolutions* often fail because fighting and governing take different skills: they spend everything breaking the old structure and don't plan to form the new one (keeping popular support, cementing goals). Thomas Sankara of Burkina Faso waited out earlier coups until he was "strong enough to decisively influence events," then as president (1983 to 1987) remade the state: outlawed female genital mutilation and forced marriage, appointed women to major cabinet posts, ran literacy and mass-vaccination campaigns, refused foreign aid ("he who feeds you, controls you"), and more than doubled wheat production in three years. But he acted as if the new system were mature and misjudged how much of the activation energy had to come from the people themselves; he repressed opposition and dismissed striking teachers, "unwilling to give them enough time to ripen in his people." He was assassinated in 1987 and the old order returned.
- *Development economics* (Joe Studwell, *How Asia Works*). Japan, South Korea and Taiwan sustained growth; Indonesia, the Philippines and Thailand slid back. The question: "At what point have you gone far enough that you can't go back?" The three critical interventions: maximize agricultural output through land reform (equal division of land, plus rural credit, marketing and agronomic training); steer investment to manufacturing, rewarding export success and culling failures; and align finance with both, accepting low near-term returns. Where land wasn't reformed, landlords rented rather than invested; firms sold to easy domestic markets; banks lent to consumers ("the best banking returns in the East Asian region are produced in the region's most backward countries"). Land reform and protection were short-term parts of the activation energy, appropriate to a stage of development.

## Catalysts: "Change agent."

**The model.** "Catalysts accelerate change." A catalyst cannot make a reaction happen that wouldn't otherwise, but it can greatly reduce the time it takes, by creating alternative pathways so more particles have enough energy (faster, safer, cheaper: bread, yogurt, detergent, the catalytic converter). It is not consumed and can be reused. No single catalyst speeds every reaction, since activation energies differ, so finding the right one is critical. Catalysts are value-neutral: they speed bad reactions too. Success can catalyze failure (lost focus, complacency); failure can catalyze success. Elizabeth Fulhame showed in the eighteenth century that water enables oxidation and is regenerated, and her peers rejected her work; Berzelius coined "catalysis"; Wilhelm Ostwald thought every reaction could be improved by one. Lars Öhrström likened catalysis to a tunnel through the Alps in place of the mountain passes.

**Autocatalysis** (sidebar). The reaction's outputs are the catalysts it needs, so it sustains and accelerates itself, for a good window if not forever. It can be positive or negative (mood-altering chemicals; a good night's sleep). "Producing an autocatalytic reaction is the gold standard."

**Cases.**
- *The printing press, "the first internet."* Before it, learning took huge activation energy: scarce handwritten books, scholars wandering between scriptoria, copying errors that made older manuscripts likelier to be accurate. The press lowered the energy and opened more pathways to learning.
- *The Black Death* (1340s, along trade routes) catalyzed change nobody wanted: labor systems collapsed, wages rose and rents fell, social mobility and labor-saving technology spread, the Church's grip loosened toward the Renaissance, women entered trades such as brewing and married later, and medicine moved from humoral theory toward observation. Peter Frankopan: far from marking the death of Europe, it was its making. Everyday catalysts: getting winded on the stairs, a milestone birthday, a health scare, getting fired or rejected.
- *Louis XV and comfort.* Endorsement by the rich, famous or respected catalyzes norms. Louis XV turned Versailles from public display toward comfort and privacy (sofas, armchairs, intimates seated in his presence, indoor plumbing, small rooms with specific functions), ideas he took from the Marquise de Montespan and the Marquise de Pompadour. His adoption made comfort desirable and cheaper, a feedback loop. Technology, a visionary leader, a book or a mentor can catalyze; "we can be catalysts ourselves—helping others find the activation energy they need to thrive."

## Alloying: "Greater than the sum of its parts."

**The model.** Combining components in specific proportions to produce something none can achieve alone. An alloy is a mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a nonmetal, with properties of its own (strength, corrosion resistance, service life). Bronze, about 90% copper and 10% tin, was harder than either and let the Sumerians rule their neighbors. Steel is iron and carbon; add nickel or chromium and it resists wear and corrosion. "With a successful alloy, one plus one can truly equal ten." Tom Freston: innovation is putting two existing things together in a new way.

**Limits.** Not every combination is better. Early steelmakers worked for some 4,000 years without knowing what they were doing, and because ores vary (phosphorus, silicon), a recipe that worked with one mine's ore failed with another's. The key is which elements, in what proportions: too little has no effect, too much makes the result brittle or unstable. Find the sweet spot.

**Moves.**
- In a person: a relentless work ethic, extreme reliability and a willingness to learn together beat any one alone; deep engineering skill, clear explanation, empathy, humility, resilience and drive in one person are incredibly rare.
- With one strong skill and missing others, partner and form an alloy. A team with only good ideas is weaker than one that also has someone who sees which ideas are worth pursuing and someone who can realize them. Consider the raw materials and how they mix. In medicine: chemotherapy plus a drug that reduces side effects; drugs on different pathways prevent resistance.
- **Disproportional wear and tear** (sidebar). What suits one part of a system may not suit all of it: coat the high-wear part in a strong alloy so production doesn't stop for replacements. Leonardo's anti-friction alloy (three parts copper to seven of tin) anticipated Isaac Babbitt's 1839 alloy by about three centuries.

**Knowledge, the ultimate alloy.** Direct experience and theory (books) are each useless alone: theory can't prepare you for the nuances of your own partner or team, and experience alone condemns you to repeat others' mistakes. Experience triggers updates to theory; applying theory produces new experience. Aristotle named five components of knowledge: *episteme* (scientific knowledge), *techne* (craft), *phronesis* (practical wisdom), *nous* (intuitive apprehension) and *sophia* (wisdom). Leonardo da Vinci practiced the alloy: intense observation (birds, eddies, plant stems, optics), guessing the principle and testing it by further observation, challenging received wisdom, learning from others (he noted whom to ask about arithmetic, about walking on ice in Flanders), and mixing disciplines, in a Florence where silk makers mixed with goldbeaters and artists with architects. Knowledge is an "alloy of experience and theory that can be further strengthened with elements of curiosity, imagination, and sharing."

**Case: Tecumseh and Brock.** In the War of 1812 the Shawnee leader Tecumseh, bringing warriors and knowledge of the land, joined the British Major General Isaac Brock. Together they convinced the American commander at Fort Detroit that thousands more Native warriors were coming, played on his fears, and took the fort without a fight, "marrying the speed and flexibility of the native force to the firepower and solidity of the British regulars": "a victory that should not have been won."
