# The Great Mental Models

The series' thesis: "In life and business, the person with the fewest blind spots wins." You reduce blind spots by looking at a problem through a *latticework of mental models*, a toolkit of reliable principles drawn from many disciplines, so that you can walk around a problem "in a three-dimensional way," see the world as it is rather than as you think it should be, and work with its principles instead of against them. Avoiding problems beats solving them (Peter Bevelin: "I want to avoid problems—prevent them from happening and doing it right from the beginning"). Use this skill to handle a situation the way the authors would: pick the models whose indicators the situation shows, look through each, connect what they reveal, act, and reflect.

## The vocabulary

Use these as leading words, in the authors' sense.

- ***Mental model***: "A mental model is simply a representation of how something works." Models chunk complexity into understandable, organizable pieces; they are largely subconscious, and they are how we infer causality, match patterns and draw analogies. The series collects the ones with the greatest utility, "the all-star team of mental models": reliable principles seen at work again and again, which apply far outside their home discipline.
- ***Latticework of mental models***: Charlie Munger's term for knowledge from different disciplines, simplified and connected so the points reinforce each other. "If the facts don't hang together on a latticework of theory, you don't have them in a usable form." "80 or 90 important models will carry about 90 percent of the freight in making you a worldly-wise person," and of those, a handful carry very heavy freight.
- ***Lens***: each model is a way of looking. "The more lenses used on a given problem, the more of reality reveals itself." A simple, well-defined problem needs few lenses; not every lens applies to every problem, and more lenses are not always an advantage, but most problems are multidimensional. The blind men each touch one part of the elephant (snake, fan, tree, wall, rope, spear); a botanist, an environmentalist, a forestry engineer and a businessperson each see a different forest. Alain de Botton: "The chief enemy of good decisions is a lack of sufficient perspectives on a problem."
- ***The man with a hammer***: "To the man with only a hammer, everything starts looking like a nail." We overuse the models we have: the engineer sees systems, the psychologist incentives, the businessperson opportunity cost and risk-reward. Use the toolbox: a hammer for nails, a wrench for bolts.
- ***Interdisciplinary travelers***: Herbert Simon's term for people who "carry new knowledge from one enclave to another." The latticework makes you one.
- ***The Three Buckets of Knowledge***: Peter Kaufman's sorting of where the most reliable principles come from, by sample size: inorganic systems, 13.7 billion years (mathematics and physics); organic systems, 3.5 billion years (biology); and human history, about 20,000 years of recorded behavior. Look for sample sizes across time, not just space; we assume the way the world is, is the way it always was.
- ***Metaphor or literal***: some models apply literally to life (systems; biology, since people and organizations are living systems), others work as metaphors (physics: inertia explains why erroneous beliefs persist; much of mathematics). Both are useful.
- ***Value-neutral***: a model can serve good or evil, and work in one situation but not another. Ask of each: "What can this model show me about what not to do? Where will it help me find a better way forward?"
- ***"All models are wrong, but some are useful."*** Every model is a simplification (see the map is not the territory), so collect models, experiment with them and learn what each is good for.
- ***Knowledge of indicators***: the trained recognition of the features in a situation that should trigger the most appropriate model. It is what practice builds.
- ***Signposts, not proof***: the historical cases illustrate a model as a lens; they do not demonstrate causation. Use them as signposts to similar situations in your own life.
- ***Supporting Idea***: the series' shorter companion concepts (falsifiability, Dunbar's number, game theory, externalities, subtext and others), on the same footing as the models.

## Why understanding fails

- **Two troubles with any model**: the model of reality is wrong (it doesn't survive real-world experience), or the model is right but applied where it doesn't belong. Test models over the largest sample size you can, and discard or update the flawed ones, remembering that a bad model (bloodletting) can survive inside a package of other models, making it hard to see where it fails.
- **Three flaws that block updating**:
  1. *Perspective*: it's hard to see a system you're inside. On Galileo's ship, below decks, a dropped ball falls straight down; a fish outside sees it move about twenty feet horizontally.
  2. *Ego*: fear of criticism keeps ideas from being tested, and criticism, when it comes, gets defended against instead of used. "Wrapping ego up in outcomes instead of in ourselves makes it easier to update our views." We optimize for short-term ego protection over long-term happiness.
  3. *Distance*: the farther you are from the results of your decisions, the easier it is to keep your current views. A hot stove gives instant feedback; organizations past a certain size remove people from consequences, and it's easier to fool yourself at the macro level than the micro.
- **Undervaluing the elementary**: we overvalue the complicated and dismiss the simple (specialists especially). Darwin noticed things "which easily escape attention" and asked why; geniuses prosper "by exploiting unrecognized simplicities" (Andy Benoit).
- **Understanding is not enough**: you must change behavior. Know that you interrupt people and keep doing it, and people tell the simplest story: you don't care. Passivity means no reflection, and no reflection means no learning.
- **Antaeus**: the giant drew his strength from contact with the earth, and Heracles beat him by lifting him off the ground. Understanding must stay in contact with reality, continuously tested and updated; the only way to know how far your understanding reaches is to put your ideas into action.

## Applying the latticework to a situation

1. **Break the problem into its substantive parts**, bottom-up, so the interconnections reveal themselves. Ask the series' framing question: "How can I work with the world, not against it?" (The *Fram* was built to rise out of the pack ice that crushed other ships, and drifted across the Arctic closer to the pole than any ship before.) Done when you can name the parts, the people involved and the outcome wanted.
2. **Check your ground.** Name the map you are using (the report, metric, plan, story or model) and how it might differ from the territory, and say whether the problem sits inside your circle of competence, what you know versus what you only think you know. Done when you can say which parts you understand, which you are taking on someone else's map, and who could fill the gap.
3. **Choose lenses by their indicators.** Match the situation's features to the triggers in the catalog below, drawing from more than one discipline and resisting the model you always reach for. Done when you hold several models from at least two disciplines and can say, for each, which feature of the situation called for it.
4. **Look through each lens.** Open its reference file and run its moves and questions against the situation, including the limits the authors name for it, and the value-neutral question of what it shows about what not to do. Done when each lens has produced a specific observation, prediction or option, or has been dropped with a stated reason.
5. **Connect the lenses.** Look for where the observations reinforce each other, where they conflict and which models illuminate each other (see the pairings below). Done when you can state a conclusion that survives every lens you applied, or name the conflict that remains and what would settle it.
6. **Act, then reflect.** Models are not an excuse for a lengthy decision process. Decide, record what you expected and which models you used, and compare with what happens. When a model didn't help, find out why: your understanding of it was insufficient, you missed aspects of the situation, or you focused on the wrong variable. When one worked, understand why, because "Knowing why a model works will help you know when to use it again." Done when the decision is made and there is a written expectation to check against the outcome.

## Building the latticework

The series' practice, repeated in every volume: pick a model (one a day, or one a week) and look at your life through that lens. What looks different? Write down what you notice, and reflect: the assumption that "experience is the key to learning" is wrong, because "the key is actually reflection." Note where you make a different choice, what worked and what didn't, and learn from the mistakes, until you know where each model is most useful. "At the beginning the process is more important than the outcome": it is slow at first and speeds up as the models become part of how you think. As you use more models you see the connections, find that some give their best insight paired with others, and the latticework grows comprehensive enough to use in any situation, "reducing your blind spots and preventing problems before they happen." Howard Gardner's standard: you understand a concept to the extent you can apply it appropriately in a new situation.

## Pairings the authors name

- Bottlenecks connect to surface area; pair scale with bottlenecks to see where growth will break a system first.
- Feedback loops underpin many interactions: incentives are feedback, network effects are a reinforcing loop that first needs critical mass, and Gresham's law spreads through a feedback loop until bad behavior reaches critical mass.
- Scarcity is a tool for second-order thinking; Keynesian beauty contests (bubbles) demand levels of second-order thinking.
- Efficiency erodes the margin of safety; specialization breeds interdependence; every optimization involves trade-offs.
- Irreducibility echoes first principles thinking; compounding follows a power law (distributions); representation is a feedback loop between map and territory.
- Game theory's Prisoner's Dilemma and tit for tat live under feedback loops; coordination games under interdependence.

## The catalog

Every model by its name and tagline, with the situations that indicate it. Open the file that holds the model you chose for its definition, moves, limits and cases.

**[`general-thinking.md`](general-thinking.md)**: checking what you know, how you represent it and how you reason from it.
- The Map is not the Territory, "Reality check.": relying on any report, metric, policy, model or plan; when the map and the ground disagree.
- Circle of Competence, "What don't you know?": deciding whether you know enough to act; entering an unfamiliar field; weighing advisors and their incentives.
- Supporting Idea: Falsifiability: whether a claim or theory could be proven wrong.
- First Principles Thinking, "Go back to basics.": stuck inside assumptions or convention; reverse-engineering a problem (Socratic questioning, the Five Whys).
- Thought Experiment, "Imagine the possibilities.": exploring what you can't try in life; weighing choices, counterfactuals and fairness (the veil of ignorance).
- Supporting Idea: Necessity and Sufficiency: mistaking the prerequisites for success for a guarantee of it.
- Second-Order Thinking, "What happens next?": consequences that unfold over time; unintended effects; building support for an argument.
- Probabilistic Thinking, "What are the chances?": uncertainty, risk, base rates, updating on evidence, fat tails and asymmetric outcomes.
- Supporting Idea: Causation vs. Correlation: two things that move together.
- Inversion, "Change your perspective.": stuck working forward; avoiding failure; finding the forces for and against a change (force field analysis).
- Occam's Razor, "Keep it simple.": choosing among competing explanations.
- Hanlon's Razor, "Don't assume the worst.": suspecting malice or conspiracy.

**[`physics-and-chemistry.md`](physics-and-chemistry.md)**: forces, motion, energy and reactions as metaphors for change and effort.
- Relativity, "Put it into perspective.": disagreements; one observer's view taken as the whole; seeing from another's frame.
- Reciprocity, "Give and take.": pushback; how others respond to how you treat them; giving first.
- Thermodynamics, "Reduce chaos and find order.": the energy it takes to create or maintain order; decay; closed systems.
- Inertia, "Change requires force.": resistance to change; starting or stopping; momentum and habits.
- Friction and Viscosity, "Movement is a battle.": what slows progress; removing resistance versus adding force.
- Velocity, "Direction over speed.": activity versus progress toward a goal.
- Leverage, "Enough to move the world.": getting results far greater than the effort put in.
- Activation Energy, "Get to the end.": the energy needed to start or finish something; thresholds.
- Catalysts, "Change agent.": speeding up change; finding or becoming a change agent.
- Alloying, "Greater than the sum of its parts.": combining people, skills or elements into something stronger than any alone.

**[`biology.md`](biology.md)**: living systems, literally applicable to people and organizations.
- Evolution Part One: Natural Selection and Extinction, "Adapt or die.": success and failure in a changing environment.
- Evolution Part Two: Adaptation Rate and the Red Queen Effect, "Getting better all the time.": keeping pace with competitors; rate of adaptation; repurposing what you have (exaptation).
- Supporting Idea: Competition: fights over scarce resources.
- Ecosystem, "Everything is connected.": intervening in an interconnected whole; keystones; resilience; the limiting resource (the Law of the Minimum).
- Niches, "Find a good fit.": generalist or specialist; finding a position competitors don't occupy.
- Self-Preservation, "Survive to thrive.": fear and threat responses in yourself or others; motivating change.
- Replication, "Copy copy copy.": spreading or scaling by copying; preserving intent as instructions travel (commander's intent).
- Cooperation, "Work together.": partnerships, symbiosis, trust, iterated relationships.
- Supporting Idea: Dunbar's Number: the size of groups and relationship networks.
- Hierarchical Organization, "Know your place.": status, rank and power within a group.
- Incentives, "Shape behavior.": predicting what people will do; designing rewards and punishments.
- Tendency to Minimize Energy Output, "Least-effort principle.": the path of least resistance; why people resist effort, risk and change.

**[`systems.md`](systems.md)**: anything with interacting parts: a body, a household, a company, a city.
- Feedback Loops, "Listen and incorporate.": learning from responses; reinforcing and balancing loops; delays.
- Equilibrium, "Dynamic balance.": stability, homeostasis, a system pushed out of its range.
- Bottlenecks, "The limiting factor.": throughput, constraints, work piling up in front of one point.
- Scale, "Bigger or smaller = different.": growing or shrinking anything.
- Margin of Safety, "Expect the unexpected.": buffers, backups, designing for extremes.
- Churn, "There's always movement.": attrition, turnover and renewal of parts or people.
- Algorithms, "Recipes for success.": rules and procedures that produce results without judgment at each step.
- Supporting Idea: Complex Adaptive Systems: systems whose parts adapt, and can't be understood by their components.
- Critical Mass, "Going critical.": tipping points; adoption; how minority ideas become mainstream.
- Emergence, "Organization without an organizer.": wholes that do what no part can; collective intelligence.
- Supporting Idea: Chaos Dynamics: sensitivity to initial conditions; limits of prediction.
- Irreducibility, "As simple as possible but no simpler.": the minimum that keeps a thing working; starting simple (Gall's Law).
- The Law of Diminishing Returns, "Hard work stops paying off.": when more input yields less.

**[`mathematics.md`](mathematics.md)**: numbers, chance and growth, used mostly as metaphors.
- Supporting Idea: Distributions: what outcomes to expect; normal versus power-law worlds.
- Compounding, "Play the long game.": long-term growth of money, knowledge and relationships.
- Supporting Idea: Network Effects: products or groups that grow more valuable with each user.
- Sampling, "Your samples become your reality.": generalizing from data or personal experience; who is missing from the sample.
- Randomness, "Predictability is often an illusion.": seeing patterns in noise; luck; using randomness on purpose.
- Supporting Idea: Pareto Principle: the few inputs that produce most outputs.
- Regression to the Mean, "Moderate outcomes follow extreme ones.": judging after an extreme result; luck versus skill.
- Multiplying by Zero, "The ultimate destroyer.": the weak link that cancels everything else.
- Equivalence, "Equal doesn't mean same.": different paths to equal results; the usual solution no longer available.
- Supporting Idea: Order of Magnitude: estimating and comparing very large or small quantities.
- Surface Area, "Know your exposure.": how much contact with the outside to seek or limit.
- Global and Local Maxima, "Embrace the peaks and valleys.": whether you have peaked; going down to climb higher.

**[`economics.md`](economics.md)**: allocating scarce resources, as much art as science.
- Scarcity, "When resources are finite.": shortage or abundance driving choices; what changes when a scarcity ends.
- Supply and Demand, "Scarcity drives price.": prices, markets, regulating one side, manufactured demand.
- Supporting Idea: Game Theory: modeling interacting decisions and payoffs.
- Optimization, "Perfecting the possible.": getting the most from limited resources; judging others' choices; what you measure.
- Trade-offs, "The cost of a benefit.": every choice that gives something up; opportunity cost; slack.
- Specialization, "Focus is key.": focusing a person, team or nation; comparative advantage.
- Interdependence, "Mutual reliance, intertwined fates.": supply chains, cascades, coordination failures, who you depend on.
- Efficiency, "Maximum output, minimum waste.": waste, fairness versus efficiency, lean systems that break; information flow.
- Debt, "Borrowing against the future.": any present gain paid for later.
- Monopoly and Competition, "Monopolistic might, competitive fight.": market power; winner-takes-all; concentrated control.
- Supporting Idea: Externalities: costs or benefits that land on someone not party to the deal.
- Creative Destruction, "How the mighty fall.": disruption; resistance to new ideas and paradigms.
- Gresham's Law, "Incentives matter.": bad behavior driving out good; information asymmetry; trust breaking down.
- Bubbles, "Don't get caught holding the bag.": hype, manias, prices or enthusiasm detached from value.

**[`art.md`](art.md)**: how things are experienced, presented and told.
- Audience, "Who is it for?": communicating, persuading or creating for someone.
- Genre, "Form shapes expectation.": categories, conventions and expectations; classifying people and things.
- Contrast, "Difference defines.": standing out; directing attention; presenting data ("Compared with what?").
- Framing, "Context shapes meaning.": how information is presented; what's left out of the frame; updating your own frames.
- Rhythm, "Pace yourself.": routines, pacing, cadence, coordinating a group.
- Melody, "Tones tell tales.": the core that makes something recognizable; common elements linking different groups.
- Representation, "Influence through presentation.": reading the purpose behind how something is depicted; open-source information.
- Supporting Idea: Balance: harmony among competing elements.
- Plot, "Sequence shapes story.": the story told about events; narrative bias; changing a narrative.
- Character, "Heroes and villains.": who is telling the story; cults of personality; attachment to characters.
- Setting, "Where the action happens.": how place and time shape what's possible; changing behavior by changing environment.
- Supporting Idea: Subtext: what is conveyed without being said.
- Performance, "Break a leg.": live interaction with an audience and space; how work keeps developing once it meets the world.
