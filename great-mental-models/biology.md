# Biology

Models from the life sciences. The authors treat these more literally than the physics models: people and organizations are living systems, so cooperation, niches and incentives apply directly. Each entry gives the model, its moves, its limits and its cases.

## Evolution Part One: Natural Selection and Extinction: "Adapt or die."

**The model.** Evolution explains success and failure, the relationship between an individual and its environment, and why you must plan for constant change. To "adapt or die," first understand what you are responding to, not just how to adapt. Jacques Monod: "A totally blind process can by definition lead to anything; it can even lead to vision itself."

**Natural selection** is not choice. The more favorable a trait in a particular environment, the likelier its bearer lives long enough to reproduce, so the trait's frequency shifts with the environment. Geerat Vermeij calls it "nonrandom elimination." Selection is "very much about advantages here and now, not in the distant future"; long-term traits emerge only because they also work for individuals now. Slow zebras get eaten, so the herd gets faster; inverted, useless adaptations are selected against. Rafe Sagarin's three elements: "variation between individuals, environmental conditions that favor (or select) certain variants over others, and a means to reproduce those variants." There are many ways to succeed (a hard kick, sharp eyes), and traits must be repeatable, passed on genetically or by learning. Vermeij: new circumstances are calamitous for a unit when they are very large or very rare relative to its size and lifespan.

**Extinction** is the death of the last member (Martha, the last passenger pigeon, in 1914, after hunting wiped out billions in decades). The point of no return comes earlier: *Allee effects*, where population density drops below a viable threshold. The precise cause is rarely identifiable; it must be inferred from outcomes, and may be gradual. Causes are competition (a better-adapted rival; two species needing exactly the same resources cannot coexist; invasive species) and environmental change (climate, deforestation, a volcano), working through nonlinear interdependencies and cascades. Of some 10 million species, under 20% are catalogued. *Background extinction* is constant; *mass extinction* has a single cause, such as a meteor.

**Meta-natural selection.** "The very evolutionary process that ensures a species' survival can also be its downfall." Selection in a stable environment is refinement, which lacks resilience to change; the best-adapted organism in normal conditions may struggle with volatility. "Generalist species are far more resilient than specialists" (the rat and the cockroach versus the panda). Another guard is producing many offspring fast; periodic mass starvation in deer and elk benefits the species. Roberta Bondar: a business's usual response to change is denial, or adapting with a model that no longer works; first recognize the need to survive, then move with new ideas.

**Cases.**
- *French.* French grew from Latin with words from Norse, Gaulish, Frankish, Arabic, Spanish and Italian. Three events made it: the fall of Rome, the conquest of England and Paris's rise as the center of power ("people who wanted power knew they needed French"). Fixed grammar and spelling served business, printing set rules, dictionaries made it easier to learn, and colonial French adapted. Its flaw: the French Academy, uninterested in industry, science and money, ignored technical vocabulary. "You aren't going to use a language ... if it has no words for the elements you need to express." Through this lens, purists freezing the language make exactly the wrong move: "If a language cannot adapt, it will cease to be useful. If it ceases to be useful, it will go extinct."
- *Latin* is dead, native to no community, having ceased as a mother tongue by the seventh century CE. Complexity was the main reason: selection pushes languages toward the easiest to learn, and without a central authority Vulgar Latin diverged into Italian, French, Portuguese, Romanian and Spanish. It survives in Vatican use, ceremony and technical vocabulary (medicine, taxonomy, law), and over 60% of English words come from Latin or Greek.

Natural selection is the hidden hand and extinction the hammer; firms are swept away by creative destruction too. Skills, knowledge and ways of thinking must evolve. There are "no permanent victories."

## Evolution Part Two: Adaptation Rate and the Red Queen Effect: "Getting better all the time."

**The model.** Vermeij: "Adaptation is as good as it has to be; it need not be the best that could be designed. Adaptation depends on context." Deal with the environment you're in, not the one you wish for; you need not be the objective best, only better than your competitors, since "living things do only as well as they have to rather than optimize." *Adaptation* is both a trait and a process. "Adaptability controls the sweet spot between reaction and prediction": it lets you meet a wide range of challenges, not just the anticipated ones. Adaptation usually means leaving, or being forced from, your comfort zone. Nature works with the raw materials at hand, so different species reach the same solutions (food production arose separately in at least a dozen places). Genetic adaptation is slow; learning works on a shorter timescale. The peppered moth's dark form thrived in industrial soot, and the light form came back with pollution control.

On a human timescale, adaptability is "recognizing when the way you have done things in the past is becoming less and less successful in a changing environment," then innovating as mutations do.

**The Red Queen effect.** You can't stop adapting, because no one around you stops; the least fit die first. "Staying the same as we are often means falling behind." Leigh Van Valen (1973): no species is protected from extinction, however long it has lasted. Lewis Carroll's Red Queen: "it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place." Leopards and antelopes can't choose; people can, and if even 20 percent try to pull ahead, little is left for the rest. In business there is no plateau to coast on: competitors keep working and customers' needs keep changing, so adapting is a daily core function. Vermeij on feedback: positive (reinforcing) feedback drives a runaway arms race; negative (stabilizing) feedback damps fluctuations. Some organisms (bacteria) run an aggressive Red Queen race, others (the cockroach) a mild one; high flexibility and learning can slow how hard you feel it.

**Limits of the race.** Human arms races carry immense cost for no advantage. Where beneficial adaptation has an end, change parts of the environment instead of racing in a way that undermines your overall ability to adapt. "Speed of adaptation is not the same thing as effective adaptation": what matters is what problems it solves and what problems an enemy's adaptation creates. Three principles:
1. An adaptation must be useful; it must improve function.
2. Adaptations come with trade-offs (big human brains mean babies born vulnerable and long dependency).
3. An organism must be viable at every stage of the adaptation: "if you are compromising your physical health or your sanity, you are not adapting." "Running as fast as you can to stay in place is not a euphemism for sixteen-hour workdays."
Define what success means for you (there's no universal human definition), but it must involve benefit. Vermeij: "Success is measured by persistence." And "No innovation comes into existence perfectly hewn. Error is thus necessary for the generation of variation."

**The trap.** Once we gain an advantage we want to hold it at all costs, which slows adaptation. Let go of what worked in the past. "There's no such thing as a permanent lead." "Complacency will kill you." "Standing still is the quickest path to extinction."

**Vestigial structures** (sidebar): traits with no remaining function (ostrich wings, goose bumps from ancestors fluffing fur, the remnant pelvis in snakes and whales, the mole's skin-covered eyes). They persist when they cause no harm, fade gradually, sometimes cost more to remove than to keep, and may have an undiscovered purpose. Organisms are not perfectly formed.

**Exaptation** (Stephen Jay Gould and Elisabeth Vrba, 1982): a trait's current use does not necessarily explain its origin. Dinosaur feathers first insulated or attracted mates, and later enabled flight; the panda repurposes a wrist bone most mammals have for gripping bamboo. Adaptation arises for its function (bat echolocation); exaptation repurposes a structure. The distinction isn't always clear, and it is the insight into repurposing acquired skills and knowledge. Vermeij: "Increased autonomy allows parts to take on new, often specialized, functions."
- *Programmability.* About 1,200 years ago in Baghdad the Banu Musa brothers built "the Instrument Which Plays by Itself," a hydraulic organ with a pinned cylinder. Music boxes and player pianos followed; in 1940 Hedy Lamarr and George Antheil, who had synchronized player pianos in his *Ballet Mécanique*, used perforated ribbons to make a torpedo's control signal hop frequencies so it couldn't be jammed. For almost a thousand years we had that meta-tool and used it only to play music (Steven Johnson: inventions depend on the other inventions that complete them).
- *Innovation without a plan.* We don't always know a thing's value at the outset, and not everything needs a justification first (Marie Curie on radium as pure science). Bubble Wrap (1957) failed as textured wallpaper and greenhouse insulation, then found its use protecting IBM computers in transit. Play-Doh spent some twenty years as a wallpaper cleaner for coal soot before a teacher used it for crafts. Botox went from treating eye disorders and spasms to Jean Carruthers noticing its cosmetic effect in 1987.
- *The move.* What exaptation "is fundamentally about is flexibility": a box of diverse tools. Its opposite is *functional fixedness*, seeing only an object's intended use (a fork is also a hook, a tack, a detangler). "Do what you can, with what you've got, where you are." The creativity test: list the uses of a brick.

**Case: France, 1940.** Versailles had held Germany to an army of 100,000 with no air force, tanks or submarines; in 1940 France fielded 110 divisions to Germany's 98, and Liddell Hart judged the French to have "ample superiority." But France had not adapted to the changed nature of war: modern equipment without modern organization, no real airpower, tanks used as servants of infantry with 1918 eyes. The Germans, "rather hesitatingly," tried the new theory of high-speed warfare. As with mutations, fitness did not require everyone to adapt at once: a few in power, like General Heinz Guderian, pushed deep penetration by independent armored forces (an idea from British postwar thinking) despite superiors' doubts, "the decisive effect of a new idea, carried out by a dynamic executant." Like moth color, it succeeded only in that environment.

## Supporting Idea: Competition

Competition is the driving force of the biological world. Resources are finite (food, status, territory, mates), the fight for them is "a zero-sum game," and it is inherently harmful to the losers. "The scarcer the resource, the more aggressive the competition." It is *intraspecific* (within a species: male zebras fight over females and kill rivals' offspring) or *interspecific* (between species: trees competing for sunlight), and *direct* (active fighting) or *indirect* (no confrontation). In abundance, species breed until they are forced to compete. Competition is why nature is so diverse. In business it forces improvement and keeps prices low and quality high, which is why monopolies are discouraged (abuse, stagnation).

## Ecosystem: "Everything is connected."

**The model.** An ecosystem is a community of interacting species plus their nonliving environment; a change in any component affects individual fitness and the health of the whole. Because the parts interact, interventions are not predictable: look at the whole, and sometimes leave it alone. *Intervention bias* is the urge to always do something. Ecosystems evolved to manage themselves; they are "quite capable of self-organizing." Vermeij: a species dominant in one interaction may be subordinate in another, so control diffuses. Ecosystems come in any size (puddle to ocean), few are closed, and migration ties them together. Human ecosystems include families, teams, cities and organizations, and people move across them.

**Keystone species** (Robert T. Paine's term, from the stone that holds an arch): organisms whose absence would change or collapse the ecosystem. They are hard to identify, often few or unseen, and "we only know for sure" when their numbers drop and the second-order effects appear. Often predators, sometimes herbivores, their value is providing what others need or controlling population levels. Sea otters eat urchins, which would otherwise strip the kelp forests that pull CO2 from the water.

**Resistance, sensitivity, resilience.** Ecosystems are not static. *Resistance* is the tendency to stay near equilibrium, taking a significant disturbance to move. *Sensitivity* is when weak disturbances have profound effects. *Resilience* is the speed of recovery. Strength has more than one dimension: a sensitive system with high resilience can be as strong as a resistant one that is slow to bounce back.

**Law of the Minimum** (sidebar). Yield is dictated by the essential nutrient in shortest supply; raise it and another becomes the limit, like a bucket with a hole (Carl Sprengel, 1820s; popularized by Justus von Liebig). Farmers skimping on one pricey fertilizer cap their whole harvest; skimp on sleep and tiredness becomes your limiting factor. (It links to Bottlenecks and Multiplying by Zero.)

**Moves.** Look at patterns of relationship and feedback, since the whole is more than the sum of its parts and behavior emerges from interactions. Systems self-correct; interventions bring unintended consequences. "Be slow to intervene," understand the cascade, and "First, do no harm." Living in a web (sidebar): too much of one external factor can kill a system, and a business depends on external stability (customers with money, a stable economy and tax regime, an education system, urban structure). "If we love our system, we must also do what we can to influence the external factors."

**Cases.**
- *Forest fires.* Suppressing every fire builds fuel until fires exceed what the ecosystem can handle; "the harder we try to control ecosystems, the harder they fight back." Fire releases nutrients, opens the canopy to light and clears invasive and diseased plants.
- *Elephants.* Captive breeding and artificial insemination are expensive and limited, with higher infant mortality, compared with preserving habitat.
- *The silver trade* (Charles C. Mann, *1493*), China and Spain in the sixteenth century. (1) Closed system: when Beijing banned foreign trade, it continued through smugglers and pirates until the criminal network threatened the government and the ban was repealed. (2) Opening: China's own currency was failing and its silver mines depleted ("an economic system without the raw materials to produce new currency is like a forest that stops getting light"); the policy change acted like an environmental change, where some adapted into new niches (silk by the ton, knockoffs of European styles) and others died, and Beijing, unable to control either the trade or the silver supply, watched silver's value drop. When ecosystems change, new species become dominant or keystone. (3) Manila's Chinese community survived seven massacres by the Spanish, who failed to value the common components of their system; trade and the town always came back, because abundance of goods and people is resilience. Sagarin: diversity imparts long-term survival.
- *Bill Walsh's 49ers.* The worst team in 1979 became a dynasty by 1989. Walsh built a culture as a system: "everyone has a role and every role is essential," players expected a lot of each other, and flexibility met unexpected obstacles. He treated the team as an open ecosystem with a Life Skills Program (continuing education, personal and family counseling, drug counseling, financial advice), pushed different buttons for different people, and scouted for each player's possible contribution, reasons to take him rather than only reasons not to. Copycats of his West Coast offense failed: different organisms produce different systems even in similar environments (the deserts of Sudan and Australia), so "ingredients matter."

## Niches: "Find a good fit."

**The model.** *Generalists* hold large territories, face more competition and are flexible; *specialists* hold small territories, face less competition and have rigid requirements. Neither is always better, but knowing which you are shapes strategy. An *ecological niche* is the role a species plays: everything affecting its ability to survive and reproduce, including *abiotic factors* (nonliving aspects). Generalists (cockroaches, rats, raccoons, humans) tolerate change and build large populations. Specialists (tiger salamanders breeding only in fishless ponds; koalas on eucalyptus; pandas on bamboo) are prone to extinction but do well in stable environments with few competitors, like tropical rainforests. Vermeij: a generalist approach is adequate when the stakes are low; specialization is mandated when they are high. Humans are a generalist species whose individuals specialize strongly. "There are riches in niches."

**Success is often a product of environment.** Someone who thrives in one setting can fail in another if part of the performance was the setting, which is why a superstar hired from a competitor often disappoints. Peter Ungar: move, die, or change.

**Competitive exclusion principle** (sidebar, *Gause's law*): perfect competition between two species needing the same resources in the same niche is impossible. Georgii Gause showed it in 1934 with microorganisms in a dish: one specializes (*resource partitioning*) or is wiped out by the other's slight advantages, which is why nature is diverse. Fitness means suited to the environment *and* adapted relative to competitors. British red squirrels (about 140,000) are losing to introduced grays (about 2.5 million), which eat acorns before they ripen, digest them better and carry a virus.

**Surviving and thriving.**
- An invention useful to everyone is a generalist entering new territory: lock down as large a territory as you can defend before other generalists arrive, and make sure it holds everything you need to survive.
- A specialized invention, once it owns its niche, is hard to dislodge, but its growth is capped (Zildjian has made cymbals since 1623).
- Generalists live with daily stress and a constant fight for share; specialists have low daily stress until the environment changes, when stress explodes and the niche may vanish (encyclopedias). "Specialists have less competition and stress, but only in times of stability. Generalists face greater day-to-day challenges ... but have more flexibility to respond when times change."
- In a career, find the niche that rewards your strengths and neutralizes your weaknesses. Niche occupants grow fragile; the successful ones evolve their niche.

**Convergence.** Unrelated organisms evolve analogous traits their common ancestor lacked (*analogous structures*, *homoplasies*) because niches with the same constraints reward the same solutions: flight in birds, bats and insects; camera eyes in humans and octopuses, whose common ancestor lived 550 million years ago; echolocation. Evolution is not entirely random, and the ways to fulfill a function are finite: "we can tell by looking at a niche what kind of organisms would occupy it." "If there's a keyhole, there's also a key." People under the same pressures and incentives reach the same outcomes, and disconnected cultures built similar tools and stories. Since "it is the environment that makes the organism," in the same circumstances we might act the same, which builds empathy: "we are all more predictable than we would like to admit." The move: equivalent problems tend to have equivalent solutions, so look at what worked for others instead of treating your problem as unique.

**Cases.**
- *The fax machine* (Jonathan Coopersmith). Invented in the 1840s, it survived 150-plus years by lurching from niche to niche, since general users didn't exist and it couldn't beat the generalist telegraph. Newspapers came first (fewer than a thousand machines in 1940, with impact out of proportion), then the military (an exact copy, nothing lost in translation). Deliberate incompatibility kept the market fragmented, until 1980s deregulation, compatibility and falling costs made it a generalist for a time.
- *Coca-Cola* became a generalist by advertising, selling an idea before a product: $750,000 in 1909, a national brand by the 1920s, bottling worldwide in World War II, the top ad budget by 1963. "Things go better with Coke" let the consumer fill in the blank, while Pepsi took the niche of young middle-class families. Flexibility, not taste, was the key.

## Self-Preservation: "Survive to thrive."

**The model.** Innate behaviors that protect us from harm govern much of what we do, sometimes pushing us to reject the status quo and sometimes holding us back; managing them helps motivate ourselves and others. Reflexes (pulling back from a hot stove, blinking) are hardwired. The *fight, flight, or freeze* response comes from the sympathetic nervous system: fight or flight when we think we can succeed, freeze when stressors have piled up too high. People can override instincts (a roller coaster is a thrill) or suffer them chronically (stress).

**Kin selection.** Survival of the group can require the sacrifice of members, so selfless behavior is selected when it benefits the population. The black lace-weaver spider is eaten by her young; elephants, zebras and sea lions protect others' offspring; worker bees die for the hive ("bad for the individuals, excellent for the hive"); ground squirrels give alarm calls at their own risk. "Biological motivation is to ensure one's own survival, but not if it comes at the cost of the survival of one's own genes."

**Territorial behavior** (sidebar). A territory is a region with resources and mating opportunities that an organism actively defends (scent, guarding, calls). Defense is costly, so it signals a serious survival advantage, and it isn't needed when resources are abundant. "The scarcer resources prove to be, the more aggressive the territorial behavior."

**More than survival.** For people, survival isn't binary; we want meaning. Gioconda Belli joined the Sandinista revolution in 1970s Nicaragua, which looks like the opposite of self-preservation, but the model explains "sacrificing short-term guarantees for long-term possibilities," a *deferred preservation* ("Your daughter is precisely the reason you should do it"). It was also the only way into a more meaningful existence: "If I gave in to fear, I would end up killing my soul to save my body." Self-preservation covers psychological well-being, social status and identity: "Anything that threatens how we see ourselves becomes a threat."

**Permanent record** (sidebar). People preserve themselves through records. The fire that destroyed Nineveh in 612 BCE baked Ashurbanipal's clay tablets, preserving a flood story, found when Austen Henry Layard excavated the site in 1849. Preserving knowledge supports the preservation of the species.

**Case: Derinkuyu.** Found in 1963 during a basement renovation in Turkey, the underground city reached 200 to 280 feet down through eighteen levels and could shelter 20,000 to 30,000 people, with schools, places of worship, stables, more than 52 ventilation shafts, single-file tunnels sealed by 1,100-pound stone disks, and a tunnel to another city: maximum leverage for defense. Yet fleeing and hoarding were not sustainable long-term (vitamin deficiencies, sieges).

**Moves and limits.** People hoard on a hair trigger at the slightest scarcity, even when it worsens things: fearing layoffs, they hoard information and work, trust breaks down, less gets done and their jobs are more at risk. "Not helping others or sharing the leverage is worse for everyone." In an emergency, have a concrete plan beyond hiding and hoarding, and consider long-term survival. Too much focus on threats means missed opportunities and stagnation; protect what's essential and let go of what no longer serves. "Often the greatest risk is to not take risk at all."

## Replication: "Copy copy copy."

**The model.** DNA copying itself; *mitosis* replicates nonsexual cells into two identical daughter cells. Three requirements:
1. A code representing what you wish to replicate.
2. A means of copying the code.
3. A place to process the code and construct the replica.

Vermeij: most copying errors harm a well-adapted unit, so fidelity, or concentrating error where it does least harm, is favored. Krishnamurti drew the line between copying outward technique, which is fine, and inward imitation, which ends creativity.

**Combination.** Replicas needn't be exact. *Meiosis* and sexual reproduction produce variation, and so options when the environment changes, at the cost of finding another half. "Exact copies perpetuate bad mutations." Replication is information transfer, always accompanied by degradation or change, and its imperfections fuel evolution.

**Keeping it all in the family: the Habsburgs.** From the eleventh to the eighteenth century the Habsburgs grew by marriage ("Let others wage war; you, happy Austria, marry") and married within the family. The Spanish branch, 1527 to 1661, had thirty-four children, half of whom died in their first decade; the line ended with the infertile Charles II. "Closed systems, those without any new inputs, die in changing environments." "Replication is necessary but not sufficient for survival. The more you copy something, the more it weakens," like a photocopy of a photocopy. "Without diversity, replication only works if the original is perfect."

**The sweet spot: commander's intent.** Components must be rigid enough to copy easily and flexible enough to adapt. Napoleon beat German armies trained in linear tactics and blind obedience; in response they developed *Auftragstaktik*, or *commander's intent*: sharing information to empower subordinate commanders on the scene. Too rigid, and plans can't adapt; too little fidelity, and the strategy fills with errors; flexibility must not cost the ability to synchronize. Clausewitz: strategy must go with the army into the field. Four elements: the senior *formulates* and *communicates*, the subordinate *interprets* and *implements*. Four criteria:
1. Explain the rationale (how the decision was reached).
2. Establish operational limits (what's off the table).
3. Get feedback often.
4. Recognize individual differences.

**Replicating a culture: tea.** Tea spread by a repeated pattern of exposure, trade, then growing it yourself: from China (about 3,000 years) to Japan (Myōan Eisai in 1191, then the *chanoyu* ceremony), to Russia (seventeenth-century trade routes, the samovar, tea promoted for temperance against vodka, a garden near Batumi in 1893), to Persia (via Central Asia, helped by Islam's ban on alcohol; a Persian consul smuggled some 3,000 Assam seedlings to Lāhījan). It replicated because it is a "firm concept within a flexible package": endlessly flexible (green, oolong, black; spices, milk, brewing methods) around a core that can't change (it comes only from the tea plant).

**Moves.** Don't reinvent the wheel: when starting, imitate what others do to reach an average baseline; once you have a feel for it, innovate to set a new baseline. Replication is exponential (viruses, viral ideas, memes), and unchecked replication is cancerous. "Effective replication requires enough structure and space to produce a copy and enough flexibility to adapt that copy." What worked for a while isn't effective in perpetuity. Favor replicating what is true and good; resist replicating what is false or harmful.

## Cooperation: "Work together."

**The model.** *Symbiosis*: an organism that can't perform a function alone uses another's body, and the other benefits too, extending both organisms' adaptive capacity. It's usually win-win, not always required for survival but improving quality of life (a shark and its cleaner fish), and it creates "emergent properties that have more power than the individual components." Mitochondria were once bacteria absorbed into a cell more than a billion years ago; they make ATP, the cell's energy currency, and without them complex life couldn't have evolved. Cows digest cellulose through gut bacteria; the Hawaiian bobtail squid camouflages itself with light from *Vibrio fischeri*. Lichens (algae plus fungi) have properties neither has alone. Sagarin: cooperation is its own evolutionary force, opening adaptive responses to future challenges. The African proverb: "If you want to go quickly, go alone. If you want to go far, go together."

**Progress takes the fast track.** Cooperation can be so valuable that organisms become permanently part of each other, and dependable cooperation frees energy for growth and innovation. Railroads and the telegraph "marched across the continent in unison" (Alfred Chandler): the telegraph reported trains' progress, the railroads provided poles and stations, and neither spent resources duplicating the other. The move: we track "the competition"; how many devote resources to looking for "the cooperation"?

**Exceptional harmony: the orchestra.** Conductor Alexander Shelley likens a great orchestra to a flock of birds, a symbiosis between him and eighty musicians; perfect cooperation is the difference between good and inspirational, and it extends to a feedback loop with the audience (Mariss Jansons). Trust is essential because musicians mainly hear the instruments nearest them, and it is all-or-nothing: one bad player ruins the performance. The Montreal Women's Symphony Orchestra (1940), founded by Madge Bowen and Ethel Stark, required only commitment and passion, included Violet Grant (the first Black Canadian permanent member of a symphony) and became the first Canadian orchestra invited to Carnegie Hall, before being shut down after funding was denied. Cooperation arises from the latent understanding that no one can do everything; no species is adapted to every condition.

**Shared belief** (sidebar, Yuval Noah Harari). Humans imagine things with no physical counterpart, and "large numbers of strangers can cooperate successfully by believing in common myths": currency, laws, corporations, nations. Most such networks have been geared toward oppression and exploitation, and by inertia, the more encompassing a shared belief, the more we forget it is a human construct.

**Conditions.** "If there is any one model that explains humanity, then this is it." Cooperation answers the puzzle of natural selection through reciprocity and shared interest: organisms cooperate when they benefit more than by competing. But "cooperation is not automatic. It requires specific conditions—repeated interactions, shared benefits, mechanisms to prevent cheating," norms that reward cooperation and punish defection, and protection from selfishness and short-term thinking.

## Supporting Idea: Dunbar's Number

Robin Dunbar: we can maintain about 150 stable relationships, a limit set by the size of the neocortex; beyond it comes information overload. Communities of about 150 recur across history (hunter-gatherer groups, military units, businesses, Domesday Book villages, Christmas-card lists). Large brains are primarily a social adaptation. The layers, each including the smaller:
- 5: inner core
- 15: close friends
- 50: friends
- 150: community, bound by "trust and obligation—there's some personal history"
- 500: acquaintances, friends of friends
- 1,500: faces you can put names to

Social success means knowing these limits and investing time accordingly: when do social efforts bring diminishing returns?

## Hierarchical Organization: "Know your place."

**The model.** A linear or near-linear dominance ranking among individuals living close together (baboons, wolves, chickens, bears, elephant seals). It is stable when those lower down accept their position, which means less fighting and more order. Peter Kaufman: civilized society handles hierarchy politely, but it permeates everything.

**Getting to the top.** The implied contract: the dominant provide direction to food, protection and the maintenance of order. Thorleif Schjelderup-Ebbe found peace only in hen flocks with an established pecking order (one hen recognized 27 others). An alpha chimp wins by leading a large, stable coalition, not by strength.

**Costs and benefits.**
- "Hierarchies in the human world act as information filters": ideas and opportunities from the bottom get missed (a low-ranking chimp's good strategy isn't copied). They are where we draw ego, status and reputation, and they condition us to grow ourselves rather than others. They reduce creativity, stress those at the bottom (raising mortality), pressure those at the top, and are inherently unequal.
- But people self-organize into hierarchy without imposed structure. In "flat" organizations people get frazzled navigating unspoken power structures; anarchist movements have leaders; removing a title doesn't change who sets the example, so ask who you want that person to be.
- Structure hierarchies beneficially (grant prestige for belonging to the group rather than to individuals): "use hierarchies as a tool, not be used by them." "Too much hierarchy leads to unrest and instability. Too little leads to chaos." Organizations that emphasize status get torn apart as people fight to the top instead of for the organization's success.
- **Boss vs. leader** (sidebar, Ronald Heifetz): authority is not leadership. There is authority without leading, and leadership without authority.
- **Status symbols** (sidebar). Fashion negotiates hierarchy (Nancy Etcoff), in the tension between the comfort of knowing our place and chafing at it. Harari: hierarchies let strangers know how to treat each other without becoming acquainted. Sumptuary rules (purple for royalty), blackened teeth in Elizabethan England (sugar was a luxury), long nails and cost barriers signal rank; dressing scruffy when you needn't signals secure status.

**Cases.**
- *The French Revolution.* France's three estates sat in a great chain of being, the Third Estate being 96 to 98% of the people; land rents rose 142% while farm prices rose 60%, and about three-quarters of peasants farmed under five hectares, under absolutism ("L'état, c'est moi"). The Revolution ran through the Terror (some 17,000 beheaded) and the Directory to Napoleon, who seized power in 1799, made himself emperor in 1804 and reinstated absolutism, though in a system based more on ability than class. Lessons: upending a hierarchy creates instability as many maneuver over its replacement, and the new hierarchy is still a hierarchy (Talleyrand survived by reinventing himself); ask what kind of leaders your hierarchical system will produce. Sam Walker (*The Captain Class*): the people who win the competition for leadership (Robespierre, Napoleon) are skilled at competing for it, not at running the show.
- *The Copiapó mine*, 2010. Hierarchy rests on *biological enablers and cultural constraints*. Thirty-three miners were trapped 700 feet down with a refuge stocked for ten men for two days. The shift foreman, Luis Urzua, had formal authority but under three months in the job and no bond with the men; Mario Sepulveda and Mario Gomez rallied people but lacked culturally backed authority. For the first day no one was accountable to anyone and kin subgroups formed. By day five, with a spiritual leader, a democratic process for food and latrines, and Sepulveda urging respect for Urzua, authority settled on Urzua and Sepulveda, with tasks assigned by skill and mental stability. Walker: great captains "lowered themselves in relation to the group"; "The easiest way to lead, it turns out, is to serve." The order held to day 17 on one bite of food every 36 hours. When a shaft broke through on day 18, a new top of the pyramid appeared aboveground; discipline declined and fights broke out (over TV channels) as the men were pulled between two hierarchies. At the rescue on day 70, Urzua came out last, and the men left as a group.

Hierarchy is "invisible scaffolding": it enables specialization, division of labor and complex behavior from simple rules (the ant colony), and it is fluid, so a change at one level ripples through.

## Incentives: "Shape behavior."

**The model.** We move toward rewards and away from punishment. The drive is intrinsic and biological, so not always rational: we value an incentive through the biased lens of self-esteem, personal narrative and physiological state. Munger: "Never, ever, think about something else when you should be thinking about the power of incentives." "If you understand the incentive, you can predict the outcome."

**Behavior modification.** Consistent but infrequent rewards change behavior more strongly than constant ones (the rat at the lever), and anticipation itself is an incentive. Storage (fat, money) gives flexibility: savings store purchasing power and make you less vulnerable to every minor incentive. We tolerate uncertain incentives because new attempts usually fail at first. The dark side: wants become requirements (addiction rewires the brain).

**Motivated by uncertainty** (sidebar). A possible payoff stimulates more than a sure thing (a board game you're guaranteed to win is no fun). The limit: when the result's value outweighs the process's (life-changing money), take the guaranteed win.

**Short-term incentives, long-term effects.** People generally can't prioritize deferred rewards.
- Democracies: voters want immediate benefits (Aristotle); Niall Ferguson notes that great-great-grandchildren are harder to relate to; rapid feedback (elections, polls, media) gives little reason to look past the current cycle.
- Public companies: quarterly reports, short CEO tenures and bonuses breed perverse incentives and a hamster wheel of tweaks in place of timeless products.
- Use-it-or-lose-it budgets: US federal agencies spend 8.7% of their budgets in the last week of the year, nearly five times a typical week, and last-week projects are two to six times as likely to be low quality.
"To change a system involving humans, we need to change the incentives." Aligning incentives is a core leadership challenge; Sun Tzu's commander climbs a height and kicks away the ladder, so the only way is forward together.

**Manipulated by incentive.** Incentives are most insidious when they appeal to our personal narrative, prompting intellectual contortions to justify the outcome as good. Levitt and Dubner: "An incentive is a bullet, a key." Money alone isn't enough to explain behavior; look at the psychological condition an incentive creates, chiefly *cognitive dissonance* (Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson) and its chain of self-justification: "I am a good person. I supported this drug... So, the drug can't be harmful." "Acceptance of an initial incentive creates a psychological state whereby we become invested in maintaining whatever story brought about that incentive," turning toward it "like a plant turning toward the sun."
- *Thalidomide.* In the 1950s Chemie Grünenthal had "a drug without a disease"; unable to find a dose that killed rats, it declared the drug nontoxic and handed out free samples, making the general population its lab. It caused nerve damage and birth defects. The team was paid a share of profits, partners avoided R&D costs, doctors were paid and articles ghostwritten, so reputations depended on defending it; the company attacked physicians, never admitted fault and settled out of court. In the US, FDA reviewer Dr. Frances Kelsey stalled approval for lack of evidence about the placental barrier; with a drug that wasn't lifesaving she had "very low incentive for approving."
- *Andrew Wakefield* published the vaccines-autism claim in *The Lancet* while being paid more than $800,000 by lawyers, leaving "no incentive to look for disconfirming evidence."

**Moves.** Be aware of the incentives directing your actions and of how others incentivize you. Weigh what you really value (impact, kindness, honesty) against the rewards on offer (money, attention, power); you "need not blindly follow incentives if they don't align with our values." Watch for subtle incentives too (social approval, habit), for short- and long-term incentives that differ (the gym), and for poorly designed incentives that backfire into short-term thinking and unethical behavior. Craft incentives that reward behavior leading to long-term success.

## Tendency to Minimize Energy Output: "Least-effort principle."

**The model.** Organisms conserve energy so they have extra in times of need (Maupertuis: nature is thrifty in all its actions). Cold-blooded animals and turtles wintering at the bottom of ponds conserve; sharkskin's backward-pointing scales cut drag. Efficiency in ongoing, repetitive requirements boosts survival, and "change is costly for most organisms," so the instinct produces resistance to change and to risk. The human task is to make minimizing output increase effectiveness rather than laziness. The model is a lens on our default patterns of thinking and moving.

**Desire paths** (sidebar). The paths people wear between two points, ignoring the zigzag laid out for them. Planners can use them; ignoring the instinct builds spaces that impede movement.

**The lazy brain.** *Heuristics* are an efficiency mechanism: a chess player weighs two or three moves, not ten million. Decisions carry *cognitive load*, so we *satisfice*, taking the first option that meets minimum acceptable conditions.
- Heuristics are reliable given "a stable environment, frequent exposure (large sample size), and immediate, unambiguous feedback" (Gary Klein's firefighters, since fires obey chemistry and physics). They are unreliable where feedback is slow, samples small and regression to the mean muddies results (clinical therapy).
- Innate heuristics include anchoring, availability and representativeness (Kahneman and Tversky). The *affect heuristic* substitutes an easy question (How do I feel?) for a hard one (What do I think?), which is fine for trivial choices (a detergent by its smell).
- Kahneman's *law of least effort*: we gravitate to the least demanding course. "Laziness is built deep into our nature."
- **Correction:** remember base rates and attend to the quality of information. If 80% of people prefer vanilla and 20% chocolate, a news story about an athlete who loves a cocoa-avocado flavor shouldn't stop you guessing vanilla. Go back to the base rates and ask whether the new information could change them substantially. Sometimes we have to invest extra calories.

**Case: office design.** Open-plan homes save energy (watching children, finding keys) but still have private spaces and trust. Open offices raise the effort of focusing (ignoring distractions), breed superficial, performative interaction, and cut face-to-face contact by as much as 70%. Frank Lloyd Wright's 1930s Johnson Wax building (high ceilings, mezzanine offices, spread-out desks), the 1950s German "office landscape" of partitions arranged by information flow, and Robert Propst's 1960s Action Office, which cost-cutting turned into the cubicle farm, show the cycle. Office design must honor the tendency to minimize energy output, giving people space to focus and to move.

"Experience doesn't become learning without reflection, and reflection is an energy expenditure." Know the tendency, and correct for it where doing so creates value.
