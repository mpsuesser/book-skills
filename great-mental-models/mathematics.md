# Mathematics

Models from mathematics, which the authors use mostly as metaphors: compounding for relationships and knowledge, multiplying by zero for the weak link, maxima for the peaks of a career. Shakuntala Devi framed mathematics as the systematic effort of solving puzzles posed by nature. Each entry gives the model, its moves, its limits and its cases.

## Supporting Idea: Distributions

A distribution describes how a group spreads out or bunches up and how often each value appears. It contextualizes expectations and lets you predict probability and frequency. Four characteristics determine the type:
1. Is it discrete or continuous?
2. Is it symmetric or asymmetric?
3. Does it have upper and lower limits?
4. How likely are extreme values?

Distributions are idealized; fitting one is part art, part science, balancing fit against ease of estimation. You never really know you have the right one, and future data may change it.

- **Normal distribution.** The most familiar and important lens, and easy to take too literally, fitting reality to the model when reality is rarely neat. Most values cluster around a midpoint that is the mean, mode and median at once: the bell curve (height, IQ, blood pressure, exam results, measurement errors, the price of toothpaste), usually because of physical constraints. Its tails go on forever, and **long-tail values**, though forgotten, can have outsize impact (the one road closure that wrecks your commute).
- **Power law.** Values cluster at the low or high end with no cap (wealth: $1,000 versus $1 billion). Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths call these "scale-free distributions" (town sizes). A normally distributed thing that has gone on too long is bound to end; with a power law, the longer it has gone on, the longer you should expect it to continue. Identifying a power law makes you realistic about the effort to break out of the end cluster, and makes you consider the full range of possible values (motivating for wealth, sobering for calamities).
- **Others:** *geometric* (when a success might happen), *binomial* (how many successes in a number of tries), *Poisson* (rare events in a large population), *memoryless* (the future probability doesn't depend on time since the last event: the bus).

**Case: Epicurus and the good life.** Around 300 BCE Epicurus held pleasure the only realistic measure of a good life, which later readers misread as hedonism: "No pleasure is a bad thing in itself. But the things which produce certain pleasures bring troubles many times greater." On a bell curve with great pain at the far left and great pleasure at the far right, he aimed for the middle, neither; pleasure meant the absence of pain ("Happiness is tranquility," in Daniel Klein's summary). Be free from hunger, thirst, cold and persistent fear; simple living makes one healthy and unafraid of chance; excess pleasure becomes pain, and immediate gratification sacrifices future selves. Attend to experience, think second-order, and value close relationships as the greatest pleasure: a philosophy of experience rather than consumption.

## Compounding: "Play the long game."

**The model.** Compound interest is interest earning interest, a snowball. Compounding follows a power law, and knowledge, experience and relationships compound too. "Most of the gains come at the end, not at the beginning," and only if you keep reinvesting the returns. Naval Ravikant: play iterated games; all returns in life come from compound interest. (Einstein supposedly called it the eighth wonder of the world; he probably didn't.) A *debt spiral* is compounding on the other side of the equation. We think linearly and struggle to picture exponential functions, so the model forces long-term thinking: "All that matters is making continuous progress, no matter how small." C. S. Lewis's warning runs the other way: a trivial indulgence can be the loss of a bridgehead.

**Moves.**
- **Early decisions compound more than later ones.** A graduate who starts in a job disconnected from their interests finds it harder to switch later; consider how consequences multiply.
- **You don't always know the payoff.** You can estimate a future account balance but not what you'll do with it (a different career, bigger risks). Investments in learning and relationships open opportunities you can't yet imagine; invest in things that benefit you now, and seize the unanticipated opportunities when they come.
- **Reinvest experience.** Experience compounds only if you build on skills in new situations; otherwise "twenty years of living becomes the same year repeated twenty times." A journalism degree doesn't compound into a Pulitzer by itself; be deliberate about how past experience serves future success.
- **Compound relationships** through win-win dynamics. *Preferential attachment*: the likelier a node is to gain a connection, the more connections it already has, so things accrue to those who have more. Starting conditions matter (Michael Mauboussin: each extra point of unemployment at graduation means 6 to 7% lower career earnings) but needn't define you. *Cumulative advantage*: grab one tiny advantage, extract every benefit from it, repeat. Relationships are reciprocal: give as much as possible before taking.

**Limit.** Where starting conditions count for more than capacity and effort, fewer people get to compound, and the first foot in the door is the hardest part.

**Cases.**
- *Jewish education* (Botticini and Eckstein, *The Chosen Few*). A first-century-CE ordinance required fathers to send sons to school from age six or seven to read the Torah: at first a sacrifice with no economic return in farming economies, valued for its spiritual benefit. Within centuries literacy, together with numeracy, written contracts, account books and institutions for enforcing contracts, moved Jews into crafts, trade and moneylending and into cities, a comparative advantage won by being first. Knowledge invested for present benefit compounded into exceptional later opportunity.
- *Mireya Mayor.* The National Geographic explorer, who in 2008 retraced Henry Morton Stanley's search for Livingstone with 1865-era gear, reinvested every experience. Her first expedition, to Guyana in 1996 (a teddy-bear backpack, trendy boots, a sleeping bag she couldn't use, tweezers that proved invaluable), made her an expert minimalist packer; performing as a Miami Dolphins cheerleader before 75,000 people taught her to perform under pressure; relationships built studying lemurs in Madagascar led to work as a wildlife correspondent. "I was putting in the legwork."
- *Sidney Weinberg* left school at 13, one of eleven children of a Brooklyn liquor dealer, and started at Goldman Sachs around 15 as a janitor's assistant. A delivery to Paul Sachs got him into the mailroom, which he reorganized, then a banking course and mentoring; he became a salesperson, a trader and, eventually, CEO for 39 years. He sat on more than 30 boards and attended over 250 meetings a year, befriending CEOs by being helpful, backed FDR when Wall Street wouldn't (which led to an advisory board of executives who became clients), advised five presidents while declining political posts, and sent $100 a week to a struggling rival: friendship before business.

Compounding works "not by chance, but by the steady, patient accumulation of efforts," turning the ordinary into the extraordinary one tiny gain at a time.

## Supporting Idea: Network Effects

A product's utility grows as more people use it (the telephone). *Indirect* network effects run across groups (more drivers attract more riders). It's a reinforcing feedback loop: hard to start, since it needs critical mass, then a strong advantage over entrants even with better products. First movers aren't always the winners (later movers learn). It tends toward winner-takes-all, users are reluctant to switch, and growth comes without marketing. It is the most important factor behind many of the significant technologies of the past two centuries, often a matter of luck, and appears outside tech too (clusters of skilled workers; gold as a store of value). **Negative network effects** are its limit: overload (trains too crowded, with no room for more lines), or so many users that the thing's nature changes (an online forum's norms diluted).

## Sampling: "Your samples become your reality."

**The model.** Taking a few sips to judge the wine: studying a small, manageable bunch to learn about the whole. Done poorly, it yields wrong conclusions. It is a first principle of statistics, behind measurement, risk and reward, and telling luck from skill. Morgan Housel: your personal experience with money is a vanishingly small fraction of what has happened in the world, yet it makes up most of how you think the world works.

**Terms.** *Population* (the whole), *sample* (the part studied) and *census* (measuring everyone). The *law of large numbers*: the larger the sample, the closer it converges on the true value (roll a die 6, 600, 6,000, 60,000 times). Small samples skew: the swans on your local pond are all white, and the rare one-legged swan gets missed, and repeated small samples from the same pond still mislead. A larger sample gives a lower margin of error and a higher confidence level. There are trade-offs: small studies show whether further research is worthwhile, meta-analyses combine them, ethics limits distressing studies, and effect size and dropout ratio matter. **Random sampling** gives every member an equal chance of selection: George Gallup said one spoonful tells you about the soup, if it's well stirred.

**Sampling bias.** A large sample is accurate only if representative. The *healthy worker effect*: a study of a chemical's effects that looks only at current employees misses those too sick to work.

**Anecdotes.** We overweight anecdotes from people close to us and from good stories, especially those that confirm our beliefs. "An anecdote is a sample size of one." The exception: one result can show something is possible (the first heart-transplant survivor).

**Not all samples are equal.** Insights are only as good as the range a sample covers (airbag safety data gathered only on male drivers). WEIRD samples (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) are outliers among humans, and adding more California three-year-olds won't fix that. Caroline Criado Perez: "When your Big Data is corrupted by big silences, the truths you get are half-truths, at best." Phones too big for women's hands, pain studies on male mice, drug trials dosed for a 200-pound man, ethnic differences in metabolism skipped because diversity is costly. Collection mechanisms bias too: over 90% of unwanted sexual behavior on New York and London transit goes unreported, so police reports mislead. Volume can't overcome unacknowledged bias: Big Data "can magnify and accelerate already-existing discriminations," and "deep data on a homogeneous population is relevant only to that population." This matters because "data determines how resources are allocated." Also study the subsets whose features averages hide.

**Moves.**
- Widen your own sample of the world: travel, live somewhere big, meet diverse people (tolerance); read across disciplines (open-mindedness); learn your industry's history (awareness of rarer, extreme possibilities).
- Recognize when one measurement isn't enough. Use the smallest statistically significant sample your budget allows, balancing precision against cost, and look for the bias that could corrupt it. "The next time you hear a statistic, think about the sample size."

**Sidebar: insurance.** Insurance spreads the cost of adverse events: "Individual risks are uncertain; aggregate risks are predictable," given a large and uniform population. The 1906 San Francisco earthquake (over $6.3 billion in today's money; about fourteen insurers bankrupted; losses equal to 47 years of industry profit) and the $31.6 billion of 9/11, after which government stepped in to back terrorism insurance, show where the uniformity breaks.

**Case: the Oxford English Dictionary.** Begun in 1856 to record every English word with a quotation of its first use, it relied on volunteers reading all of English literature. A word like "take" has many senses (remove, hold, a film take, on the take), and the editors had to find every use and confirm there were no more. Over six million slips later, it was finished on December 31, 1927: 12 volumes, 414,835 words, 1,827,306 quotations.

## Randomness: "Predictability is often an illusion."

**The model.** People are poor at comprehending true randomness; we see order and patterns (the world is out to get us), and predictability is often an illusion. Leonard Mlodinow: the mind is built to find a definite cause for each event. Random means "without definite aim, reason, or pattern," and "randomness is the rule, not the exception." We misunderstand it whenever we attribute causality where none exists. Shown random dots, we draw lines. Work with randomness, not against it, and confront the lack of control. "The history of ideas is a history of gradually discarding the assumption that it's all about us." The past was as random as the future; hindsight orders it into narrative, and which documents survive is itself random, more incomplete the further back you go.

**Randomness as a tool.** Immune lymphocytes form random shapes to match unknown invaders; ants forage randomly and coordinate through pheromone trails without central control. Probabilistic elements let a small population explore an enormous space of possibilities. Embracing randomness makes you less predictable and more creative, and Christian and Griffiths note that deliberately using chance can be the best approach to the hardest problems.

**The gambler's fallacy**: believing what happened in the last round affects the next. Six heads in a row is as probable as any other specific sequence. In 1913 at Monte Carlo, black came up 26 times in a row, and gamblers lost millions betting on red.

**Two perspectives on randomness** (sidebar). *Pseudorandomness* is the appearance of randomness from our inability to detect a pattern, though causes exist; *true randomness* follows a probability distribution detached from any cause. Our narrative habits and suggestibility (recency) make people pseudorandom, and exploitable. The 1950s and '60s mentalist Chan Canasta relied on chance yet also subtly influenced volunteers' "random" choices. Common "random" picks: carrot (broccoli after George H. W. Bush), square, rose, 3 from 1 to 5, 7 from 1 to 10, blue, chair. True randomness is hard to generate (radioactive decay, atmospheric noise, lava lamps). Divination produced it: Naskapi foragers in Labrador heated caribou shoulder bones and read the cracks as a hunting map, so the caribou couldn't anticipate their routes.

**Case: serendipity and creating.** Writers are asked where they get their ideas, as if there were an idea bank. Jane Smiley's answer is to "get on with it": half-attentive rumination while changing diapers, baking bread, driving, writing unplanned scenes and rethinking them the next day, "willingly entered a zone of randomness"; the "only standard of achievement" is the accumulation of pages. Her remedy for being stuck: "find out more—read more, travel to the spot," since "if you are bored with your subject, it is fatal to try to think your way out of it": experience your way out. If you're stuck, everything you currently think can't help; go stumble into new things. You can't master creativity, but introducing randomness helps you make new connections.

Humans are terrible at recognizing randomness: we see patterns where there are none, give coincidence meaning, and think we can beat the odds.

## Supporting Idea: Pareto Principle

In 1906 Vilfredo Pareto observed that 20% of Italians owned 80% of the land (he had seen a similar split in his pea plants). In the 1940s Joseph Juran found 80% of defects came from 20% of production issues and named the principle: in systems, about 80% of outputs come from 20% of inputs (publications and researchers, words in a language, healthcare users, customers and profits, your own results). It's a rule of thumb, not a law of nature, though the true split is often surprisingly close. Since inputs and outputs aren't evenly distributed, it tells you where to focus: if 80% of users touch 20% of a program's features, make those excellent. Hans Rosling: a few items matter more than all the others put together.

## Regression to the Mean: "Moderate outcomes follow extreme ones."

**The model.** Because luck is random, an outlier result with a luck component will probably be followed by a more moderate one; data far above or below the mean tends to be followed by data close to it (exam scores normalize over iterations). Francis Galton found it in the heights of parents and children. From a single result you can't separate luck from skill (an athlete's bad game, a warm winter day, a hot investment). "Failure or success is usually followed by a result closer to the mean, not the other extreme." The larger luck's role in an extreme event, the less likely it is to repeat. (General Thinking covers the statistics under Causation vs. Correlation.)

**Moves.**
- After a success, ask how much was skill and how much luck (were competitors making poor choices? will stronger competition pull you back to average?). The model shows where an individual result sits on the spectrum of possibility.
- Success sows the seeds of its own destruction: overconfidence, more opportunities than you can handle, complacency, greed. The skills to become successful differ from the skills to stay successful; when things go badly, get back to basics.
- With repeated iterations, results converge on true ability, so don't overweight initial outcomes. Beginner's luck is real because beginners who fail spectacularly quit. Keep trying: most results will be average, but lots of average results don't preclude the occasional exceptional outlier (a weekly blog post kept up for years).
- "The best way to assess someone's abilities is to consider their track record, not their greatest achievements."
- When new to something, find where the mean lies, so you know whether early results are representative. "An extreme event is not necessarily the start of a new trend." History is too complex for a simple statistic, but the model puts extremes in perspective.

**Cases.**
- *The Sports Illustrated curse* (sidebar). Athletes reach the cover at a peak of skill plus luck, then regress, while injuries accumulate with play; Cy Young winners tend to decline. Outside sports: after a terrible company culture, the next is likely normal; after an amazing relationship, the next is likely less extraordinary.
- *The Ford Edsel.* There is always an average, and pressure to knock it out of the park makes average look like failure. After the Thunderbird's success, Ford named a car for Henry Ford's son and ran a two-year campaign that never showed it, promising the greatest car ever made, building to "E-day" in 1957. Its vertical grille looked like a grimacing mouth, it had technical problems, and it was stopped within two years at a cost put at about $2 billion today. "The Edsel was just a car": an average car that overhype guaranteed would fall short. Judged against the Thunderbird and the Mustang it seems a colossal failure, but release enough cars and some will be average; the disappointment lay in the contrast with the marketing.
- *The Trung sisters.* In 39 to 43 CE, Trung Trac and Trung Nhi led a revolt against Han rule (in place since 111 BCE) after Thi Sach was executed for protesting taxes, raising an army of eighty thousand with women generals and elephants, driving the Chinese from sixty-five cities, and ruling for three years (abolishing taxes, reviving the language). Their success owed something to the Chinese being poorly prepared; the Chinese then rebounded to their mean and retook the territory in 43 CE.

The model says "not so fast": enjoy the extraordinary, but it probably won't last.

## Multiplying by Zero: "The ultimate destroyer."

**The model.** Look for the zero: the weak part that threatens to bring down the whole. "Any multiplicative system is only as strong as its weakest link." In a multiplicative system there's no point optimizing other components while ignoring the zero; invest in the weakest part, because nothing compensates for it. One complaining team member, a CEO's racist comments, a security system with the front door open. Paul Lockhart: multiplying by zero destroys information, with no reverse process. The value lies in finding where a zero might be, avoiding introducing one, and transforming an existing one. In a restaurant, bad food is the zero: great food compensates for slow service, not the reverse.

**Finding zeros.** A zero creates a mirage: big numbers elsewhere seem to compensate. But "zeros don't hide"; they're obvious when you step back to see the whole. We deliberately ignore them, hoping they'll fix themselves, often because they are persistent structural flaws that intimidate us, and fixing one would mean admitting failure. Avoiding them leaves us open to snake-oil fixes. Success has many factors, but often one thing determines failure.

**Transforming zeros.** A zero doesn't become a one overnight, but every zero among required components can be turned into a one, and doing so capitalizes all the other strong numbers. The goal is often not to remove the zero entirely but to shift it just enough to become a one, activating the rest of the equation.

**Cases.**
- *East German technology theft* (Kristie Macrakis, *Seduced by Secrets*). Work environments are multiplicative (sales, marketing, R&D and finance depend on each other). East Germany wanted a self-sufficient computer industry, but its system punished creativity, innovation and collaboration. Under embargo, the Stasi stole blueprints and hardware and spent billions of marks reverse-engineering them; a planned factory for tens of millions of 256K memory chips never materialized, and scientists weren't allowed to travel. Innovation comes from failing and learning why things work, which lets you troubleshoot and adapt: "A scientific establishment based on pirated and cloned technology can never be a leader." Stolen machines didn't work and had no repairman; the Stasi's "cult of secrecy clashed with the scientific ethos of openness." The zero was the lack of collaboration and of knowledge earned from experience, so more spies and more money changed nothing. It needed people with earned knowledge and a culture that let them exist (inventors who ask questions and challenge the status quo).
- *Crop diversity* (sidebar). When consequences are high, avoid multiplying by zero. Dependence on a single crop invites disease, parasites, weather and soil depletion; diversity means multiple equations, so one zeroed doesn't negate the others. In the Irish potato famine of 1845 a fungus destroyed the main food of more than a third of the population, for up to five years, because the potatoes were clones with no genetic diversity.
- *Stuttering.* A perceived personal zero can undermine every other capability, often through a sense of inadequacy others impose. Stuttering is a disconnect between idea and expression, not intelligence, worse with excitement, fatigue or stress, managed rather than cured. James Earl Jones, George VI, Carly Simon, Winston Churchill, Marilyn Monroe and Emily Blunt (helped by acting lessons) all stuttered. B. B. King found fluency in singing ("I talk through music"); Rubin "Hurricane" Carter, who started fighting after being mocked, copied singing's fluidity, practiced cadences and spoke before groups. Zeros can form us and push us to develop new skills.

"When you multiply by zero everything else becomes irrelevant."

## Equivalence: "Equal doesn't mean same."

**The model.** Things don't have to be the same to be equal (5 kilometers and 5,000,000 millimeters). There are many paths to success. In math, if A = B and B = C, then C = A: different symbols, equal in at least one respect. The model helps when the traditional solution is no longer viable and you want an equivalent result another way, and it helps you look past apparent differences to the underlying equality of human experience and connect with others.
- *Human universals* (Donald Brown): taboo language, distinguishing behavior under one's control from behavior that isn't, promises, inheritance rules, attempts to predict and influence the weather, bodily adornment: different manifestations, equivalent purposes.
- *Historical recurrence*: the Lincoln and Kennedy assassinations; Napoleon and Hitler invading Russia. People in similar situations facing similar incentives behave similarly (George Eliot: history repeats itself with a slight change of costume).

**Multiple discoveries** (sidebar). The solitary genius crying "Eureka!" is a myth; most discoveries are cumulative, and several people often reach equivalent results independently (steel, slingshots, the abacus). Kevin Ashton: inventions come in steps, not leaps, so they appear in several places at once. Darwin and Wallace on natural selection; Scheele finding oxygen in 1772 (publicized three years later) alongside Priestley and Lavoisier; du Hauron (pigments) and Cros (dyes) inventing color photography in the 1860s; Nettie Stevens and Edmund Beecher Wilson on the X and Y chromosomes; Kajita and McDonald sharing the 2015 Nobel for neutrino mass; Taqī al-Dīn's steam turbine in 1551 Syria before England's 1698 patent. Patent law misunderstands this (it presumes nonobviousness, and the first to file profits). The results are equivalent though not identical, applying the same principle to the same problem; credit goes to whoever popularizes it, and female and minority scientists get overlooked.

**Cases.**
- *Madeleine Vionnet and the bias cut.* With corsets falling away and World War I restricting material, Vionnet saw more than one way to make a body look good: let its movement shape the fabric, with no boning, rubber or elastic. Fabric stretches little when pulled at the middle of an edge and a lot at the corners, so in 1922 she cut cloth at 45 degrees to the vertical stress (the *bias cut*), making it contract laterally and cling. J. E. Gordon: there are more ways to get a fit than strings and hooks. The dresses "moved like water," used less fabric and became a staple.
- *How we deal with the universal of death.* The need is universal and the processing varied: sitting with the body in Jewish tradition, the Hindu pyre, gatherings with food, processions, Tibetan Buddhists leaving remains on a mountain, South Koreans turning ashes into beads, somber or lavish. Herodotus told of Darius asking Greeks whether they'd eat their dead fathers and Callatiae whether they'd burn theirs; each was horrified by the other's custom. Funeral rituals are scripts for making sense of death, and "all societies see death as a transition." Their intent is equivalent: consoling the living and dealing with the dead. "None are the same, but all are equal in the ways they help people."

**Moves and limits.** Equivalence is the art of making things interchangeable, swapping without changing essence (a red Lego brick for a blue one): different inputs, identical results, and more than one way to solve most problems. It simplifies complex systems and allows change without breaking fundamental structure. Not everything is interchangeable (a car engine and a hamster wheel); the art is knowing where equivalence applies and telling essential differences from superficial ones. Look for underlying patterns and for components you can swap or simplify.

## Supporting Idea: Order of Magnitude

Brains struggle with very large and very small numbers (primitive counting systems sometimes go from one and two to "many"), yet science is measurement, from a cell's weight to a galaxy's size. Orders of magnitude are compact notation: one order larger is 10 times, one smaller a tenth; negative orders cover values between 0 and 1. They give comparisons context (Earth versus a car), and they suit imprecise numbers and estimation. A crowd of twenty and a crowd of a million are different things; at a dollar a second, a million dollars takes just over eleven days and a billion about thirty-two years, three orders of magnitude apart. The Richter scale (Charles Richter and Beno Gutenberg, for Southern California) runs from 0 to 10 (no 10 has been recorded); each step means 10 times the ground motion and about 32 times the energy. Chile's 1960 quake was 9.5; each year brings about 1.3 million quakes of 2 to 2.9 and about one of 8 or more. Richter's point: precision was neither expected nor required, only a way to sort large, moderate and small shocks from instrument readings, free of personal estimates.

## Surface Area: "Know your exposure."

**The model.** Surface area is how much of something is in contact with, and able to react to, the outside world (a teaspoon of sugar versus a cube; the paper needed to wrap a gift). The model shows when increasing exposure helps and when it causes problems: enlarge it for exposure to new ideas, shrink it for protection. In chemistry, more surface area means a faster reaction (powder versus lumps, kindling versus logs). In biology, lungs and intestines have enormous surface areas, cold-climate animals have a lower ratio of surface area to volume, and we curl up when cold. Dependencies and assumptions are surface area: code with little surface area ages well and is robust; a project depending on ten teams is less likely to finish on time. Different situations require different surface areas.

**The surface area of a life** (sidebar, in Shane Parrish's voice). The larger your surface area, the more energy it takes to maintain. A second house expands it more than linearly; so do more friends (time is finite), more wealth (assets to track) and more beliefs. Hiring help (assistants, property managers, family offices) scales the surface area of responsibility while masking it behind abstraction. More to defend and maintain leaves you anxious, stressed and behind, craving focus, and the expansion becomes identity ("the busy person," the man with four houses). Competition drives expansion that benefits the group and makes individuals unhappy. The happiest people he knows keep a small surface area: billionaires with two houses, four or five close friends, productive people focused on one or two things. It takes less work to keep surface area small than to maintain a large one.

**Cases.**
- *Circus schools.* To solve a creativity problem, increase your surface area of exposure to other disciplines. The traditional circus (big tent, ringmaster) was passed down in families who "*lived* them" (Duncan Wall), but it was a closed system: "beholden to tradition, each generation mindlessly duplicated the work of the last"; technical ability rose while the art stagnated into "cheap uniformity," marketed to children, and declined. After the Russian revolution the state rebuilt the circus with a school modeled on ballet schools, teaching philosophy, physics, math and chemistry, bringing in artists from other disciplines and running circus "labs"; by the 1950s and '60s the Soviet circus soared (a hundred million tickets a year). France's national circus school culminates in original work, teaching students to *create*, not just *perform*. Circus creativity exploded in the past fifty years (Cirque du Soleil earns billions). A multidisciplinary education is increased surface area, giving more creative reactions.
- *Guerrilla warfare.* Reducing surface area makes you less vulnerable to influence, manipulation and attack; in security, keep it as small as possible without compromising function. Network security counts the opportunities for unauthorized access (employees with access, connection points as attack vectors), and perfect security is impossible. Medieval castles had slit windows and a few guarded entrances, so defense could concentrate. A small surface area is also offensive: guerrillas use small autonomous units, hold no territory and present no fixed targets, "the resort of the weak against the strong" (Max Boot), dispersed bands never forming "a front, flank, or rear" (Robert Greene). Castro began against Batista with about twenty men, had 180 by April 1958 and 220 in his biggest operation that August, against thousands, attacking isolated units, communications and supply lines from mobile mountain bases; Batista fell on January 1, 1959. T. E. Lawrence: "a thing intangible, invulnerable, without front or back."
- *When you can't tell the whole truth.* Maps reduce conceptual surface area to "a chosen aspect of reality." Mark Monmonier: "A good map tells a multitude of little white lies"; it "must omit details that would confuse or distract." Harry Beck, an electrical draftsman who drew circuit diagrams, designed the 1933 London Underground map as an outsider, against the publicity department's derision: colored lines, station circles, no scale, near-equal spacing, a grid with 45-degree intersections, abandoned stations left off. A trial print was loved. When communicating, reduce surface area to what's useful: get the traveler to Piccadilly Circus.

## Global and Local Maxima: "Embrace the peaks and valleys."

**The model.** Maxima and minima are the largest and smallest values over a domain. There is one *global maximum*; smaller peaks are *local maxima* (the highest mountain versus the hills in your neighborhood). Maxima occur at a critical point of change. The model helps you know whether you've hit your peak or there's potential higher, and shows that sometimes you must go down to go back up. Christian and Griffiths: you may need to temporarily worsen a solution to keep searching. "Hill climbing" searches a landscape of hills and valleys, and the challenge is knowing whether you're on the highest peak; even when things go well, you're often just at a local maximum. At a local maximum, things are as good as they get with the current structure. A higher hill requires walking through a valley, back to being a neophyte, or stepping back, broadening your view and checking your direction (Dave Rael: chasms are opportunities to find a new slope).

**Optimization** (sidebar). To find the lowest point in your hometown, a basketball rolls to the local minimum on your street. A giant ball a quarter mile across rolls over houses toward the global minimum but is too big to settle into it; then switch back to the basketball. Lessons: "make the big changes first, before we try to optimize the details"; watch directionality, since the ball's roll tells you which way is down; local maxima and minima are traps, so step back and make a bigger jump (a bigger ball). Know how and when to optimize, and avoid overoptimizing.

**Cases.**
- *The sports bra.* In 1977 runner Lisa Lindahl and Polly Smith designed the first sports bra (straps that don't dig, seams that don't rub, minimal movement); the prototype wasn't scalable, and with Hinda Schreiber they built Jogbra, facing every question of production, sales, logistics and marketing. Her rule: "always about learning, gaining information, then accruing the knowledge to apply it correctly." She sold it in sporting-goods stores as equipment, not lingerie, in the Title IX era, persuading male sales reps it was as essential as a jockstrap, and it was profitable in its first full year: a local maximum. Each new skill meant going back down a hill (bad at marketing before good). Mistakes followed in names, colors, styles and in not staying a one-product company (a failed Jogmit, a successful men's line); "if you can improve, you know you haven't reached your global maximum." She eventually sold to Playtex. What you thought was the pinnacle was "only the floor of your next level." She also made her epilepsy "a one instead of being a zero."
- *Queen.* With no function to compute for subjective states, analyze whether you've gone as far as possible within your parameters or must regroup and change scale. In a band the members are the giant balls and the chords the basketballs: no point changing a chord if the guitarist doesn't fit, so get the people right first, then image and style. Each future member of Queen had reached a local maximum in earlier bands: John Deacon's long, "extraordinarily thorough" musical apprenticeship; Roger Taylor leading a band at seventeen; Brian May learning songwriting, harmonies and stagecraft in bands with Tim Staffell, then Smile (May, Taylor, Staffell), whose record deal went nowhere; Freddie Mercury (born Farrokh Bulsara) with Ibex and Sour Milk Sea. Changing members is rolling the big ball: a group's emergent properties make its sound unpredictable. May, Taylor and Mercury came together in 1970 and went through three bassists before Deacon; only then did they refine element by element, soliciting friends' criticism and incorporating feedback.

Progress isn't a steady climb but peaks and valleys; going down to climb higher means short-term sacrifice for the long game. It's easy to find a pretty good solution and stop looking. Don't settle for the first peak; keep exploring.
