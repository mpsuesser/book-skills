# Economics

Models from economics. The authors hold that "economics is as much art as it is science": its core models are timeless principles rather than laws of nature, and much economic behavior rests on the stories we tell ourselves and the stories our culture tells us. Benjamin Graham: "Price is what you pay; value is what you get." Ha-Joon Chang: knowing the key principles lets you make robust judgments without the technical details. These models spark solutions even where "not one cent is in play." Each entry gives the model, its moves, its limits and its cases.

## Scarcity: "When resources are finite."

**The model.** Economics exists because scarce resources must be allocated to meet limitless needs; every resource is scarce in the sense of finite (one pizza, a hundred people). Societies develop by overcoming kinds of scarcity, with technology increasing access or decreasing requirements, and scarcity forces creativity and invention (raise supply, cut demand). Scarcity pushes prices up, but economic value requires something to be both scarce *and* desirable: scarce but unwanted, or desirable but plentiful, means low value. Some things are valuable purely for being scarce, as signals of wealth.

**How it bends judgment.** Perceived scarcity impairs decisions by triggering self-preservation: we get fenced in by limits, real or not. Temporary scarcity of essentials shapes behavior long after it ends (frugality after poverty or the Great Depression). Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir: immediate scarcity looms large and crowds out unrelated important things, and the omissions add up; constant calculation is mentally taxing and leaves less attention for everything else.

**Abundance has its own consequences.** Plenty is historically new and not always good (refined sugar): we struggle to stop consuming a formerly scarce resource. When one resource becomes abundant, another becomes the bottleneck (money versus the time to spend it), so moderating one may be how you realize another. Abundance usually raises consumption (rice in Asia, bread in Italy, coconuts in Costa Rica); expected shortage triggers hoarding (toilet paper early in COVID).

**Scarcity perpetuates itself.** There's an incentive to maintain scarcity even amid abundance. Diamonds, now even lab-grown: "They're not selling diamonds; they're selling the story." Luxury runs on controlled scarcity: Hermès makes fewer than 100,000 Birkin bags a year and asks buyers to wait months or show a purchase history, and the scarcer it is, the more we want it.

**Moves.**
- Use scarcity as a tool for second-order thinking: if I get more or less of something, what is the result? With more money or time, ask "Now that I have more money or time, what am I going to do with it?" and "If I do those things, then how does my life change?" Lottery winners get richer, not happier (lost privacy, exploitation, being valued for their money, disputes over fairness). Lifestyle creep follows the cues of each economic rung; a change in one part of a system generates changes in another.
- Scarcity can motivate (rare skills, scarce jobs).
- Make scarcity work for you: a rare combination of ordinary qualities (honest, hardworking, smart) is rewarded disproportionately.
- Recognize when scarcity is driving your choices, and ask whether those choices align with your true values; sometimes the value is real, sometimes "a mirage, a trick of the mind." Businesses with a scarcity mentality remove shock absorbers and run so lean that disruption breaks them, and a culture of scarcity triggers hoarding.

**Case: reducing the scarcity of information (the printing press).** Before print, scribes copied scarce texts onto parchment, errors accumulated (older copies were likelier to be accurate), no index of all works existed, and knowledge passed orally on a need-to-know basis. Print made transmission efficient (Elizabeth Eisenstein), with ripples no inventor could foresee:
1. Texts could be compared side by side, so inconsistencies spurred investigation, new combinations of ideas and new systems of thought.
2. New products appeared: job-printing for advertising, propaganda, sedition and red tape; propaganda needs mass replication, so organizations formed to use it.
3. Educational independence: an "avalanche" of how-to books undermined the guilds and apprenticeship, pressured teachers and raised competition.
4. More people created information: correspondent networks, solicited corrections, early crowdsourcing and the very notion of an edition, a feedback loop that reduced scarcity further.
5. Most important, preservation: knowledge was preserved by making it public rather than secret, which was central to early modern science and the Enlightenment.
The lesson: "The more fundamental the scarcity being reduced, the higher the likelihood of widespread impact." Time and money are the most fundamental. Identify such reductions and plan to manage the changes they bring. Herbert Simon: a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention.

## Supply and Demand: "Scarcity drives price."

**The model.** How people allocate scarce resources by setting prices: when demand exceeds supply, prices rise until demand drops; when supply exceeds demand, prices fall (a new game console versus old phone cases). The system is "actively stable," never really at equilibrium but dynamic. Its complementary interests (one wants to buy, one to sell) and competing ones (low price versus high) make an economic win-win, vulnerable to interference.

**What the curves leave out.**
- The textbook curves assume no one can influence price; in reality both sides can be manipulated. Large firms affect supply, and total supply differs from available supply (*artificial scarcity* in luxury goods). Preferences are easily influenced (Eric Beinhocker), and marketing creates demand; Josh Kaufman's advice is to discover what people already want and present an offer that meets that pre-existing desire.
- Substitutes (orange or apple juice, or water) create instability, and new businesses challenge incumbents. Joan Robinson and Edward Chamberlin's *imperfect competition*: boot brands compete through marketing, and monopoly risk is limited by makers targeting slightly different audiences.
- We rarely demand what we don't know is possible (faster horses). Joel Mokyr: technology is invented "if not randomly then in a highly unpredictable fashion," so demand is not a major factor in invention.
- Beinhocker: things with low entropy have value, because they are scarce and need energy, materials and information to make. We overvalue human and machine labor and undervalue the energy it takes to grow a tree or an elephant or an ecosystem.
- Supply isn't fixed: rising demand enlarges the pool (votes, doctors, degrees). Central control of production is impossible and would limit innovation.
- Demand isn't binary but a matter of how much (one coffee, or seven macchiatos).

**Cases.**
- *Never underestimate the power of demand (sex work).* Where there is demand, there is supply. Law can effectively target only supply; you can't legislate that people stop wanting something, so bans create underground markets (illegal drugs, jeans in communist Eastern Europe, banned treatments). Regulation of sex work has been mostly supply-side: zoning and stigma in medieval London, which pushed it outside the walls (Kate Lister), walled districts in seventeenth-century Japan, state brothels in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century France that screened the workers and not the clients, so disease spread. Bans were brutal (noses cut off in Bologna in 1259; forced retraining in China after 1949) and drove it underground with fewer rights. Demand-side attempts (British Army examinations of soldiers; American wartime condom and safe-sex campaigns) were unpopular and fell back to the supply side. No attempt to abolish or control it has worked; criminalization breeds exploitation. Supply is created to meet demand, so regulating one side alone rarely works.
- *Out of thin air (GM's model year).* Thorstein Veblen: "Invention is the mother of necessity." Ford's Model T was mass-produced, black only from 1914 to 1925, its design unchanged. Alfred Sloan at GM targeted existing owners instead, inventing the *car model year*: a new look every year, because "restyling a car was cheaper than reengineering it," with the same mechanics underneath. New models had to be distinctive yet continuous (you had to recognize the neighbor's new Cadillac), and Harley Earl passed Cadillac innovations down to cheaper makes, so status came from trading in. The three-year cycle ran new model, facelift, restyled fenders, deck and hood. "Our big job is to hasten obsolescence": average ownership fell from five years to two after 1934. GM created demand without adding real value, shifting the question from "Does it still work?" to "Does it still look good?" It may have cost market share when GM, committed to chrome and fins, was surprised by American Motors' and Volkswagen's small cars, and critics asked whether manipulated demand could grow forever on finite resources.

**Moves.** Economic cycles are driven by human nature: profits breed overconfidence, greed and complacency; their absence breeds fear, saving and ruthless efficiency. Supply and demand also decide allocation, who gets what: excess demand encourages substitutes and more supply, and excess supply discourages investment. We are the market, active agents in the store, the salary negotiation and the product launch.

## Supporting Idea: Game Theory

Economic activity consists of individual actors' decisions and their effects on others. Game theory mathematically models how agents' decisions lead to outcomes, focusing on interactions and payouts, and applies in biology, military strategy and psychology. A single model of a game yields insight into many real situations where people seek the best payout. Games aren't predictive; they show the mathematical underpinnings of decisions stripped of distracting detail. (See the Prisoner's Dilemma and tit for tat under Feedback Loops, and coordination games under Interdependence.)

## Optimization: "Perfecting the possible."

**The model.** Making the best use of resources (time, money) for the biggest benefit with the least waste, like packing a suitcase. Economics assumes people "do the best for themselves given how much money they have and how much things cost." Everyone has a different *optimization formula*, because values differ, and no one optimizes in only one dimension (whether an hour is worth spending to save a dollar is subjective; firms optimize profit along with employee satisfaction and reputation). Jon Bentley: the most important optimization is doing the right thing; a close second is doing the thing right.

**Optimization is dynamic.** The economy is an evolutionary system, not an equilibrium, so you don't reach the optimum and stop; you keep experimenting and adjusting, always at some risk, and you're not choosing from a fixed menu. Short-term and long-term optimization require different paths. Individual criteria change over time (a fancy car now, a retirement home later) and with kids, careers, recessions, pandemics and wars; in a novel situation you can't know the optimum, so experiment with several formulas. Optimization involves trade-offs 100 percent of the time.

**Limits.**
- **Overfitting to an environment.** If evolution optimizes, why do species go extinct? An insect that drinks from only one flower dies when climate kills the flower: optimization becomes a liability when the environment changes. Companies corner a market and then can't adapt; keeping options open diverts resources from immediate needs. Beinhocker calls it the tension between "executing for today versus adapting for tomorrow."
- **Choice presupposes choices.** Culture, laws and norms limit options. Nancy Folbre: real choices are more complex than maximizing utility with perfect information, fixed preferences and sovereign self-interest.
- **Measurement.** "Just because you can measure something doesn't mean you need to optimize it. Some of the most valuable things in life are intangible."
- Optimization works until it doesn't (a student who never shows their work). Know when to use it, when to let it go, and when to avoid it.

**The key move.** "Beware of judging others for making suboptimal decisions if you don't know what they're optimizing for."

**Utility** (sidebar). Utility is the underlying reason for a person's choice (Jeremy Bentham's term, from his calculus of pleasure and pain). It is what makes something worth paying for (Marx: nothing can have value without being an object of utility), and it varies by person. Edison: "Its sale is proof of utility, and utility is success." *Marginal utility* is the value of one more unit; it diminishes at some point ("Diminishing marginal utility keeps consumers from consuming an infinite quantity of donuts," in Beinhocker's line), and sometimes jumps or reverses at a critical point (water saves a thirsty man, then can eventually kill him). Someone may choose tea over coffee for price, the first sip or the social ritual, and society and marketing shape utility (the intellectual tea-drinker stereotype). The criticism is that the concept is too broad: Julie Nelson notes it makes the utility of children comparable to ice cream, and not everything that satisfies wants has, or can have, a price.

**Cases.**
- *The foundations for optimization.* Beinhocker: "In evolutionary systems, history matters." You can optimize only from your foundation (a service worker can't be a carpenter tomorrow). When vertebrates left the ocean, where water is about eight hundred times denser than air, they adapted what they had: fewer, sturdier bones, necks, tear ducts and eyelids, and a jaw-supporting bone (the hyomandibula) turned into a sound transducer, nothing you'd design from scratch. Nintendo began in 1889 making playing cards, moved into toys through its card distribution, and under Hiroshi Yamauchi tried rice, taxis and "love hotels" with no success, until it used its card-and-toy-shop network for arcade and handheld games, entered the US in the early 1980s with *Donkey Kong* (built by rewiring unsold machines; Mario's first appearance) and then the NES. Its educational games failed, constrained by its foundation in games as entertainment, with consumers performing natural selection. Foundations are both burden and opportunity, and building on what you have is one way to optimize.
- *The tools of optimization (clock time).* Optimization needs measurement, "we tend to optimize only what we can measure," and the tools shape what we optimize for. In 507 CE Theodoric's water-clock tower in Verona was meant to let people "distinguish the various hours of the day and thus decide how best to occupy every moment" (David Rooney). Clock time became a feedback mechanism for productivity, with an unplanned effect: "we started buying into the idea that time could be wasted." Benjamin Franklin wrote "time is money" in 1748, and saving time became a virtue. Yet clock time didn't standardize time's value (a forest walk is waste or meaning, depending), even as precision gave us GPS, fitness watches and a synchronized world.

## Trade-offs: "The cost of a benefit."

**The model.** "Everything is a trade-off": any situation where making a choice means forgoing some other benefit or opportunity. Investors trade risk and reward, doctors a drug's benefits and side effects, parents momentary happiness and long-term well-being, a service team hiring speed and training, students study and a job. Unassembled furniture is cheaper but costs time and risk (your thumb, the last missing screw). Trade-offs rest on what you value most (time-rich or money-rich). Russ Roberts: making a choice means giving something up. The optimal path is the one with the greatest net present value, all things seen or foreseen. The opportunity cost of your time should rise every year, so trading time for money gets more expensive with age.

**Opportunity cost** (sidebar). Every yes is a no to something else. Opportunity cost is the potential benefit of the best forgone alternative, an unseen price tag, immediate or future, sometimes nebulous (satisfaction, love). "We see what we choose but not what we lose." It's "the ghost of the path not taken" (Frost's "The Road Not Taken"), like a coffee chain launching a fizzy iced coffee instead of improving what it has. Evaluate against the best possible alternative; some things are far more expensive than they look. Opportunity cost and trade-offs are two sides of the same coin.

**How trade-offs behave.**
- Changing trade-offs drive large-scale change (as women's employment rose, the opportunity cost of childcare rose and birth rates fell).
- People who seem to do everything have hidden trade-offs. "Those who know how to make trade-offs can get so much more out of life than those who try to get everything." "To get really great at a few things, you have to accept being mediocre at a lot more" (the dog pleading for you to throw the toy it won't let go of).
- Trade-offs surface over the long term (a chocolate bar every day) and, in complex adaptive systems, optimizing one area exacts a price elsewhere (steroids and organ damage; a fancy house masking debt). Paul Dolan: we allocate attention at odds with what gives us purpose and pleasure.
- **Slack** (Mullainathan and Shafir): enough of a resource to absorb unexpected demand. More slack makes trade-offs less critical (the $10 latte doesn't cost dinner) and licenses buying things with only some appeal; abundance affords the luxury of packing poorly, not thinking and not minding mistakes.
- True trade-offs can't be planned away: they are evidence that it's difficult or impossible to increase one thing without decreasing another (donuts and cholesterol). They can't always be resolved with more time or money, they aren't all known in advance, and their value is subjective, so another person might choose differently. You can't have your cake and eat it too.

**Limit: analysis paralysis.** Don't weigh trade-offs so extensively you can't act. They're hard to identify and quantify, highly contextual and not two-dimensional; rarely "A or B" but "if this, then not that, so then this other thing..."

**Cases.**
- *Buildings.* Who is it for, what is its purpose, how must it function now and later? Durability trades against cost: too weak and it breaks, built "forever" and it's too heavy and expensive (J. E. Gordon), which is why a wood-frame house is affordable now. "There are no 'optimal' solutions applicable to all projects." Cheaper to build can mean expensive to maintain. Adaptability can't be fully planned: over 2,500 years the Parthenon was a temple to Athena, a church, a mosque and a munitions store. Trade-offs give us variety: if a building could have everything, all buildings would be the same, instead of skyscrapers, stone churches, log cabins and geodesic domes. Embrace them.
- *Migration* (voluntary). It's never a win on every front; something is always lost (home, career status, the people who share your memories). Migrants weigh the new place's potential against what they leave, often for their children and grandchildren, under uncertain payoffs, and often enter the labor market at the bottom (engineers driving taxis in New York, doctors delivering in Toronto). A six-country European study found migrants in Southern Europe had lower unemployment but less qualified jobs, and in northwestern Europe more qualified jobs but higher unemployment risk. Mazin Sidahmed: "In a search for prosperity, we sacrificed stability." Kay Iguh: "in building a future, you dismantle a past." The optimal choice is neither obvious to outsiders nor the same for everyone.

"Wisdom is anticipating the consequences of your choices." The goal is not having it all but having what matters most: know your opportunity costs (fast loses features, cheap loses quality, high pay costs hours), and choose thoughtfully and intentionally.

## Specialization: "Focus is key."

**The model.** Division of labor: a person, group or nation focusing on one good or service to produce it in less time, at higher quality and lower cost. It works at the micro level (individuals) and macro level (nations) and always involves a trade-off. Adam Smith's pin factory: one untrained worker might make one pin a day, certainly not twenty; ten specialized workers made 48,000. Specialization and development feed each other, as do specialization and trade (Beinhocker: trade enables specialization). Count how many objects you touch that you made yourself. Critics call specialization boring and alienating from one's products and others' labor; on balance it maximized the effectiveness of resources and, unevenly, raised living standards over two hundred years. Munger: avoid doing anything someone else at Berkshire can do better. "If you try to do everything, it's hard to do anything well."

**Costs.** Solving problems creates new ones.
- Coordination costs grow with the number of complementary specialists (Gary Becker), from a kitchen to a multinational, and specialization breeds interdependence, a benefit (cooperation) and a cost (vulnerability, cascading failures, social gaps that destabilize).
- It requires trust, and among strangers, legal frameworks and enforcement.
- **Overspecialization leads to poor nonsense detection.** Specialists erect barriers to protect their specialty (expensive licenses) and have an incentive to exaggerate its distinctness. Becker: modern expertise comes at the expense of narrowness and ignorance of what others do.
- A specialty can become obsolete.

**Moves.** Do what you do best and work with others for the rest, mindful of your vulnerabilities, and don't put yourself where you can't adapt. The more you specialize, the more other fields can teach you: "the most exciting finds often happen at the edges between areas of knowledge." "Specialize without getting stuck."

**Comparative advantage** (sidebar). *Ricardo's principle*: as long as one party has a higher opportunity cost for producing something, it makes sense for each to specialize and trade, even if one party is better at everything (David Ricardo's argument for unrestricted trade). Limits: Raúl Prebisch showed that trade between countries of very different wealth can trap the poorer one in primary products whose "terms of trade" worsen against the cars it buys; the remedy was to diversify and protect homegrown industries while incentivizing international competitiveness (the early US, Japan, South Korea). Nancy Folbre: Ricardo was correct but incomplete, leaving out military power (Britain prohibiting handloom weaving in India; battleships forcing the opium trade on China). It yields mutual prosperity only when advantages aren't exploited. Beinhocker: "All competitive advantage is temporary."

**Cases.**
- *Computers to programmers.* Specialization is path-dependent (a new medical specialty grows from doctors, not lawyers). You can predict that specialization will occur but not where the lines will fall; at the front end of a disruptive technology the unknowns create opportunities and the first arrivals shape development, so let specialization grow bottom-up rather than predefining roles. In the 1940s women worked as human "computers." When ENIAC (about 18,000 vacuum tubes) was built at the University of Pennsylvania, John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert Jr. did the hardware, and the line between hardware and software was blurry, even nonexistent (Claire Evans). Six former human computers, Kathleen McNulty, Betty Jean Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Frances Bilas and Ruth Lichterman, programmed it with no instructions, standing inside the machine among hundreds of cables and some three thousand switches; Grace Hopper later built standards and the first compilers. "Those who write the software make all the difference too." The past experience of early workers draws the lines; allow the evolutionary process.
- *The best teams (hockey).* Specialization requires trust. Forwards, defense and goalie; wingers and defensemen placed by shooting side, size and chemistry; and fluid on-ice specialization where one player chases the puck and four anticipate. A goalie can't win alone. In the 1987 Canada Cup, Mario Lemieux scored off Wayne Gretzky's pass because Larry Murphy drove to the net and Dale Hawerchuk took out a backchecker. Know your role and what teammates expect of you, execute it flawlessly and trust the others: "Trust, teamwork, accountability, sacrifice." Hayley Wickenheiser, a five-time Olympic medalist, focused on offense rather than trying to be expert at everything, and in business hires "people smarter than me ... and I trust them." Gretzky: "everyone has a role."

## Interdependence: "Mutual reliance, intertwined fates."

**The model.** Specialization leads to interdependence: the more specialized you are, the more you rely on other specialists, in webs where everyone needs everyone to win. Kurt Lewin: an "interdependence of fate" is what makes a group. Leonard Read's "I, Pencil" (1958): no one knows how to make a pencil from start to finish. Benefits: opportunities, a disincentive to war, lower costs (a chicken sandwich), and diversity of jobs.

**Downsides.**
1. Dependencies can be manipulated: you tolerate bad behavior and bow to demands.
2. Breakdowns spread: the smallest part can shut down an industry. The Chūetsu offshore earthquake (July 16, 2007) damaged Riken's piston-ring plant, and with just-in-time inventory, Japanese auto production stopped. "When everything works, interdependence works well."
3. Cascades (Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu, 1950s): when 1930s oil fields drove prices down, homes switched to oil heat, coal-mining jobs fell, steel prices rose, people drove more, railways declined and highways grew, and towns boomed or died.

**Moves.**
- Appreciate the ripples only by being honest about where you truly depend: economics long overlooked unpaid household labor, mostly women's (Alfred Marshall argued against counting it).
- Problems can be so enmeshed they can't be solved separately. Mindful of interdependencies, use them as leverage for mutual benefit, and "be careful upon whom you are dependent in a crisis."
- Drop "the illusion of self-sufficiency. No one is entirely self-made." Interdependence is both vulnerability and strength (alliances, partnerships, families, communities).

**Coordination failures** (sidebar). In a coordination game the best payoff comes when everyone does the same thing, and deviating lowers your own payoff. A *Nash equilibrium* is a state where, given the others' strategies, no player can do better by changing theirs. There can be several Nash equilibria with different payoffs, each working only if everyone adopts it, so if everyone is stuck in a suboptimal one, it's hard to change. Many major problems are coordination failures, solvable only if everyone agrees to do the same thing at the same time, while existing incentives discourage challenging the status quo (which side of the road to drive on, wearing a suit to an interview, nuclear arsenals).

**Case: the Ju/wa and xaro.** Interdependence gives people reason to stay on good terms ("Hurting you will ultimately result in hurting myself"). Elizabeth Marshall Thomas studied the Ju/wa hunter-gatherers of Nyae Nyae, on the Botswana-Namibia border, in the 1950s. Sharing "was perhaps the most important element in the social fabric," reducing anxiety and weaving a social network, and "the goodwill of the group was one's most valuable asset"; marginalization was the fear. In *xaro* gift-giving, gifts traveled hundreds of kilometers, couldn't be refused and had to be reciprocated, later and from the heart rather than as trade, in lifelong partnerships (about fifteen partners each, with three or four months a year spent visiting). It invested over a longer horizon than gathering food and paid in resources, information, social options and less jealousy and ill will. Hunting brought prestige that could become exploitable power, so meat was distributed by the owner of the arrow that killed the animal, not the hunter, and anyone could own arrows. Interdependence can be built deliberately to promote cohesion, not just suffered as a by-product of specialization.

## Efficiency: "Maximum output, minimum waste."

**The model.** The optimal path to your end (the most points in the least time). In economics, an allocation is fully efficient when no resource can be reallocated without someone losing; if you can make one person better off without hurting anyone, it isn't efficient. *Pareto efficiency* (Vilfredo Pareto) means no wasted resources, since waste raises prices and costs; an inefficient allocation is a *market failure*. Perfect efficiency (highest revenue at lowest cost, whoever wants a good most getting it) is an impossible state, so efficiency is a matter of degree. It's not a moral judgment: many distributions qualify, including giving most resources to a few, so it says nothing about fairness or equity (of a hundred oranges, 60 and 30 with ten unsold is inefficient; 99 and 1 is efficient). Ha-Joon Chang: markets weed out inefficiency only when no one can manipulate them.

**How it works.**
- Efficiency isn't static (a drought kills the apple trees, and oranges take their place); maintaining it takes work, and it still ends.
- Markets grow more efficient as information spreads, and they work because a plurality of views cancels anomalous ones. Take care, when making something "more efficient," not to remove a valuable source of information.
- An efficient economy may not meet needs, because there's more profit in some people's wants. Signaling forms a reinforcing loop: Veblen's *conspicuous consumption* diverts energy from needs to display. The model shows where to improve getting people what they need, and what it takes to keep one group's actions from leaving others worse off.

**Veblen goods and Giffen goods** (sidebar). *Veblen goods* become more desirable as their price rises, because the price signals wealth and prestige: you pay for the signal. *Giffen goods* (Sir Robert Giffen) see demand rise with price without being status symbols: when bread, a staple, gets dearer, the poor can afford less of anything better and buy more bread. "In economics, there are always exceptions to any rule."

**Limits.** Efficiency runs into diminishing returns; find the sweet spot before costs outweigh benefits. "Efficiency works until it doesn't. The more perfectly efficient a system, the more vulnerable it becomes to any change." Maximal short-term efficiency rarely yields maximal long-term efficiency, because it erodes the margin of safety: the opportunity cost of spare cash, inventory and people looks too high until a supply shock. "Inefficiency in the short run is often very efficient in the long run." Be prepared for the future, not just optimized for the present. Efficiency is also doing the right things, not just doing things fast: say no to the small stuff.

**Cases.**
- *Keeping everyone happy (Mexican comics).* Sometimes the efficient move is maintaining the status quo: by ensuring no one loses, everyone wins. From about 1940 to 1970 Mexican comic books, melodramas about ordinary Mexicans read by all ages and classes amid urbanization, rising literacy and women entering work, drew opposition from the Catholic Church, champions of family values and national unity, and critics of American cultural imperialism (Anne Rubenstein). The government's commission had no real enforcement powers (fines it couldn't collect, arrests it couldn't compel), so protesters "met with just enough success to prevent them from giving up, but not nearly enough to satisfy them," while powerful publishers and vendors by the millions kept working. No one could be made better off without someone worse off, and thirty years of protest changed little, which preserved an environment where everyone pursued their ends.
- *Equalizing spaces (taverns).* Efficiency requires access to and sharing of information; gossip enables efficient decisions, and common spaces enable protest and political organizing. Ray Oldenburg's *third places* (1989) are neither home nor work: "neutral ground," "a leveler," where conversation is the main activity and people meet on equal footing. Some colonial American towns had a drinking place for every twenty-five men, with fixed drink prices that kept taverns from catering to narrow groups. Taverns were "seedbeds of the rebellion" (Steven Johnson): they didn't cause the Revolution, but change that variable and the path differs. *Common knowledge*, where people know something and know others know it, is what collective action needs, because it lowers individual risk. "If you want to empower people, make it easier for them to share and aggregate information."

## Debt: "Borrowing against the future."

**The model.** Borrowing now, paying back later, whether money, sleep, calories or a debt of gratitude. "The benefits of debt are visible, but its liabilities are often hidden." Interest compensates the lender for the risk of nonrepayment; set too high, the debt grows faster than it can be paid. We value future costs less, so we spend more if we pay later. Borrowers default, sometimes deliberately when an asset is worth less than its debt, and go bankrupt. *Odious debt* is incurred by a regime acting against its nation's interests, debt as an instrument of control. Michael Hudson: governments in antiquity were never chronic debtors. Public debt can build infrastructure whose future revenue exceeds it (banking, transport, defense): the Meiji government borrowed from about 1873 for a central bank, commercial banks, railroads and war, and repaid within thirty years. Harari: credit financed discoveries, discoveries built trust, and trust brought more credit, though possible isn't the same as good. Good debt builds assets with greater later value (a degree).

**The move.** "Debt, as a model, prompts us to consider whether the actions we will have to undertake in the future to compensate for what we do now are worth it." Debt is undesirable when the cost of repayment exceeds the value gained, and present needs blind us to that invisible cost. Consider whether you can afford the cost of making right the wrong actions you take today.

**Limits.** Debt is a tool and a chain, any future obligation (a favor, a social obligation, lost sleep). It makes you fragile, "driving across a huge desert without a spare tire," where the smallest hiccup strands you. The more you borrow, the less room you have to weather uncertainty; it seems cheap now and often costs more; it gives leverage and takes freedom. "Respect its power, but fear its edge."

**Debt forgiveness in ancient societies** (sidebar). Some debt is corrosive. Michael Hudson found widespread debt forgiveness in the Bronze Age Near East (Babylonia, Assyria; records from Lagash, Assur, Isin, Larsa, Babylon). It canceled agrarian debts, not merchants' commercial debts, to keep land and production self-supporting rather than to create equality: harvests failed (drought, flood, pests), and rulers needed a free population for the army, for labor on temples and walls, and to keep creditors and officials from concentrating land and bonded people. "Economic polarization was reversed by canceling debts." The practice faded under Rome, which protected creditors and put moral blame on debtors; Livy and Plutarch held that classical antiquity was destroyed mainly by creditors using interest-bearing debt, with barbarians succeeding only as societies weakened from within.

**Case: land mines as future costs.** Modern mines, from World War II on, are cheap and improvisable, and they help win battles while making wars harder to win. Short-term debt: the adversary uses them too, so all territory must be treated as mined, everyone freezes, and victims need more blood, surgery and resources, overwhelming medical teams. Long-term debt, owed globally for decades or centuries: civilian deaths, restricted humanitarian aid, stunted postwar recovery. Jody Williams (Nobel Peace Prize, 1997): mines violate proportionality and distinction; in Vietnam they "waited in deadly silence." Most postwar victims are civilians (farmers, women at rivers, children), and the costs compound in cleanup, treatment, lost productivity, trauma and stagnation. Maps are rarely made and cleanup is rare; 164 countries signed the treaty. A 2016 estimate put about 110 million antipersonnel mines in about sixty countries, killing or maiming one person every hour.

## Monopoly and Competition: "Monopolistic might, competitive fight."

**The model.** Markets become efficient through competition (buyers seek the lowest price, sellers the highest). A monopoly is a single company controlling a market (the only bridge across the water), able to set higher prices, which is why countries prevent them. *Oligopolies* are more common. In a *winner-takes-all market*, one company takes most of the profit, a few take modest shares and the rest get a minuscule remnant; such markets are hard to disrupt and suppress entry. Emerson: nature hates monopolies.

**When monopoly is positive.** Where upfront investment is huge (sewage, electricity), a *natural monopoly* with steep barriers to entry forms, and governments regulate its prices. Joseph Schumpeter argued temporary monopolies reward risky innovation (pharmaceutical patents).

**Winner-takes-all distributions.** Book sales concentrate in a handful of authors; the top 100 websites draw more than ranks 101 to 999 combined; the same goes for citations and the first Google result. They are self-perpetuating (famous for being famous); Robert Frank notes the winners are often no more special, since positive feedback amplifies tiny initial differences.

**Limits.** "Monopolies interrupt evolution": they leave less room for new things, diversity and resilience. "Monopolies inevitably sow the seeds of their own destruction"; the only question is how long it takes. Competition is the default (firms as price takers, not price makers, efficient and innovating); monopoly is the entrepreneur's dream of becoming the market. We need both: competition keeps firms honest and drives innovation, and a monopoly's deep pockets fund moon shots. Balance them.

**Case: extreme monopoly (totalitarianism).** Monopolies feel threatening because they hold power over you, with barriers so high no one can compete, which is unnatural. Read as a lens on the theory rather than the particulars, totalitarian states (Stalin's Soviet Union, Hitler's Germany) are monopolies over all of life: an "internal invasion by the state of its civil society" (Robert Nisbet), eliminating churches and unions and replacing schools and sports clubs. Carl Friedrich and Zbigniew Brzezinski listed the features: an ideology, a single party led by one man, terroristic police, and monopolies of communications and weapons, with a centrally directed economy. Hannah Arendt described the leader's "absolute monopoly of power and authority." Their techniques were duplicated offices (Hitler's overlapping, conflicting organizations) and random purges (Stalin), terror to "atomize" people, depriving them of meaningful ties so no credible alternative could form. But it goes against how the world works: people can't be stripped of the propensity to create and seek alternatives, and a monopoly on thought is impossible. It sows its own failure: "constant removal, demotion, and promotion makes reliable teamwork impossible and prevents the development of experience," so mistakes repeat and nothing adapts (Stalin's famines; Hitler's ruined economy). Totalitarianism sees the world as a giant machine, but "the earth is not a machine."

## Supporting Idea: Externalities

An externality is a market failure where someone incurs a cost or receives a benefit without being compensated or paying. Externalities can be positive or negative; either way they're inefficient (scarce resources not put to their most useful end), and they're hard to prevent, so they're an accepted part of markets.
- **Fixing spillover costs:** add costs for the party causing them, at the margin, not on average (pay per additional unit). A per-trip charge for driving into a city center beats a flat fee, since a flat fee makes every extra trip free and invites people to get their money's worth.
- **Positive externalities:** vaccines benefit people who can't be vaccinated (those with immune deficiencies) without their paying, which is why vaccines are often free or cheap.

## Creative Destruction: "How the mighty fall."

**The model.** Joseph Schumpeter (*Capitalism, Socialism, and Democracy*): an "industrial mutation" that is "incessantly destroying the old one, incessantly creating a new one. This process of Creative Destruction is the essential fact about capitalism" (also called "Schumpeter's gale"). The authors widen it into an evolutionary process: the market is an ecosystem and firms its organisms, a more efficient newcomer usurps the incumbent, and "whoever survives, wins." Technology is mutation; not every mutation is beneficial or sticks. Schumpeter emphasized the entrepreneur; the updated view is that anyone can produce change. Joel Mokyr: innovation doesn't require invention; it can be borrowing, extending and adapting.

**Its costs.** It leaves chaos: lost jobs, retraining, bankruptcies, hollowed local economies, health and environmental risks, pressure on wages; Schumpeter: you can only reckon its achievements in the long run. History is written by the victors, every leap leaves some behind, and hindsight forgets the unease and suffering. It wipes out existing wealth, which becomes raw material for new forms.

**Moves.**
- "For societies to remain creative, the forces pushing new ideas forward must be stronger than those protecting the established order." "There never was a 'good old days.'" Stopping creative destruction means stopping evolution.
- Let go of entrenched ideas; there are always better ways, and we are always discovering and implementing them. Nothing is sacred (smartphones and flip phones, streaming and rental stores, cars and horses). It prevents stagnation and moves resources to their most productive use, offering both the opportunity to disrupt and the threat of being disrupted. Don't cling too tightly to the old.
- The model also explains resistance: understand people's "natural resistance to change when they don't see themselves as having a place in the new world order" (the Luddites deserve understanding, not pity).
- It's hard to use as a model because its mechanism shows best over long history. Its most useful lesson: new ideas cause fundamental changes in how we live and organize. "Prepare for a world that is going to change and don't be afraid to contribute."

**Cases.**
- *The evolution of the law.* New ways of thinking cause shifts like new technologies do, and the process has no endpoint (Schumpeter feared it would be codified and stop being creative). Groups without laws run on norms; law was born in Near Eastern city-states of non-kin, where rules took legal force when "general, transparent, and prospective" (David Beatty), to settle conflict peacefully, since more people meant more conflict, unrest and a weaker ruler. Hammurabi's code (Babylon, about 1790 to 1750 BCE) was publicly posted and built of examples rather than general rules ("If a man burns down his neighbor's house, then he is burned to death"): guidance, not binding precedent. In Greece, Solon made laws legitimate by the people's agreement, with juries able to overturn magistrates. Uptake is slow (Leonardo's flying machines waited 500 years), and law grew bottom-up into the rule of law, binding rulers too, through the Greeks, sharia, seventeenth-century English jurists and the 1787 American constitution: a "restless legal analysis" mirroring Schumpeter's restless entrepreneurs.
- *The end of "Clovis First."* New ideas meet resistance from the existing order regardless of merit. Thomas Kuhn: *normal science* works within a *paradigm* (Newtonian physics). An *anomaly* is first assumed to be a mistake, then ignored or worked around, since paradigms are always imperfect and there's no reason to question them without a crisis. In a crisis the field focuses on the anomaly, which seems to grow until it's insurmountable; but a replacement is needed before the old paradigm is abandoned, since science can't work without one. Scientific revolutions are gradual, may take centuries, and let two paradigms coexist (Newton and Einstein); early adopters improve the new paradigm, converting all but the holdouts, who die off or leave. In the 1930s bones and points found near Clovis, New Mexico, carbon-dated in the 1950s to 13,500 to 12,900 years ago, made the Clovis people the oldest North Americans: they crossed the Beringia land bridge, walked an ice-free corridor through Alberta down to Chile and killed off the megafauna. It was taught in schools. Anomalies piled up (sites in southern Chile 18,500 to 14,500 years old, possibly 33,000; the Yukon at least 24,000), and the researchers behind them faced attacks on their reputations, marginalization and ostracism. The corridor proved inhospitable, no Clovis remains turned up in it, and the megafauna's extinction predated Clovis. Holders of the old view had invested time and reputation and saw no place for themselves in the new order. "We cannot possibly understand anything if we deny it exists before we even look for it."

## Gresham's Law: "Incentives matter."

**The model.** "Bad money will always drive out good money." When coins of different metal value circulate at the same face value, people spend the cheaper ones and hoard the valuable ones, so the currency fails as a medium of exchange and good coins become a store of metal value, saved or exported. Sir Thomas Gresham, an advisor to Elizabeth I, criticized Henry VIII's debasement of the coinage (40 percent base metals); Henry Dunning Macleod named the law in 1858. Ibn Taymiyyah described the same pattern centuries earlier. At heart it's about the consequences of *asymmetry* where there isn't supposed to be any (legally equal coins treated as unequal), which breaks trust, and human systems run on trust.

**The inverse** (sidebar). Absent legal tender laws, good money drives out bad: when a new state's currency competes with an older, reliable foreign currency, the imported money predominates if the laws are hard to enforce.

**How it spreads.** When supposedly equal values become asymmetrical, the market distorts (with no bad coins on grocery day, you don't shop). Bad behavior gets reinforced in a feedback loop that eventually drives out the good, in any domain with an asymmetry. In lending, bad lending drives out good: borrowers go where restrictions are fewest, standards fall and holdouts get punished (by their share price), as in the 1980s savings and loan crisis and, amplified, 2008. A critical mass of bad behavior without oversight or the right incentives makes good behavior not worthwhile. "If we are hoarding the good coins, we can bet everyone else is."

**Information asymmetry.** George Akerlof's "The Market for 'Lemons'" (1970): used-car buyers can't tell good cars (peaches, worth $1,000) from bad (lemons, $500), so they offer $750 for any car; owners of peaches withdraw them, buyers then pay at most $500, and the market fails as lemons drive out peaches. With too much asymmetry, people stop participating because they can't verify and feel ripped off; the edge is short-term, and in the long run everyone loses. Symmetrical information builds trust.

**Cases.**
- *Kingston Penitentiary, 1971.* The model is about when a situation goes from functioning to nonfunctioning: how asymmetrical can information get before trust breaks down? Canada's most notorious prison was more than 100 years old, damp, 50 percent overcrowded, understaffed and mixing inmates of every severity; the warden had warned in January that it was close to exploding. In April, inmates led by Billy Knight rioted, stressing nonviolence; about thirty protected six guard hostages ("only insurance"), and Knight faced down an early attack on the "undesirables" (sex offenders, informants). But trust eroded through asymmetric information: a citizens' committee mediated without being able to speak for the government, radio and TV carried unvalidated reports, the army surrounding the prison signaled there'd be no peaceful end, and the Solicitor General said on radio he wasn't prepared to negotiate. The information gradient ran from the government (everything) to the committee (some) to the leaders (little) to the rest (nothing). Knight lost credibility, trust collapsed into fights over food and blankets, and undesirables were assaulted and two murdered. It stopped short of total disaster because Barrie MacKenzie, who had protected the guards, and committee member Ron Haggart trusted each other: accurate information flowed, the hostages were released and the inmates surrendered peacefully.
- *Cycling's doping era.* "Everyone is doing it": each of us can become the hoarder of good money, thinking it's the only way to participate. One doper is an individual; all dopers is a system. George Hincapie: it was "not possible to compete at the highest level without them." Tyler Hamilton raced clean at first, then took EPO from team doctors after seeing the "white bags" in 1997 ("I could measure the injustice"; "Unless something changed, I was done"). Testing was predictable and weak: Bernhard Kohl was tested two hundred times, a hundred of them while doping, and caught once. "The rewards were too big, the punishments too mild," clean riders didn't win multiday races, and the practice was so institutionalized that riders didn't feel they'd done wrong. Dr. Michael Ashenden: "If I had been put in their situation, I would do what they did." After the 2012 scandal (titles stripped, sponsorships repaid, years of investigation) the system had a place for good behavior again; in Hamilton's view the sport was far from 100 percent clean but "significantly better and slower." The goal is a culture that tips people away from bad behavior so positive actions remain possible.

"In the short run, bad often drives out good. But in the long run, true value wins out." Preventing it takes constant effort.

## Bubbles: "Don't get caught holding the bag."

**The model.** A bubble is an emergent property of markets, with no single clear cause and rarely deliberate fraud: a price rises enormously, even exponentially, in a short time because buyers expect it to keep rising. A sustained rise isn't a bubble; a bubble must pop. When expectations shift to a drop, everyone rushes to sell, supply soars, demand vanishes and prices plummet. John Kenneth Galbraith noted that the dictionary's words for a bubble (*show*, *scheme*) wrongly imply a deliberate creator.
- Early buyers make fortunes; later entrants get worse terms. The price detaches from reality when people far outside their circle of competence buy, assuming the immediate past is the immediate future: "contagious optimism, seemingly impervious to facts," fear of missing out, gold-rush fever.
- Skeptics discard their own judgment (everyone can't be wrong), and naysayers who join amplify it. The quality of information gleaned from others' behavior worsens into social proof, until it seems irresponsible not to jump on the bandwagon. It's a social phenomenon, like fashion.
- Morgan Housel: "Beware of competing with those who have different time horizons to you."

**Keynesian beauty contests** (sidebar). Speculation is estimating others' estimates of value. Keynes imagined a contest to pick the six most popular faces of a hundred, won by predicting others' picks rather than your own preference. Prices are set by perceptions of others' perceptions, not intrinsic value; "first-order thinking is not enough," and the more levels of second-order thinking you go, the more accurate you get. It contradicts the idea of rational markets and explains bubbles (prices rise because people think others think they'll keep rising), in any asset market, especially ones open to nonprofessionals.

**Lessons.**
- Your assumptions about the future influence your future, and success can sow the seeds of failure. "Making something ubiquitous is not the same as making it indispensable."
- Bubbles aren't only bad: they encourage investment and experimentation (spaghetti thrown at the wall), and a wide spectrum of attempts enables a healthy middle ground. "Innovation and development can be impossible without an early dose of overconfidence." Clifford Geertz: a new idea gets applied to everything, then we learn its limits and settle on a narrower, durable definition. Markets explore new frontiers this way (cars, computers and the internet were once manias), funding infrastructure for future revolutions out of financial wreckage.
- Harness the exuberance without mistaking it for a new persistent state. Whether investing money, time or reputation, ask how reliable the information is and how much you can bet on it. A shaky foundation is a big caution sign until facts solidify it; "structures built on the wishful thinking of others are ephemeral." Stay grounded in value and economic reality rather than story or hype, so you can stand alone as a bubble expands. "All bubbles pop eventually."

**Cases.**
- *Good intentions, bad results (dot-com).* In the late 1990s any "dot-com" company drew money regardless of profit, revenue or business model, on a story of promise, while Warren Buffett was cast as old and out of touch. Buyers counted on a greater fool, until someone was "the one still at the ball when the clock struck midnight." Recognizing bubbles is a skill.
- *A bubble in space (Apollo).* Like Dutch tulip mania, Apollo was, even by NASA's own reckoning, a bubble. After Sputnik (1957) and Yuri Gagarin's flight (April 1961), Kennedy announced the moon goal in May 1961. NASA had clarity of goal, political support, a safety culture, funding and prestige, and political will overrode the public and scientists who saw better returns in robotic exploration. Opinion swung: the press on board in 1962, skeptical in 1963; in 1964 over three quarters wanted to continue or go faster, then "NASA fatigue" set in, with nearly half wanting cuts (Gil Scott-Heron's "Whitey on the Moon"). Spinoffs included cordless vacuums and reflective thermal blankets for marathon runners. The 1969 landing drew some 600 million viewers, a fifth of the world. Then the bubble burst: "one of the most exceptional and costly projects ever undertaken by the United States," whose successors (the shuttle) never sustained the investment, with innovations at a cost disproportionate to returns. Bubbles can foster outsize creativity, but they're unsustainable; watch the bubble and notice when it's about to pop.
