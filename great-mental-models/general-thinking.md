# General thinking concepts

The first volume's models are general tools for walking around a problem "in a three-dimensional way": checking what you know, how you represent it, and how you reason from it. Each entry gives the model, its moves, the limits the authors name, and the cases that carry it.

## The Map is not the Territory: "Reality check."

**The model.** "The map of reality is not reality. Even the best maps are imperfect." A map is a reduction of what it represents. A map with perfect fidelity would stop being a reduction and would be useless (Lewis Carroll's map drawn at a scale of a mile to the mile). A map may also be a snapshot of a moment, showing something that no longer exists. Financial statements, a policy manual, a parenting guide and a performance review are all maps. "Maps are useful to the extent they are explanatory and predictive." Being flawed is no excuse to ignore them.

Alfred Korzybski (1931) put it as: the description of the thing is not the thing; the model is not reality; the abstraction is not the abstracted. His points:
1. A map may have a structure similar or dissimilar to the territory. The London Underground map serves travelers and is useless to drivers: every map has a specific purpose and cannot be everything to everyone.
2. Two similar structures have similar "logical" characteristics (if a map shows Dresden between Paris and Warsaw, so does the land).
3. A map is not the actual territory.
4. An ideal map would contain a map of the map, and so on, a "self-reflexiveness" in which detail becomes overwhelming.

**How it goes wrong ("But my GPS didn't show that cliff").** Trouble starts when our knowledge becomes knowledge of the map rather than the territory. Then we think we have all the answers, write static rules for the map, close off feedback loops, and miss that the terrain has changed. "If the aim becomes simplification rather than understanding we start to make bad decisions." Maps cannot be dogma; when reality changes, the map must change. News is abstraction made by others, and we tend to consume it "as gospel" without the mental work, so we miss when map and territory part. The biggest problems are the risks in the territory the map does not show: "we do not understand a model, map, or reduction unless we understand and respect its limitations."

Elinor Ostrom's two-part warning about maps used in policy: (a) if the territory matches the map in a couple of respects, we assume it matches in all; (b) adherence to the map becomes more important than new information. Constraints assumed fixed for analysis get "taken on faith" as fixed in the real setting. Models "are tools for exploration, not doctrines to force conformity. They are guidebooks, not laws." Their value is the thinking they generate. George Box: "all models are wrong; the practical question is how wrong do they have to be to not be useful." David Hand: models aim to understand the real world, not an abstract mathematical one.

**Three considerations when using any map.**
1. **Reality is the ultimate update.** Update maps from your own experience; good maps are built by "feedback loops created by explorers." Stereotypes are maps: efficient, but they make you forget how much more territory a person contains. Karimeh Abbud's photographs of Palestine's middle class made a new map against the European ethnographic one. The faster the territory changes, the harder for a map to keep up.
2. **Consider the cartographer.** Maps carry the values, standards and limits of their makers. The borders of Syria, Jordan and Iraq drawn after World War I map British and French interests better than local custom. Ask: What was the cartographer trying to achieve? How does that shape what is shown?
3. **Maps can influence territories.** Jane Jacobs showed planners reshaping real neighborhoods to fit their model of the "statistical city": altering reality to fit the map, forcing complexity into the simplification.

**Cases.**
- *Newton and Einstein.* Special relativity (1905) did not void Newtonian mechanics; physicists "carefully delimit" where each map is a useful guide, and enter uncharted territory (the quantum) carefully rather than assume their maps explain everything.
- *Tragedy of the Commons* (Garrett Hardin, sidebar). On a pasture open to all, each herdsman weighs adding one more animal: the gain is nearly +1 (all his), the cost a fraction of 1 (overgrazing is shared), so he adds another, and another. "Freedom in a commons brings ruin to all." Aristotle: what is common to many is taken least care of. Ostrom's work showed the model was too general: it left out how people actually solved commons problems, which is the map-territory error applied to a famous model.
- *Model of management* (sidebar). Frederick Taylor's scientific management (split tasks small, specialize, pay by incentive) worked for a time. Its limits: people who know the model is being used on them may stop responding; competitors adopt it and flatten the field; it may suit only the factory; and people are not automatons with only financial motives.

"In order to think a few steps ahead we must think beyond the map."

## Circle of Competence: "What don't you know?"

**The model.** Picture an inner circle, *what you know*, inside a larger dotted circle, *what you think you know*. "When ego and not competence drives what we undertake, we have blind spots." Knowing what you understand tells you where you have an edge; being honest about what you lack tells you where you are vulnerable and can improve. Thomas Watson: "I'm no genius. I'm smart in spots—but I stay around those spots."

**The Lifer and the Stranger.** The Lifer in a small town knows the lineages, who owes whom, where the bodies are buried. The Stranger, after a few days, thinks he knows it all and carries more risk than he realizes (is this the right land, the best price?). "True knowledge of a complex territory cannot be faked": the Lifer can stump the Stranger, never the reverse. Depth gives *flexibility* (more than one solution to every problem) and *efficiency* (bad choices eliminated fast). Rule of thumb: without at least a few years and a few failures in an area, you cannot count yourself competent there.

**How you know you have one.** You know exactly what you don't know. You decide quickly and fairly accurately. You know what extra information you need and what is unobtainable, and you can tell knowable from unknowable. You anticipate and answer objections. You see many options. You know what can be adjusted and what is invariant. A circle cannot be built quickly ("A little learning is a dangerous thing," as Pope wrote).

**Building and maintaining it.** A circle is never static or to be taken for granted. Three practices:
1. **Curiosity and a desire to learn.** "Learning comes when experience meets reflection." Learning from others' experience is the more productive route.
2. **Monitoring.** Keep an honest track record; most people believe they are better drivers, managers or traders than they are, and self-reporting is unreliable. Keep a precise diary of decisions (a trader's log of trades; a leader's record of results against intentions). Ask: What went wrong? How could I do better?
3. **External feedback.** Use trusted people or a coach. Atul Gawande, already a skilled surgeon, hired a coach and learned where his technique was suboptimal. When defensiveness rises, focus on the result you want.

**Operating outside your circle.**
1. Learn at least the basics, while remembering you are a Stranger: basic information is easy to get and gives unwarranted confidence.
2. Talk to someone whose circle is strong. Research first so you can ask good questions: asking for the answer is being given a fish, while detailed, thoughtful questions teach you to fish. Probe the limits of their circle, and ask how their situation might shape what they tell you.
3. Use a broad grasp of basic mental models to augment your limited understanding.

You will be a Stranger even inside your own profession (HR decisions, a new technology, a new product).

**Incentives inside advice** (sidebar). An advisor's circle is skewed by how they are paid (commissions on financial products). You are not on solid ground until you know the compensation arrangement: what does the advisor stand to gain? A mechanic has an asymmetry of general knowledge and of knowledge of your problem, and an incentive to charge the most that keeps you as a customer; learn a little of the trade as needed and check before any major spend that you are not being bluffed.

**Cases.**
- *Everest and the Sherpas.* Since 1922 climbers outside their circle have relied on Sherpas, who know what visitors don't know they don't know (about 200 bodies remain on the mountain). Tenzing Norgay led the first ascent in 1953 after some twenty years of climbing and trekking and "lots of lucky failures." Sun Tzu: you cannot turn natural advantage to account without local guides.
- *Elizabeth I's Privy Council.* She meant to direct her actions "by good advice and counsel": a small council, blending old and new, of people who were not yes-men and whose varied opinions were debated.
- *Staying in your circle* (sidebar). Buffett: stick to your area of special competence and be very reluctant to stray. Rose Blumkin of Nebraska Furniture Mart understood cash, furniture and real estate, not stocks: she would buy 5,000 end tables at the right price but not GM at 50 cents a share.

Darwin: "Ignorance more often begets confidence than knowledge."

## Supporting Idea: Falsifiability

Karl Popper: a theory is part of empirical science only if it conflicts with possible experiences, and so is in principle falsifiable. If you cannot prove something wrong, you cannot really prove it right. A good theory risks being wrong under stated conditions.

- **The test statement:** "If x happens, it would show demonstrably that theory y is not true." Then design the experiment, physical or thought. Failing to falsify strengthens a theory, much as natural selection strengthens what survives.
- Freud's theories make no specific testable predictions, so they cannot be called true or false.
- **Historicism**, the belief that history has fixed laws or trends driving to inevitable outcomes, is untestable (a "Law of Increasing Technological Complexity"). Calling interpretations "laws" lends unearned authority and immunity to falsifying evidence. "Trend is not destiny": trends depend on conditions, and conditions change. Bertrand Russell's chicken, fed every day, takes a trend for a law until its head is chopped off.
- **The worst case.** We assume the worst that has happened is the worst that can happen, yet every "worst" broke an earlier worst. Prepare for the extremes physics allows, not the record so far.
- For unfalsifiable theories, the best we can do is estimate their probability of being true.

## First Principles Thinking: "Go back to basics."

**The model.** One of the best ways to reverse-engineer complicated situations and unleash creative possibility. Reasoning from first principles separates the underlying facts from the assumptions built on them; what remains are the essentials, and you build new knowledge on those. The tradition runs from Socrates, Plato and Aristotle to Descartes.

First principles are not absolute truths. They are the elements that are non-reducible *in the context of a given situation*: the laws of thermodynamics are first principles for a refrigerator maker, though a physicist might break the second law down further. They change as understanding grows. "First principles are the boundaries that we have to work within in any given situation." Without taking things apart and testing assumptions, you stay bound by what others tell you, "trapped in the way things have always been done," and carry on as if the environment had not changed. "Everything that is not a law of nature is just a shared belief" (money, borders, bitcoin, love).

**Two techniques.**
- **Socratic questioning**, disciplined and systematic, to reveal assumptions and separate knowledge from ignorance:
  1. Clarify your thinking and the origin of your ideas. (Why do I think this? What exactly do I think?)
  2. Challenge assumptions. (How do I know this is true? What if I thought the opposite?)
  3. Look for evidence. (How can I back this up? What are the sources?)
  4. Consider alternative perspectives. (What might others think? How do I know I am correct?)
  5. Examine consequences and implications. (What if I am wrong? What are the consequences if I am?)
  6. Question the original questions. (Why did I think that? Was I correct? What conclusions follow from the reasoning?)
  It curbs reliance on gut feeling and strong emotional reactions.
- **The Five Whys**: ask "why?" repeatedly, as a child does, aiming to land on a *what* or *how*, not on introspection ("Why do I feel like this?"). If the whys end in a falsifiable statement of fact, you have hit a first principle. If they end in "because I said so" or "it just is," you have found an assumption resting on popular opinion, cultural myth or dogma.

Both slow you down at first, and after one or two questions you will often be lost. Faced with your own ignorance, keep going rather than retreat into self-defense.

**Tactics vs principles.** To improve something, understand why it succeeds; otherwise you copy tactics without their rationale. Temple Grandin's curved cattle chute worked, but later research found straight chutes sometimes worked as well. Grandin's answer: the curve is a tactic; the first principle is that reducing stress to the animals is the single most important thing, because stress affects conception, weight and immune systems. "You can change the tactics if you know the principles." The payoff is "moving away from random change and into choices that have a real possibility of success." Harrington Emerson: methods may number a million, but principles are few; the person who grasps principles can select their own methods.

**Cases.**
- *H. pylori.* "The dogma of the sterile stomach" held that bacteria could not live in stomach acid. Pathologist Robin Warren saw bacteria; with gastroenterologist Barry Marshall he spent years challenging the assumption, rejected for decades, until a Nobel Prize in 2005 (the bacterium had appeared in the literature as far back as 1875). "Because I said so" was not enough.
- *Meat.* The first principles of meat are taste, texture, smell and use in cooking, not "once being a part of an animal." The Maillard reaction (sugars with amino acids) gives flavor and smell. Reasoning from those, about thirty laboratories are developing lab-grown meat.

## Thought Experiment: "Imagine the possibilities."

**The model.** "Devices of the imagination used to investigate the nature of things." Their chief value is letting us do in our heads what we cannot do in life, and explore a question from more angles. They help us learn from mistakes, take on the impossible, weigh consequences, re-examine history, and work out what we want and how to get it.

Bet on a one-on-one basketball game: LeBron James against Woody Allen, and you'd bet everything; LeBron against Kevin Durant is hard, and one game would settle nothing. You ran both games in your head.

**Steps.** Thought experiments are not daydreaming; they take the rigor of a real experiment.
1. Ask a question.
2. Conduct background research.
3. Construct a hypothesis.
4. Test it with (thought) experiments.
5. Analyze the outcomes and draw conclusions.
6. Compare to the hypothesis and adjust (new question, and so on).

You can change a variable as often as you like. Run LeBron against Allen 100,000 times and Allen wins only a few (LeBron has a fatal heart attack). Seeing the full spectrum of outcomes shows what you can influence and what you can reasonably expect.

**Three uses.**
1. **Imagining physical impossibilities.** Einstein's elevator: a closed elevator accelerating in space feels the same as one at rest on Earth, so acceleration and gravity are the same, which led to general relativity. Everyday versions: "if money were no object," "if you had all the time in the world" show what you value.
2. **Re-imagining history:** the *historical counterfactual* (if Y had happened instead of X, what would the outcome be?) and *semi-factual* (would the outcome be the same?). Use most caution here. History is a chaotic system where small changes in starting conditions produce very different outcomes, and running counterfactuals is an easy way to mislead yourself. Yet history is "a deck of cards that has been dealt out only one time," and exploring unrealized outcomes helps you factor in rough probabilities. Worked example: Gavrilo Princip shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand on June 28, 1914; on June 30 Austria changed its policy toward Serbia, and Germany offered full support. Refine the question to how the assassination changed Austrian policy; frame hypotheses (no, partial, total effect); imagine Princip with stomach cramps; note he was not a lone wolf ("How many Serbians would have to get the stomach flu?"). "The more scenarios you can imagine where war comes to pass without the assassination, the weaker the case for it being the critical cause."
3. **Intuiting the non-intuitive.** Check intuition by running the experiment deliberately. John Rawls's *veil of ignorance*: design a society without knowing who you will be in it (economic status, ethnicity, talents, interests, gender), and you build it as fairly as possible. Apply it to company policies (hiring, etiquette, parental leave): what would you design if you didn't know your role?

**Cases.**
- *The trolley problem* (sidebar). A runaway trolley will kill five unless diverted onto a spur where it will kill one (Philippa Foot; Judith Jarvis Thomson, who added a doctor who could kill one patient to give five others his organs). Thought experiments let us explore situations that cannot be reproduced without harm.
- *Reduce the role of chance* (sidebar). Buy $100,000 of Google stock, half cash and half margin loan. The stock doubles: $200,000, less the $50,000 owed, triples your money. Genius? Run the "Theoretical World Generator": Google could have fallen 50% (nearly all stocks have) or 90% (the whole NYSE did, 1929 to 1932), triggering a margin call and wiping you out. Was this bet LeBron against Allen, or LeBron against Durant? Across 100,000 runs, how often do you go broke versus triple? Judge the decision regardless of its outcome, recognize luck, and work on the process to reduce the role of chance.

"Thought experiments tell you about the limits of what you know and the limits of what you should attempt."

## Supporting Idea: Necessity and Sufficiency

The mistake is assuming that because some necessary conditions are in place, all the sufficient ones are. The gap is the gap between becoming a published author and becoming J.K. Rowling (a participation certificate versus the winner's trophy). "The gap between what is necessary to succeed and what is sufficient is often luck, chance, or some other factor beyond your direct control." Capital, hard work and intelligence are necessary to build a Fortune 500 company but not sufficient: there is no recipe. In battle, assessing the enemy, a plan, logistics and a flexible strategy are necessary, not sufficient; in sport, physical capability and the means to train. In set terms the necessary set is part of the sufficient set, which is far larger. Without the distinction you get misled by the wrong stories.

## Second-Order Thinking: "What happens next?"

**The model.** *First-order thinking* anticipates immediate results; it "is easy and safe but it's also a way to ensure you get the same results that everyone else gets." *Second-order thinking* is "thinking farther ahead and thinking holistically": actions, their immediate consequences, and the subsequent effects, "the effects of the effects." Ignoring them invites the Law of Unintended Consequences. A good first-order consequence can carry bad second- and third-order ones, and a bad first-order consequence can carry good ones.

Garrett Hardin's First Law of Ecology (1963): "You can never merely do one thing." The denser the web of connections, the farther consequences reach. And "the genie never gets back in the bottle": you cannot delete consequences to get back to the starting conditions.

**Two concepts to hold:** include the second and subsequent effects, and be observant and honest about the web of connections. "How often is short-term gain worth protracted long-term pain?" Second-order thinking does not predict the future; it uses available information to see likely consequences. It is also no excuse to power ahead and wait for the post-mortem.

**Two uses.**
1. **Prioritizing long-term interests over immediate gains.** Ask whether what you do now gets the results you want (the candy: is this what I want my body or life to look like in ten years?), and don't judge solely by the outcome. In 48 BC Cleopatra, 21 and ousted by her co-regent brother, could reconcile with him, seek another country's support, or align with Caesar; every choice displeased someone. She chose Caesar, accepting first-order pain (her brother's plots, angry Egyptians, a months-long siege). The Alexandrian War gave her everything she wanted and cost her little. Delaying gratification saves time later.
2. **Constructing effective arguments.** Arguments land better when you show you've considered the second-order effects and that they are desirable. Mary Wollstonecraft argued for women's rights through their benefits to society (educated women as better partners and mothers, self-supporting, raising smart children).

**Cases.**
- *Cobra bounty.* In colonial Delhi the British paid per dead cobra, so people bred cobras, and the problem grew.
- *Tire traction.* Grippier tires are safer, but the engine works harder: worse mileage, more CO2, more rubber particles.
- *Antibiotics in livestock.* First order: more weight per pound of feed, sold by weight, so more profit. Second order: antibiotic-resistant bacteria in the food chain, foreseeable with a little biology (organisms evolve, and short life cycles adapt fast).
- *The parade* (sidebar, Buffett). Once a few spectators stand on tiptoe, everyone must; no one sees better and all are worse off.
- *Trust* (sidebar). Trust grows from repeated interactions. Grabbing the immediate payoff (unless it's win-win) makes the interaction a one-off; benefits are maximized over time, so weigh effects on others and on your reputation.

**Limit: the slippery slope.** Second-order thinking can turn into paralysis: action A leads to B, C, D, E, F and then disaster. Hardin noted that wedge arguments treat people as if they lacked practical judgment; by that logic you'd forbid vehicles any speed above zero. The first drink might lead to alcoholism, but most of the time it doesn't. Evaluate "the most likely effects and their most likely consequences," the typical results, case by case; worrying about every possible effect means never acting.

Ask the critical question: "And then what?" Thinking in systems means consequences have consequences; consider time, scale and thresholds.

## Probabilistic Thinking: "What are the chances?"

**The model.** "Trying to estimate, using some tools of math and logic, the likelihood of any specific outcome coming to pass." Lightning will or won't strike you, but you can't know until the day is lived, so imperfect information calls for probability. Our evolved heuristics (Kahneman and Tversky) need a consciously added "layer of probability awareness" in complex social systems. Mandelbrot called probability the only tool for mapping the unknown and uncontrollable. Three aspects:

1. **Bayesian thinking (Bayesian updating).** Thomas Bayes's essay on the doctrine of chances was brought to the Royal Society by Richard Price in 1763, after Bayes's death; Laplace developed the theorem. The core: when you learn something new, take into account everything you already know, all relevant prior information (statisticians call it a *base rate*). A headline "Violent Stabbings on the Rise" may mean a 1-in-10,000 chance became 2 in 10,000, against violent crime at its lowest in decades: your safety has barely changed. Diabetes in the US rose steadily from 0.93% in 1958 to 7.4% in 2015, and that steady climb, read against the priors, is a worrying trend. Priors are themselves probability estimates, not binaries; new information that challenges a prior lowers its probability (the *likelihood ratio* or *Bayes factor*), and some priors get replaced completely. Ask: "What are the relevant priors? What might I already know that I can use to better understand the reality of the situation?"
2. **Fat-tailed curves.** A bell curve (normal distribution) has predictable extremes and limited deviation; no man is ten times average height. Fat tails have "no real cap on extreme events": wealth (people 10, 100, 10,000 times richer), terrorism, as opposed to deaths on stairs. With fat tails, the most common outcomes do not represent the average. Ask: "In the next ten years, how many events are possible? How fat is the tail?" Since you can't imagine every tail scenario, position yourself to survive or even benefit from the wildly unpredictable future. "Always be extra mindful of the tails: They might mean everything."
3. **Asymmetries ("metaprobability").** The probability that your probability estimates are any good. Estimates skew optimistic: investors pitch 20% to 40% a year while the US market has averaged about 7% to 8% before fees over the long run; you almost never arrive 20% early through traffic, but often 20% late. Aim for 25% and you will likely land nearer 10%.

**Sidebars.**
- *Conditional probability.* When using past events to predict, mind the conditions: independent events (a coin toss) differ from dependent ones. Someone who chose vanilla three times: were they choosing from 100 flavors, or was chocolate gone? Observe the conditions preceding an event.
- *Orders of magnitude* (Taleb). A small error in measuring the risk of an extreme event can put you off by orders of magnitude (10x, 100x, 1,000x); an "every 1,000 years" event may be likely in any given year.
- *Antifragility* (Taleb). Some things are harmed by volatility, some are neutral, and some benefit, "like a package that wants to be mishandled." Since predicting rare high-impact events is impossible, "It's more efficient to prepare." Two ways: (1) **upside optionality**, seeking situations with good odds of opportunity where the worst case is nothing (go to the cocktail party; you'll never meet anyone sitting at home); (2) **learn to fail properly**: never take a risk that takes you out of the game, and build the resilience to learn from failure and start again, so "you can only fail temporarily." Trial and error carries information that study does not.
- *Insurance companies* are the most probability-acute businesses (they insure models' legs, athletes' arms, a contest's payout). They attend to the important factors, even when not fully predictable, and price accordingly: "You can always say yes to insuring, but the trick is to come up with the right price."

**Case: Vera Atkins and the SOE.** As second in command of the Special Operations Executive's French section, Atkins chose spies by weighting factors (fluent French? confidence? too tied to family? a problem-solver?), a "series of continually updated, educated estimates." "Intelligence is not evidence": weigh the relevance, quality and timeliness of information. Roles were organizers, couriers and wireless operators, whose average life expectancy in France was six weeks; of 400 agents sent, 100 were captured and killed. "Probabilistic thinking can only get you in the ballpark."

The practice: roughly identify what matters, get a sense of the odds, check your assumptions, then decide.

## Supporting Idea: Causation vs. Correlation

We see two things together (correlation), conclude one causes the other (causation), and act on it, succeeding only by luck.

- **The correlation coefficient** runs from −1 to 1 and measures the weight of the factors two things share. Bottled water consumption vs suicide rate: about 0. Celsius vs Fahrenheit: 1, both governed by molecular velocity. Height vs weight: between 0 and 1, since other factors are at play.
- **Reverse it.** Parents' drinking correlates with children's poor grades; perhaps struggling children drive parents to drink. Inverting the relationship helps sort claims.
- **Regression to the mean.** "Whenever correlation is imperfect, extremes will soften over time. The best will always appear to get worse and the worst will appear to get better, regardless of any additional action." Kahneman: depressed children given an energy drink improve, as they would standing on their heads or hugging a cat twenty minutes a day. A control group is expected to improve by regression alone; the question is whether the treated group improves more than regression explains. For individuals or teams with only their own past as benchmark, compare against an industry average, cohort peers or historical rates, none perfect. (Mathematics covers this at length under Regression to the Mean.)

## Inversion: "Change your perspective."

**The model.** Inversion identifies and removes obstacles to success by approaching a situation "from the opposite end of the natural starting point": think backward, start at the end. "Avoiding stupidity is easier than seeking brilliance." Carl Jacobi: "invert, always invert." Two approaches:
1. "Start by assuming that what you're trying to prove is either true or false, then show what else would have to be true."
2. "Instead of aiming directly for your goal, think deeply about what you want to avoid and then see what options are left over."

**Set your assumptions (approach 1).**
- *Hippasus*, a follower of Pythagoras, got nowhere hunting the square root of 2 by dividing whole numbers; he proved instead what it could not be, and found the first irrational number. Atoms and quarks were likewise known first through what their existence would predict.
- *Bernays and Lucky Strike.* Edward Bernays, hired by American Tobacco in the 1920s, asked not "How do I sell more cigarettes to women?" but "if women bought and smoked cigarettes, what else would have to be true?" What would have to change in the world to make smoking desirable and acceptable? Then he made those things true: a campaign against sweets (a cigarette after dinner instead of dessert), slimness promoted through journalists and doctors' testimonials, cigarettes on hotel dessert menus, kitchen cabinets with cigarette compartments, and "torches of freedom" carried in the 1929 Easter Sunday parade. These were "appeals of indirection" that sold new ways of behaving.
- *Sherlock Holmes, "A Scandal in Bohemia"* (sidebar). If Irene Adler has the photograph and plans blackmail, she values it and keeps it where she can grab it in a hurry; so Watson fakes a fire, she goes to it, and its location is confirmed. Start from the logical outcome of your assumptions and validate those.

**What are you trying to avoid? (approach 2).** Ask how you would bring about a terrible outcome, and let that steer you. John Bogle built Vanguard's index funds by asking not how to beat the market but how to help investors minimize losses to fees and poor manager selection. Invert "I want to be rich" into not getting rich but avoiding being poor: cut the behaviors guaranteed to erode wealth (spending more than you earn, paying high interest on debt, not saving early enough to compound).

**Force field analysis** (Kurt Lewin, 1930s):
1. Identify the problem.
2. Define your objective.
3. Identify the forces that support change toward your objective.
4. Identify the forces that impede change toward the objective.
5. Strategize a solution: augment or add forces in step 3, and reduce or eliminate forces in step 4.
Most people stop after step 3; inversion happens between steps 3 and 4. Think what would make the problem worse, then avoid it or remove the conditions that keep it going. Sun Tzu: "He wins his battles by making no mistakes."

**Cases.**
- *Florence Nightingale*, the first woman elected to the Royal Statistical Society (1858), found poor sanitation was the leading cause of death in the Crimean War hospitals (the death rate fell from 23% in the first winter, 1854 to 1855, to 2.5% the next). She pressed statistics toward prevention: not so much "how do we fix this problem," but "how do we stop it from happening in the first place."
- *Marie Van Brittan Brown* (sidebar), a nurse in Queens, asked what would have to change for her to feel safe at home: she needed to see and speak to whoever was at the door. With her husband Albert she filed the first patent for closed-circuit monitoring in 1966 (a camera moving between four holes in the door, a TV monitor inside, a way to talk to the visitor without opening the door, and a choice to let them in or sound an alarm to a neighbor or watchman).

"Simply invert, always invert, when you are stuck."

## Occam's Razor: "Keep it simple."

**The model.** "Simpler explanations are more likely to be true than complicated ones." Base decisions on "the explanation that has the fewest moving parts" instead of spending time disproving complex scenarios. The husband is late because he got held up at work, not a car crash; the son seems to have grown less because he was mismeasured; the toe hurts because the shoe is tight. We tend to assume vast icebergs of meaning beneath the tips we see. William of Ockham: a plurality is not to be posited without necessity. Simpler explanations are easier to falsify, easier to understand and generally more likely to be correct. It is "not an iron law but a tendency and a mind-frame": when two models have equal explanatory power, the simpler one more likely suffices.

**Why simple is more robust.** If an explanation needs 3 things to be true, each 99% likely, it is wrong about 3% of the time; if it needs 30, about 26%, roughly nine times as often. "The simpler explanation is more robust in the face of uncertainty." Hume defaulted to skepticism about miracles: more likely the witness misdescribed it, or it was a common phenomenon not yet understood (Sagan's sunflower following the Sun before phototropism was known). Sagan: "extraordinary claims require extraordinary proof."

**Simplicity buys efficiency.** You cannot chase every plausible theory; the filter keeps you off dead ends, and complexity tends to paper over flaws.
- *Ivanhoe Reservoir*, Los Angeles: chlorine, bromide and sunlight were forming the carcinogen bromate. Instead of a tarp or a retractable dome over ten acres, a biologist proposed "bird balls": three million black plastic balls at about 40 cents each.
- *Bengal tigers* in the Ganges delta killed about sixty villagers in 1989. Tigers attack only when unseen, so villagers wore human-face masks on the backs of their heads (an idea drawn from eye patterns on butterfly wings); for three years no one wearing a mask was attacked.
- *Dark matter.* When Vera Rubin found in the mid-1970s that Andromeda's edges rotate as fast as its center, unseen mass ("dark matter," Fritz Zwicky's 1933 term) was the simplest explanation. It has never been directly detected, and Rubin later felt the longer it went undetected, the likelier a modification of gravity, which would itself need extraordinary proof.
- *Medicine* (sidebar). Flu is likelier than Ebola: "When you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras." A counter to hypochondria, and Bayesian updating in disguise.
- *Leadership* (sidebar). Louis Gerstner at IBM in the early 1990s: "the last thing IBM needs right now is a vision." Serve customers, compete now, focus on already-profitable businesses: simple, tough-minded execution.

**Limit: some things are not simple.** Ponzi schemes (part accident, part designed deceit) and human flight (airflow, lift, drag, combustion in precise combination) are genuinely complex. "Irreducible complexity, like simplicity, is a part of our reality," so the razor cannot be used to create artificial simplicity. Like code, an explanation can be simplified "only to the extent that it can still provide an accurate understanding."

## Hanlon's Razor: "Don't assume the worst."

**The model.** "We should not attribute to malice that which is more easily explained by stupidity." It guards against paranoia and ideology, keeps options open, and prompts the question: is there another reasonable explanation? "The explanation most likely to be right is the one that contains the least amount of intent." Of all possible motives, the ones that take least energy to execute (ignorance, laziness) are likelier than active malice, which takes work.

**Why we assume malice.** A driver who deliberately cuts you off must notice you, judge speeds and time the swerve, risky work; far more likely they didn't see you. The Linda problem (Kahneman and Tversky, 1982): told Linda is 31, single, outspoken, bright, studied philosophy and cares about social justice, most people judge "a feminist and a bank teller" likelier than "a bank teller," though every feminist bank teller is a bank teller (the *fallacy of conjunction*). Vivid, available detail leads us to over-conclude and package unrelated factors, and assuming an offense was intended is the same error. "Always assuming malice puts you at the center of everyone else's world." For every act of malice there is far more ignorance, stupidity and laziness.

**Moves.** Practice it diligently as a counter to confirmation bias. Assuming someone is out to get you puts you on the defensive and narrows your vision to the perceived threat; dropping the assumption reveals realistic options.

**Cases.**
- *Honorius and Stilicho*, 408 AD. Stilicho persuaded the Senate to pay off Alaric; Emperor Honorius assumed he wanted the throne and had him executed. Two years later Alaric sacked Rome, the first barbarian to do so in nearly eight centuries.
- *Vasili Arkhipov*, October 27, 1962. US forces dropped practice depth charges to force Soviet submarine B-59 to surface, but Soviet command had not passed on the signal. The captain wanted to fire the nuclear torpedo, which required all three senior officers to agree; Arkhipov refused and insisted on surfacing to contact Moscow.
- *The Devil Fallacy* (sidebar, Heinlein's "Logic of Empire," 1941): "You have attributed conditions to villainy that simply result from stupidity."

There are fewer true villains than you suppose; people make mistakes and fall into laziness, bad thinking and bad incentives.
