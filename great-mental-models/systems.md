# Systems

Models for anything with interacting parts: a body, a household, a company, a city, an ecosystem. The authors hold that systems models apply more literally than most, since so much of life is part of some system. Peter Senge's frame: systems thinking means seeing wholes, interrelationships rather than things, and patterns of change rather than snapshots. Donella Meadows: follow a system wherever it leads, across disciplinary lines. Each entry gives the model, its moves, its limits and its cases.

## Feedback Loops: "Listen and incorporate."

**The model.** *Feedback* is information communicated in response to an action: a formal review, body language, a tone of voice. A *feedback loop* is when a system's output also acts as its input, refining it over time, as in a conversation. Technically, the outputs (information) of a system affect that system's behavior, from one source or many interconnected ones. Incentives are feedback. Get upset at criticism, and colleagues will tell you only good things. The test the authors pose: "The world offers us feedback, but do we listen and incorporate, or do we just keep wanting it to work differently than it does?"

**Two types.**
- **Balancing** (negative) loops tend toward equilibrium (a thermostat). They are more common and more sustainable.
- **Reinforcing** (positive) loops amplify (fashion trends, poverty). Breaking one takes outside intervention, a change in conditions, or burnout. "Any system with an unchecked reinforcing feedback loop is ultimately unsustainable and destructive."

**Delays and strength.** In complex systems feedback is rarely immediate, and the delay obscures cause and effect. Maladaptive behaviors can give positive short-term feedback and negative long-term feedback: "immediate feedback isn't the only feedback" (junk food now, type 2 diabetes later). Faster useful feedback means faster iteration, but feedback that is too fast and strong makes the system surge (a learner driver jerking between gas and brake).

**Moves.**
- Learn to filter: feedback is everywhere, and not all of it is useful. Decide which loops to pay attention to and adjust.
- Give feedback kindly and clearly, hear it without defensiveness, and partner with people who can receive it.
- Begin by noticing the feedback you give and respond to. Decide where to direct system changes and at what pace to monitor them. "Without feedback, a system just does the same thing over and over."

**The legal system as a lens** (Ward Farnsworth, *The Legal Analyst*), four aspects of managing loops:
1. **Create the right future incentives.** Avoid a choice today that starts a negative reinforcing loop: paying kidnappers gives them reason to kidnap again. Courts ask "what incentives people will have after the case is over" and rule to make the suffering less likely later (a government isn't liable for refusing a ransom). Copyright: once books and music exist there's a case for free distribution, "but then they are less likely to exist at all next time." Unfairness in a specific case can create fairness on the whole.
2. **Influence behavior at the margins.** People change at different intensities and rates, by increments, in shades of gray: "a little less along one dimension and a little more along another." You can nudge without forbidding the opposite choice (tax sugary drinks, limit where or to whom they're sold). Margins are often where reinforcing loops start: after a price rise the newest customers leave first, sales drop, reinvestment falls, and more leave. *Marginal deterrence* scales penalties so there is always something more to fear from doing a little worse (if theft carried the death penalty, a thief would have no reason not to kill). Larger systems have more margins, and balancing one can start a reinforcing loop elsewhere (sugary drinks pushed into homes, normalized for children). Meadows: a system can cause its own behavior.
3. **Deal with information cascades.** When people decide by watching others, they converge (a selling stampede, virality): a reinforcing loop, which happens because we rarely have perfect information. "People draw inferences from what they see others doing"; restaurateurs seat the first diners by the window. "Ignorance and uncertainty are the best soil for a cascade," and the consensus is fragile, easily disrupted by shocks. Remedies for damaging cascades (speeding, piracy, insider trading): public disclosure, and prosecuting high-profile cases (Al Capone for tax evasion), sending signals "stronger than the ones people get by watching each other."
4. **Build trust.** "Law often amounts to a substitute for trust in situations too complex or dispersed for trust to arise." In the Prisoner's Dilemma, both cooperating earns a year each, one defecting earns him nothing and the other five years, both defecting two years each. In a one-off game you are always better off defecting; in an iterated game trust is worth the investment, and reputation is the feedback. *Tit for tat* (Robert Axelrod): cooperate first, then do what the other did last round, creating a loop that signals you can be trusted. Contracts "give everyone a convenient way to beat prisoner's dilemmas," as do rules and penalties for common stocks (taxes, fishing quotas). Without cooperation a negative reinforcing loop sets in, so prevent the first defection.

**Cases.**
- *Adam Smith*, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*. Others' approval and disapproval are feedback; we'd lose more sleep over a lost little finger than over the deaths of distant strangers, yet the loop of others' reactions is what civilization rests on (Russ Roberts). Norms form this way (fail to say hello and see the reaction). A person raised without others would have no awareness of their own character.
- *Kandinsky's Painting with White Border* took twenty sketches over five months, each one or two steps from the last; the finished work was the twenty-first iteration. Success stories hide the iterations.

## Equilibrium: "Dynamic balance."

**The model.** A stable state where forces are in balance and nothing changes until disturbed; a system that strays too far falls apart. *Static equilibrium* is rare. *Dynamic equilibrium* is a dance within a range, self-correcting through feedback loops: components change, but at least one variable stays within a specified range. The lens shows where to intervene to promote equilibrium, with a caution: in complex systems, anticipating what's needed is exceptionally hard.

**Homeostasis** (Walter Cannon's term, *The Wisdom of the Body*, 1932): an organism's continual adjustments toward ideal conditions (blood glucose, temperature, sodium). Antonio Damasio's sidebar view: homeostasis keeps life in a range "conducive to flourishing," countering the drift into disorder by maintaining order "at a new level." Feelings are a feedback loop about how we're doing; after a disaster, homeostasis returns a system to where it "feels good" under the new conditions, not to its previous state. How a system defines "feeling good" shapes its ability to adapt. People can change the state they aim for.

**Properties.**
- There are *multiple equilibriums*, and being at equilibrium doesn't mean functioning as well as possible (an inefficient equilibrium: working extra hours while ill).
- Short-term deviations are often needed to maintain long-term equilibrium (a sibling argument that clears the air).
- Adding a commitment means resetting the equilibrium: a household that adds piano lessons cuts dining out; a new dog means more cleaning.
- We talk as if we'll reach a steady state ("once X thing happens, we'll be in Y state"), but things are always in flux. "The endless adjustments are our lives." Equilibrium is double-edged: stability and stagnation. Strive for it where needed, and know when to break free into the disequilibrium that drives progress.

**Exploiting assumptions** (sidebar). Depending on one particular equilibrium makes you vulnerable; functioning across a wider range makes you versatile, especially with processes to recover. Competitively, ask what your opponent expects and considers normal, and rethink what the equilibrium in your field is. The magician Ralph Hull's "Tuned Deck" had no single secret: he switched methods whenever the audience started catching on, breaking the assumption that a named trick is done the same way every time.

**Cases.**
- *Shared decision making.* Maintaining homeostasis requires information. Doctor and patient have unbalanced power and knowledge, yet the patient bears the consequences; involving the patient minimizes blind spots and bias and improves outcomes (less anxiety, quicker recovery). *Shared decision making (SDM)*, described in a 1997 paper, decreases the informational and power asymmetry, keeping the information variable in range. Raw information isn't enough; trust is needed too (a 2014 neonatal-intensive-care analysis found parents' dissatisfaction came from their needs going unacknowledged, down to staff using the baby's name).
- *Biosphere 2.* Near Tucson, a sealed glass structure some 27.7 meters tall held five ecosystems (coastal fog desert, rainforest, savanna, mangrove wetland, ocean); in 1991 eight Biospherians were sealed in for two years. Oxygen sank and had to be brought in, calories ran short, and the crew fell out. It wasn't a failure: "Experiments aren't meant to 'succeed.' They're meant to provide us with data," and thinking they knew everything was hubris. Ecosystems are complex adaptive systems with countless feedback loops; in an artificial one, humans must maintain every loop and learn to sense problems before they happen. "When left alone in their typical conditions, systems are pretty good at reaching equilibrium. But when we try to control them ... it takes a lot of effort," and small misguided interventions cascade (Linda Leigh found the bats needed some twenty moths each night, but the air handlers killed moths, and screening them cost electricity). About 30% of species went extinct, against predictions of up to 70%.

## Bottlenecks: "The limiting factor."

**The model.** The slowest part of a system limits its output. A constraint can stifle, or serve as a strategic choke point that compels efficiency and innovation. People who can't delegate are bottlenecks. Bottlenecks create waste as resources pile up in front of them, and information that arrives on time goes stale waiting. The bottleneck is the point under most strain and most likely to break. "Focusing on anything besides the bottleneck is a waste of time"; it only adds pressure there. Liebig's law of the minimum pictures it as a barrel whose yield spills at the shortest stave.

**Distinctions.**
- **Bottleneck vs constraint.** A bottleneck can be alleviated; a constraint is a fundamental limitation (a machine that breaks versus the 24 hours in a day).
- **Masqueraders, or false dependencies.** "I won't do X before Y" (you'll start writing every day once you move and have a dedicated desk) is often a way to feel good about procrastinating when you are the bottleneck; you can still find a way (the library).
- **Validate** that it is really the limiting factor, or you solve the wrong problem.

**Moves.**
- Every system has a bottleneck; remove one and another becomes the limit. Anticipate and plan for it, and use it as impetus: add more of the same, or rethink that part.
- Avoid opening one bottleneck only to create worse ones later; if you must, put them where they disrupt least. Some bottlenecks are better to have, being easier to organize around.
- "Speed up the slowest part, and you speed up the whole system." The *theory of constraints*: find the bottleneck and focus all effort on it; don't optimize the fast parts.
- Fix root causes, which makes many bottlenecks disappear at once; simulate conditions to find bottlenecks ahead; ask how the system could be designed not to have the problem. It is a never-ending job to build into planning.
- "Be intentional about your bottlenecks." A deliberate bottleneck (airport security) focuses effort and maintains quality; an accidental one is drag. "Bottlenecks are the leverage points."

**Cases.**
- *The Trans-Siberian Railway*, 9,458 km from Saint Petersburg to Vladivostok. "Part of solving a bottleneck is anticipating the consequences," and bottlenecks move around systems (T. R. Reid's textile mill saves fifteen cents a spool on cheaper thread and loses $150 an hour to breakages). The railway faced scarce local supplies, brutal seasons, a central committee whose weeks-long delays bred uncoordinated short-term fixes, the world's deepest lake (crossed by ferry for years), and five sections built at once competing for the same labor. Money poured to unsupervised contractors without tendering; spending on labor left too little for materials, so builders skimped (narrow embankments, thin ballast, poor drainage, steep grades, tight curves), and each section needed rebuilding as soon as it was done. Russia effectively built the railroad several times. "Spending money without quality assurance only moves problems into the future," money for the labor shortage gave incentives to keep the shortage going, and "throwing money at the problem without understanding the system is unlikely to yield the intended benefits."
- *Bottlenecks and innovation.* Shortages, especially in war, prompt alternatives. Nylon replaced silk from Japan (stockings, then parachutes). Waldo Semon's synthetic rubber Ameripol, almost a million tons of it, kept the Allies in tires. The Civil War pushed prosthetics and ligatures, cutting infection mortality from 60% to 3%; World War II pushed penicillin production. World War I rickets led Kurt Huldschinsky to treat children with ultraviolet light, the vitamin D behind today's sun lamps.

## Scale: "Bigger or smaller = different."

**The model.** Systems change as they scale up or down; "size influences function." Scaling is rarely multiplication: double the dough and you don't get double the bread, because the geometry changes fermentation. The micro can mislead about the macro and vice versa. More connections mean more potential blockages, so pair scale with bottlenecks: imagine some areas scaling faster than others to anticipate breakages. Staying small can be strategic. Geoffrey West: scaling from small to large moves from simplicity to complexity while the building blocks are conserved.

**Moves.**
- A garage startup and a 600-person company are different systems (HR, teams). Systems scale safely by dividing into parts, provided the links remain, but teams never communicate like individuals, and keeping information flowing takes more work.
- Ask how the system will fare in a year, ten, a hundred: how will it age? Resilience comes from independence between parts. "Macro dependencies tend to age poorly because they rely on every one of their micro dependencies aging well."
- Neither up nor down is intrinsically better; the right scale depends on goals and context. A cake for four and a cake for four hundred need different processes. Ask how it will work at 10x, 100x, 1,000x customers, re-engineer as you grow, and build with scale in mind.

**Economies of scale** (sidebar). Marginal cost shrinks with bulk purchasing and growing demand, until a point where there are no more savings or customers, or finite resources run out (energy, raw material, computing power).

**On being the right size** (sidebar, J. B. S. Haldane, 1928). A species can't grow much bigger without changing shape: a gazelle scaled up becomes short and thick like a rhino or spindly like a giraffe. Drop a mouse, a rat, a man and a horse down a mine shaft, and the ratio of weight to surface area decides who survives.

**Cases.**
- *Japanese shinise.* "Success often sows the seeds of its own destruction"; scaling is seen as inherently good but makes things fragile, and "staying small and simple can be a superpower" (insects are most of the world's species). S&P 500 companies average a 24-year life span, and falling. Japan has more than 50,000 companies over 100 years old and nearly 4,000 over 200. Their traits: small (under 100 employees), family or close relationships, a small area, durable loyal customer relationships, and a strong internal philosophy that lets them adapt; small size also means less diffusion of responsibility. Kongo Gumi built Buddhist temples from 578 AD to 2006 over forty generations, with flexible succession (a younger son, an adopted son-in-law, a widow) and coffins during World War II.
- *Artificial light.* We think linearly, but systems scale nonlinearly. Each improvement in light scaled up a fuel infrastructure and changed the scale of productivity: animal-fat lamps 40,000 years ago, Roman beeswax, Middle Ages towns shutting down at night; whale oil in the eighteenth century, the first fuel people didn't make themselves, turning light into an industry. Gas lighting "began as industrial lighting" (Wolfgang Schivelbusch); Frederick Albert Winsor's centralized supply by pipe lowered the marginal price, and "gaslight divided light—and life—from its singular, self-reliant past" (Jane Brox), costing household autonomy, requiring utility regulation, and creating nightlife. Electricity copied the gas grid, ran factories all night and raised fears of light "from which there is no escape." The lessons: problems solved at small scale need solving again at larger scale; new possibilities appear; impact on other systems grows; it does "not result in just more of the same"; a larger interconnected system handles variation better but becomes vulnerable to widespread failure; and scaling may require new materials or a rethought approach.

## Margin of Safety: "Expect the unexpected."

**The model.** Complex systems respond nonlinearly. A *margin of safety* is a meaningful gap between what a system can handle and what it is required to handle: a buffer between safety and danger, order and chaos, success and failure (following distance on a highway). "Engineers know to design for extremes, not averages": a bridge for 5,000 cars a day must handle buses and trucks, high winds, a sports-match crowd and the population in a decade. A margin reduces failure without eliminating it. For investors, it is the gap between intrinsic value and price; since intrinsic value is subjective, make the buffer as large as possible. In life: an emergency fund, health insurance, friends. "You need it the most when you don't have it." "The greater the cost of failure, the bigger the buffer should be."

**Backups**: spare components, capacities or subsystems (alternate routes to the office). A system without backups is unlikely to function long, and higher stakes demand more (a pen versus an airplane; a stroll to local shops versus wilderness hiking). Flying is safer than driving partly because of backups.

**Limits.**
- **Overconfidence and risk compensation.** Seat belts lead people to drive with less care, putting pedestrians at risk (though seat belts still save lives). A watch set fifteen minutes fast fails once you correct for it in your head.
- **Too much caution.** "There is a difference between what's uncomfortable and what ruins you"; most systems can be down for an hour. Too much margin wastes resources and makes you uncompetitive and complacent; too little invites destruction. A margin isn't free: in the short term it feels like leaving money on the table, and in the long run it separates winners from losers.

**Minimum effective dose** (sidebar). The difference between medicine and poison is the dose. The window between the minimum effective dose and the maximum tolerated dose lets doctors start low with a dose likely to work (vaccines).

**Learning as a margin of safety.** "The more we learn, the fewer blind spots we have. Blind spots are the source of all mistakes." Learning more than you need looks inefficient but reduces blind spots; books give vicarious experience. Chris Hadfield: "every astronaut is essentially a perpetual student," "trained to look on the dark side and to imagine the worst things that could possibly happen," then to train for them, with constant debriefs aimed at learning rather than shame. The core skill is to "parse and solve complex problems rapidly, with incomplete information, in a hostile environment." With three to seven people on the station, at least one, preferably two, must be able to handle any problem: "having 'overqualified' crewmates is a safety net for everyone." Ego gets in the way: we learn enough for today's problem, coast on strengths and fear being worst at something; "early success is a terrible teacher." Build a vast repertoire and the ability to tell what's relevant. "Truly being ready means understanding what could go wrong—and having a plan." "If you've got the time, use it to get ready." "When the stakes are high, preparation is everything."

**Anticipating the worst.** You can't back up everything, but where stakes are high, invest in a comprehensive margin. "Extreme events require extreme preparation." James Gleick notes that professionals plan for "mild randomness" and misunderstand "wild randomness," underestimating catastrophe. Jacques Jaujard ("To lead is to anticipate"), director of the Louvre, had seen the Prado during the Spanish Civil War. Before World War II he closed the Louvre for three days "for maintenance" while hundreds packed everything and convoyed it to castles, dispersed across many sites with climate equipment, relocated whenever a site was in doubt, marked with colored circles by importance, with curators sleeping beside the works. The Nazis stole some 5 million artworks across Europe; Rose Valland, working in the Nazis' art-theft division, secretly copied the records of French works (they didn't know she spoke German), and her records later brought many home. Not one Louvre item was lost or damaged. "The greater the threat, the more important it is to plan for the worst."

The questions: "What if I'm wrong? What if things don't go as planned? How much extra capacity do I need?" Build the bridge for ten times the load.

## Churn: "There's always movement."

**The model.** Components wear out or get used up (materials, information, parts), so keeping a system working means replenishing them; that process of attrition is *churn* (sneakers, skin cells, car parts, migration out of cities). Understand how and why the parts wear out.

**In business.** Customer churn depends on alternatives, ease of switching and satisfaction, and indicates product-market fit. High churn means running out of money acquiring customers, who must stay long enough to repay their acquisition cost. A 90% retention rate seems great, yet 5% versus 10% churn compounds into very different growth curves. Employee churn differs by setting (fast food versus government); some brings new perspectives, too much prevents expertise accumulating. Churn too high means "running to stay in the same place": step back and reassess whether it's worthwhile or there's another way.

**Questions.** Can you use churn to your benefit? Are you contorting yourself to keep every customer, when you could let some go and focus on your core? Are you retaining disengaged employees, or people who would grow elsewhere?

**Cases.**
- *Synanon.* "When the purpose of an organization becomes preventing members from leaving, it turns into a cult." Charles Dederich founded it in 1958 as an addiction rehab, started with a $33 unemployment check. A decade in, he decided addicts could never recover and must stay forever; then came brainwashing, shaved heads, forced divorces, no children, "the Game" (traumatic bonding), a paramilitary wing (a rattlesnake in a lawyer's mailbox), and a declaration that it was a religion, because rehab has an end point and religion doesn't. It collapsed in 1991 after losing its tax exemption. Churn is inevitable; trying to eliminate it perverts the goal, and "a group that tries to stop anyone from leaving can only end up violent and coercive." The ability to leave checks abuse of power (people vote with their feet), so "lack of churn can be a powerful indicator that something is not right."
- *Bourbaki.* Churn at the right level is healthy, and where it isn't naturally high enough, build it in; mandated churn reduces people staying for the wrong reasons. In 1935 a group of young mathematicians in a Paris café (André Weil, Henri Cartan, Claude Chevalley and others) set out, under the fictional name Nicolas Bourbaki, to compile all of mathematics into a single theory. France had not exempted academics from World War I and lost a generation, leaving teachers elderly and out of date. Members retired at fifty and invited younger replacements, and could leave anytime, so membership was never static, bringing new ideas, up-to-date knowledge and enthusiasm. (Even so, no system functions forever; the environment changed.)

Churn is a "silent killer," a slow, hidden leak. A snake sheds its skin; "some turnover allows us to maintain stability," and replacing worn parts is a chance to upgrade. "Some churn is inevitable. Too much can kill you."

## Algorithms: "Recipes for success."

**The model.** An algorithm turns inputs into outputs: a recipe, a set of rules that, followed precisely, lead to a predictable end, often a series of unambiguous if-then statements. Harari: a methodical set of steps for calculations, problems and decisions; a method, not a particular calculation (averaging any two numbers). Daniel Dennett: a formal process that can be counted on to yield a certain result whenever run. They matter because systems adjust on information from algorithms, and because algorithms let systems scale without starting from scratch. Dennett's three characteristics:
1. **Substrate neutrality**: the logical structure matters, not the material (phone or book).
2. **Underlying mindlessness**: each step is utterly simple, leaving no room for interpretation.
3. **Guaranteed results**: "An algorithm is a foolproof recipe."

Algorithms range from simple (a recipe) to complicated (crime prediction); DNA and human learning are biological algorithms. Some evolve and learn; others are static. Increasingly they are designed to be "directionally correct over perfect," since neither nature nor people aim for optimal output every time.

**Moves.**
- Consider the underlying instructions that dictate a system's behavior to decide how to intervene.
- **Algorithmic thinking:** use the model to choose and refine the inputs you feed the system, organizing it to leave as little to chance as possible. "You don't always need to be good at knowing the answers, you just need to have a good algorithm for finding them."
- Algorithms are if-then machines for tuning out noise and zeroing in on signal ("Have the specs been met?"). Thinking algorithmically means searching for processes that reliably produce results. They are also a model for our own decision-making (sidebar on AI, from Turing's work on Enigma onward).

**New numbers** (sidebar). Standard inputs can create brand-new outputs. Algorithms come from codifying repetitive actions (long multiplication). The subtraction algorithm, run on six minus nine, produced negative numbers (Paul Lockhart); the square-root algorithm, run on 2, produced irrational numbers.

**Cases.**
- *Pirate constitutions* (Peter Leeson, *The Invisible Hook*). Groups need coherent algorithms with the right incentives; where people can't leave, force fills the gap, while groups people choose evolve rules that guard against unjust leadership. A constitution is a high-level algorithm limiting power and defining responsibilities: never perfect, but clear goals, consistent application and amendments make it work. Pirates had no government, prisons or police; crews averaged about eighty, multinational, earning 100 to 1,000 times a merchant seaman's pay. Their articles turned labor and gunpowder into plunder and produced repeatable behavior that tamed uncertainty: they couldn't control the weather, but they could control the crew. Typical articles: keep weapons clean, no gambling, no drinking below deck after 8 p.m., settle quarrels on shore, equal shares (leaders slightly more), bravery bonuses, disability benefits, set punishments, limits on leaders, and rules for making new rules, all agreed unanimously. Captains and quartermasters were elected and removable anytime, with power divided (captain in battle, quartermaster day to day). Ching Shih, commanding the Red Flag fleet of 70,000 to 80,000 pirates on up to 2,000 ships, enforced rules such as no harm to those who surrendered, no going ashore without permission, reporting all goods and keeping at most a fifth, and death for unsanctioned orders, harming villagers or raping captives; she retired peacefully and rich. Algorithms ensure cohesion by aligning the parts' goals, and enforcement is critical: same input, same output, applied without exceptions.
- *Bayer and sulfa* (Thomas Hager, *The Demon under the Microscope*). Heinrich Hörlein saw that drug research lacked scale and depended on individual genius, so he built an industrial system running one algorithmic process. Chemist Josef Klarer made hundreds of compounds; Gerhard Domagk ran the "recipe," testing each against a panel of diseases in test tubes and in mice by three routes (intravenous, under the skin, by mouth). Thousands of mice died, and Domagk "changed neither his methods nor his approach." In fall 1932 compound KL 695 cured strep in mice (though not in the test tube), found while Domagk was on vacation because the process ran without him; refining the inputs gave KL 730, and the active part proved to be sulfa itself. Industrial-scale investigation guided by therapeutic goals became the standard for drug research.

## Supporting Idea: Complex Adaptive Systems

Scott E. Page separates *complex systems*, whose entities follow fixed rules, from *complex adaptive systems*, whose entities adapt, giving the system greater capacity to respond. A simple nonadaptive system (a pocket watch) can be understood by its parts. A complex adaptive system has properties greater than the sum of its parts and cannot be understood through its components: parts interact nonlinearly, a few basic rules let it self-organize without central control, interaction and information passing create complexity, it changes in response to its environment and in pursuit of goals, and it has "memories" (traffic is one). Melanie Mitchell's definition: large networks of components with no central control and simple rules giving rise to complex collective behavior, sophisticated information processing, and adaptation by learning or evolution.

- Parts are interdependent and tiny changes get amplified (one broken-down car backs up a city). "In a complex adaptive system, we can never do just one thing." Unintended consequences of intervention are almost inevitable, and we make things worse because we overestimate our degree of control.
- The macro can't be understood from the micro (Murray Gell-Mann: no gluing together of partial studies gives the whole). Be comfortable with the nonlinear and unexpected.
- These systems learn and change in response to new information: a flu model must anticipate how people change behavior after warnings.
- Be humble, use the scientific method, don't mistake correlation for causation, expect yesterday's learning to change tomorrow, and don't give up because it's complex.
- They work best slightly disorganized (mutation, experimentation); deviations cancel out in the long run.

## Critical Mass: "Going critical."

**The model.** A system is *critical* when it is on the verge of changing state: one more unit of input, just like all those before it, has a disproportionate effect (the straw that breaks the camel's back), and past the threshold a tiny nudge changes everything. In social systems, *critical mass* or the *tipping point* is enough adopters that growth sustains itself (Thomas Schelling, *Micromotives and Macrobehavior*, 1978, borrowing the term from nuclear engineering). The energy needed varies by system: water at 211°F versus 212°F; a business that no longer needs outside investment; a vaccination threshold that protects those who can't be immunized.

**Moves.**
- Understand the effort required for sustained change, not just the tipping point. "The last piece matters only because of all the pieces that came before it."
- Target the right parts: opinion leaders, rather than equal effort on everyone. Changing a system doesn't require changing everything, only a small percentage of its parts; once past the threshold, the change perpetuates itself.
- Critical states are precarious and don't last. Recognize when a system is poised at the edge and what the straw could be: a pencil balanced on its end looks like equilibrium but isn't stable.
- "Things happen slowly and then all at once." Identify the elements to change and the work to put in, and keep going until change is self-sustaining. "Once you pass a tipping point, the whole nature of a system changes. It develops new properties."

**Overton window** (sidebar). Joseph P. Overton (Mackinac Center, 1990s): the range of ideas politicians can acceptably propose. It shifts over time, and far-out ideas get advanced to make moderate ones palatable, along the progression unthinkable, radical, acceptable, sensible, popular, policy. Politicians prioritize the window over their beliefs, and windows differ by country. Attitudes are not static.

**Minority opinions** (sidebar). A majority isn't required. Once opinion leaders hold a view, those who don't face negative consequences. Research at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute put the threshold at 10% regardless of network type; other research says about 25%. People are less committed to their opinions than they imagine, and the threshold varies with the stakes and the minority's social clout.

**Cases.**
- *New Zealand women's suffrage.* In September 1893 New Zealand became the first self-governing country where women could vote, 25 years before the UK and 27 before the US, after a slow shift of the Overton window. The groundwork: settlers wanting a fairer society, a small population (under 750,000) with fewer minds to change, prominent male politicians' support, girls' education (the first girls' school in 1871; women half of university students by 1893) leading to jobs, influence and unions, and the temperance movement, where women learned the arts of organization, administration and leadership. Kate Sheppard's petitions gathered 32,000 signatures in 1893, and the bill passed by a whisker.
- *Organic cities.* In nuclear physics, critical mass is the minimum fissile material for a self-sustaining reaction: the question is how much you need to kick-start something so it continues without further input. City planners misidentify the elements: "it's not the amount of infrastructure that produces interactions, it's how that infrastructure is laid out." Jane Jacobs: "A city sidewalk by itself is nothing"; mixed uses put eyes on the street, given a "substantial quantity of stores and other public places," including ones open at night, and that organic social control works better than police. A sidewalk needs a minimum number of interactions, and "activity attracts more activity." Charles Montgomery: the richest social environments let people edge closer or move apart, scaling gradually from private to public; an overly large square repels. Copenhagen pedestrianized Strøget in the 1960s despite doubts about the cold and the lack of café culture, and it drew 120,000 visitors on the last Sunday before Christmas (Jan Gehl: "Street culture is not specific to certain cultures"). Planned cities built from infrastructure checklists segregate functions: Brasília (Oscar Niemeyer and Lucio Costa) separated uses on a grid, provided no low-income housing and got no street culture; "only by deviating from the plan can it function at all."

## Emergence: "Organization without an organizer."

**The model.** Systems at the macro scale show capabilities absent at the micro scale that can't be predicted from the parts. Aristotle: "The whole is something over and above its parts." New capabilities arise from innocuous elements (a flock of birds), so you can't understand them by reduction. Termite mounds reach up to seventeen feet, moving a ton of soil and several tons of water a year, with ventilation, fungus gardens and the queen's chamber, and no leader.
- **Weak emergence** has identifiable rules and can be modeled (bird-flocking simulations). **Strong emergence** has no identifiable rules and can't be modeled (consciousness).
- **Self-organization**: order from the bottom up, without central control (birds keeping an even distance).
- **Emergence and complexity** (sidebar) are not synonymous. A complex system may have only resultant properties (a nuclear power plant whose parts work as expected); a simple system can show emergence (chess: simple rules, unpredictable outcomes).
- "Emergence is irreducible" (the goose that laid golden eggs): parts don't display emergent properties, only the aggregate does, so disassembly loses them.

**Moves.** The model is not about predicting emergent properties but acknowledging they are possible. "You don't always need to plan things all the way to the end. If you have a simple starting point on the right trajectory, surprising things can pan out." Mix it up: learn new skills, meet new people, read new things.

**Cases.**
- *The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo.* Groups produce results that don't follow intuitively from individual capabilities, and cumulative actions yield outcomes different from anyone's intentions. During Argentina's Dirty War (1976 to 1983, a name given only in retrospect), some 30,000 people were "disappeared" (*los desaparecidos*) and babies were taken and given away. On April 30, 1977, fourteen mothers began walking in the plaza; they grew to hundreds, in white headscarves embroidered with their children's names and birth dates, every Thursday from 1977 to 2006. The government called them crazy and misread the emergence: a regime that relied on fear and silence was most vulnerable to people speaking out. "Visibility was both a refuge and a trap" (Diana Taylor); founders were murdered, but foreign media and human rights groups took notice. After 1983 more than 850 people were charged and more than 120 stolen children identified. The effects went further: world attention, a regime losing its sense of control, groups inspired worldwide. "What the mothers achieved was not inevitable. Many other similar groups failed."
- *Social innovation.* Social interaction matters as much as, or more than, individual smarts. Joseph Henrich: technologies emerge "not from singular geniuses but from the flow and recombination of ideas," and "dumb" processes over generations produce practices smarter than any individual or group (how many people would you need to guarantee one could start a fire?). Among Arctic foragers (Boyd and Richerson), most people imitate and a few improve slightly, and cultural transmission preserves the nudges; we use items we couldn't build and practices whose reasons we don't know. *Collective brains* form in large interconnected groups that learn with sufficient fidelity and have strong norms; language itself is an unconscious emergent product. "Innovation does not take a genius or a village; it takes a big network of freely interacting minds."

## Supporting Idea: Chaos Dynamics

Steven Strogatz: systems behave linearly only near equilibrium and when not pushed too hard. Chaotic systems are sensitive to initial conditions, the *butterfly effect*. In the 1950s Edward Lorenz at MIT reran a weather model with a number rounded off and got starkly different weather. Prediction would require perfect starting conditions, since inaccuracies grow exponentially over time, which contradicts the assumption that tiny differences don't matter; only probabilistic predictions are possible (a 60 percent chance of rain tomorrow). Laplace (1814) thought knowing every particle would let you predict anything; chaos shows deterministic rules can still defeat prediction. "In the face of chaos, we should expect to be surprised," though behavior keeps its own logic ("For want of a nail..."). What popular culture gets wrong (sidebar): a wing flap doesn't *cause* a typhoon; a difference in starting conditions is enough for a typhoon in one world and not another, so you can't look at an outcome and name the change that caused it. "No moment is any more significant than any other. Every single moment changes everything that happens after."

## Irreducibility: "As simple as possible but no simpler."

**The model.** Reduce a theory or system to its most understandable level, past which it loses meaning; that point, where further reduction loses usefulness, is effectively irreducible (feelings, life and a chess game are taken as wholes). It echoes first principles thinking: the minimum time, components or structure that keeps a thing's overall qualities. The key question: "What is the minimum amount necessary for a thing to still be that thing?" Find the point beyond which you change the fundamentals, so you recognize when you are turning the system into something else, and don't get sidetracked by what you think ought to be there. Einstein: keep the irreducible basic elements as simple and few as possible without giving up adequate representation of a single datum.

**Gall's Law** (sidebar, John Gall, *The Systems Bible*): complex systems that work invariably evolved from simple systems that worked; building a complex system from scratch is ineffective (bureaucracies grow from a single form, tigers from bacteria, cities from towns, airplanes from bicycles). Avoid designing complex systems from scratch, and accept you can't always tell how one works from its parts.

**Cases.**
- *Loose lips sink ships.* Simple communication carries less ambiguity: five words and a sinking boat say don't talk, there are spies, and everyone has a role. Wartime posters that read like novels failed. Abram Games sought "maximum meaning from minimum means," but never so simple as to be ambiguous. Common symbols (an eagle, the color red) are culturally specific: "The less you have to explain, the more you can communicate." "Simplicity can convey a powerful meaning. But too much simplicity conveys no meaning at all."
- *Typography.* Simplify past a point and a thing stops working; knowing the limits lets you experiment. Eric Gill (1931): "Letters are signs for sounds," not pictures; a Roman capital A doesn't stop being an A because it's sloped. What constitutes "A-ness"? Strip an A to a bare triangle without its crossbar and it fails. Context matters: a rounded square reads as O until it sits beside a D made the same way, so "irreducible elements of a system are not fixed and depend on the context and goals." Paul Felton: a headline's irreducible element is being immediately noticeable, not being larger (bold works anywhere). You can't make a baby in one month with nine women; mistake the irreducible components and jettison the rest, and you change the system's nature (type becomes visual art instead of communication).

Irreducibility is about essence. Sometimes zoom out instead of breaking down; some problems are irreducibly messy; focus on what you can change by understanding what really matters.

## The Law of Diminishing Returns: "Hard work stops paying off."

**The model.** Input and output aren't linear: inputs raise output until each further unit yields less, and pushed further, total output may fall (sugar in lemonade, workers crowding a factory floor, fertilizer beyond the right ratio). An extra hour of work helps; an extra three breed mistakes. Tweaking details, oversized funding (pushing investor returns over customers), and practice in a skill (huge early gains, smaller later) all show it. Inputs are not equal: an extra worker in a ten-person factory differs from one in a hundred-person factory, an hour at 9 p.m. from one at 9 a.m. "Often we focus on the trivial at the expense of the meaningful." Calculate where the point lies for each system.

"Diminishing returns happen because systems adapt": they grow accustomed to inputs, so expect to get less over time.

**Moves.** Easy wins come first; then you're squeezing the lemon. Beginners gain fast, and "going from good to great is often harder than going from bad to good." Put resources where they give the biggest bang for the buck, know when to stop optimizing and move on, and when more effort brings less return, change tack: "noticing diminishing returns means it's time to try something new."

**Sidebars.**
- *Mass incarceration.* Removing the most violent (a tiny minority) makes everyone safer; each further increment buys less safety while costs to taxpayers and lost contributions grow, so "always a good idea" has a logical end point. Émile Durkheim: some crime is inevitable, since crime is defined by "collective sentiments"; suppress the worst crimes and minor ones gain significance. Buffett: "What the wise do in the beginning, fools do in the end."
- *Societal collapse* (Joseph Tainter). Complexity demands more "energy flow" (hierarchy, information, centralization, specialists); at some point its cost exceeds its benefit to individuals, societies disintegrate, and returning to a simpler level makes sense.

**Cases.**
- *Viking raids on Paris.* After Charlemagne died in 814, Viking fleets returned to the Franks again and again; in 845 a fleet of 120 ships reached Paris, and Charles the Bald paid a ransom of 7,000 pounds of silver and gold, setting a precedent: from 845 to 926 the Franks paid about 685 pounds of gold and 43,000 of silver. The Franks adapted with walls, fortified bridges and towers, making sieges long and costly; areas ran out of wealth, Franks stopped rebuilding the monasteries, distant villages cost more to reach. By 886 a weakened raider asked for just sixty pounds, and in 911 Rollo accepted land, a title and a marriage from Charles the Simple in exchange for protecting the area, which became Normandy. You can't repeat the same actions and expect the same results; returns are dramatic at first, and when more effort yields less, change tack.
- *Exploitation films*, 1950s to 1970s. Reactions to novelty diminish (the first snowfall, a roller coaster), so holding attention means raising the bar. Exploitation films capitalized on trends and sensational content, first dressed as education to slip past censors, then flourishing after the 1948 antitrust ruling and the rise of TV and youth culture. The pattern: a strong response, hasty copying, lost impact, more gore needed (*What Ever Happened to Baby Jane?* spawned *What Ever Happened to Aunt Alice?*; *Don't Look in the Basement* begat *Don't Open the Window*, *Don't Go in the House*, *Don't Answer the Phone!*; taglines reassuring viewers "it's just a movie"). Copying deadened responses and made audiences skeptical, and the genre largely died as its fringe was absorbed into the mainstream and audiences were desensitized. Exposure therapy for phobias works by the same mechanism. Watch for cycles where the fringe becomes banal mainstream and resets what counts as average.
