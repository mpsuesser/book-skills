# Games: rules and coaching notes

Glouberman's games come from his charades classes, his theater classes ("improvised behavior": "theater but dumber", the running around and behaving foolishly without character or story) and his noise-music classes and events (Terrible Noises for Beautiful People). Groups run from pairs to 100 people.

## Running any game

- **New problems make new games.** When a class goes wrong (people too shy, sure their sounds aren't musical, uneven in comfort, stuck making the same kinds of sounds), invent a game that fixes it. "By far the most productive source of new games is new problems."
- **Train the component skills first.** In charades he held off a real game until the second or third meeting: "irresponsible to cast people into the game without training or warm-ups." A drill can be harder than the game, the way lifting weights prepares you for a sport.
- **Games have to be enjoyed to be played properly**, and that means taking pleasure in limited control.
- **Pretend fighting is basic to play.** Young animals play by pretend-fighting, and a game is a pretend battle. About half of each of his theater and music classes is fighting. To "Why aren't your games more positive?": "You should be nice in real life. You don't have to *play* at niceness." "A good way to know that you're playing is if you're doing something you would never do in real life—like having a ridiculous fight."
- **Combine abandon with tight structure.** Strict forms followed by "freestyle" teach people to do what they want within a range of options, "but to be really aware of what they're doing."
- **The purposes are local.** He bans rhythm, melody and harmony in noise classes in order to investigate what's possible without them. "I have to be strict because if I'm not, the investigation collapses." It isn't a manifesto against those things.
- **Noise frees people from the amateurism trap.** Learning "good" music means imitating professionals, "playing the sort of stuff you hear on the radio, but worse." Nobody has a preconception of what noise music should sound like.
- **Choose games by what winning says about you.** He played Mafia once and won by convincing everyone he wasn't Mafia, then never played again. Winning at charades tells your friends you communicate well. Winning at Mafia tells them you're good at convincing them you're honest while lying.
- **Mistakes from one perspective are successes from another.** In a game that is also a music piece, the players getting it wrong often make the most interesting sounds.

## Charades

There are two skill sets, acting and guessing, like fielding and hitting.

- **Acting.** The other person doesn't know the answer, so build a gesture a stranger could decode. When a gesture fails, change it rather than repeating it more exasperated. Be precise in your gestures, be wholehearted, and bring emotional content.
- **Guessing.** "Assume that every detail is important." If someone drinks daintily with a pinkie out, the word isn't *drink*. It might be *tea* or *English*.
- **Treat it as a dialogue.** Guessers throw out lots of guesses, which lets the actor adjust and shows whether they're on track. "If you just sit and watch, waiting until you know for sure, you'll never get it right." Actors keep adjusting to what is getting through.
- **The point.** "Without the difficulty, there is no game." On one level the goal is to convey a clue as fast as possible under constraints. "Really, though, the goal of charades is to amuse people." You learn "to enjoy yourself while trying to communicate with people who don't understand you and don't know what you know."
- **"Tournament regulations"** (he invented them, since no tournaments exist): no hand signals for letters, and no talking at all, not even "*Whoa, this one's hard*" or "*Yes, yes, you're on the right track.*" The game has a built-in limit on how seriously you can take it, since sign language would win.
- **"The whole thing"**, a big sweeping gesture through the air, means you'll act out the movie or book itself rather than the words of the title. It is the most important gesture and often the most fun.

### Drills, in teaching order

1. **Quick Single Words in Pairs.** While waiting for stragglers, have everyone write single words on slips of paper and put them in a bag. Pairs take turns acting out the words for each other, skipping any that are too hard. After three or four minutes, switch partners, and keep switching. Everyone plays with everyone, which builds one-to-one camaraderie quickly, and the low pressure means shy people have to play. (Single words are actually harder than titles, since you can't pick the easy words first.)
2. **Acting Practice.** Comfort is half of acting. The whole group at once acts out words from the bag, *being* the thing rather than indicating it: "*Everybody be a telephone.*"
3. **Charades Instruction Circle.** Use full clues, with everyone standing in a circle. One person acts a clue for the person on their right, who has thirty seconds. If they fail, the clue passes on. Everyone watches, and after each clue the group discusses what worked and how each person's strategy went.
4. **Speed Drills.** Speed gets people out of their heads and into surprising physical choices. In pairs, one person acts only and the other guesses only, for two minutes, getting as many full clues as possible with up to about three passes. Pairs report their counts. If the best pair got five, challenge the room to get seven.

### Homework: the hardest possible clues

To answer "*Oh, this is impossible*", run it in two parts:

1. Everyone goes home and brings back ten to twenty insanely hard clues.
2. Shuffle them. Each person takes home a random set and gets a week to strategize, then everyone acts them in class.

Remind them first that acting is a dialogue: come with several strategies and be ready to adapt. His last class guessed every one.

A hard clue is one with many words that are hard to act out. An obscure title doesn't count: "stupid and wrong," since there are infinite titles nobody has heard of. The fun is **recognition**, digging something out of "their brain's dusty corners" that "should still be recallable." *Oryx and Crake* beat a whole class even though more than half of them had heard of it. Other examples from his list: *Being and Nothingness*, Søren Kierkegaard, E Pluribus Unum, *Koyaanisqatsi*, trickle-down economics, shock and awe, "Que Sera, Sera", Mr. Snuffleupagus, the Dutch tulip craze of the seventeenth century, and "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife."

## The gibberish game

This comes from Keith Johnstone ("the inventor of improv as I learned it"; Glouberman has been rereading Johnstone's *Impro* for over twenty years). It "makes it impossible for anybody to really be in control."

- **Setup.** Two actors onstage, each with an offstage translator. An actor speaks a line of gibberish "with as much specificity and intention as she possibly can." Her translator immediately gives the English, instinctively and confidently: the translator "has to know what the gibberish means." Then the second actor speaks and her translator translates, and so on.
- **Notes for actors.** Use very clear, specific gestures and enunciate the nonsense. Craft doesn't matter; *blah blah blah* is fine.
- **Notes for translators.** Make the translation sound like the gibberish, following its intonations, sounds and rhythms. The cheap jokes (a long sentence translated as "*Yes*", a loving tone made angry, a child made into an adult) "basically constitute refusing to play the game."
- **When it works.** The translator isn't uncannily accurate. Instead, every translation throws the actor off-balance with something similar but different, and the effect is "giddy and dizzying." Afterward people are mistaken about who said what. Every line is a collaboration between two people. With practice, people reach a third state: "to feel that the other person said exactly what you intended, but that you didn't know it until you heard it."

## Home Maladies

This trains people to accept whatever their scene partner gives them, wholeheartedly. It is the classic gift-giving exercise, played with *The Doctors Book of Home Remedies*. Player one says "*Oh, I got you a gift*" and hands over the book. Player two thanks them warmly, opens to a random page and takes the malady at the top ("*You got me diarrhea*"). Player two then suffers the most severe case of it while expressing great joy and gratitude, and gives a gift back. It teaches people "they don't have to run away from bad things in scenes," and it plays with "the tension between delight and horribleness." The book works because there's about one ailment per page and few serious diseases, so people give each other dandruff, not leukemia.

## Fighting games

Pairs argue in gibberish (language-like sounds, no real words) through a sequence of forms, "a martial art for ridiculous arguments":

1. **Form 1.** Exactly one person speaks at a time, with no pauses. You must keep speaking until interrupted. When interrupted, you must stop, though you can interrupt right back a microsecond later.
2. **Form 2.** One person speaks at a time, and you may never interrupt. When the other person stops, you must start.
3. **Form 3.** Both speak all the time. The challenge is to listen and respond without ever stopping.
4. **Form 4.** Either both speak or both are silent. While silent, both must be moving, and the argument continues wordlessly as they circle and gesture.
5. **Form 5.** Both are always silent and always moving.
6. **Freestyle.** Invoke any form at any time. The rules aren't taken away; people choose within the range, aware of what they're doing.

People lose themselves easily in these games, because anger is easy to tap into and nobody has to invent words or a story.

**Variation 1: endings.** At any moment one player must start to cry and collapse to the floor, and the other must do a victory dance. Some people struggle to be the crier and some to dance, but they get over it quickly.

**Variation 2: the arms race.** At any moment a player can raise their hands and cower, which means the other player now has a weapon and must threaten them as they back away. The armed player can throw up their hands in turn, meaning the other has a bigger weapon, and the roles reverse. The threatened player can end the arms race by refusing to cower and yelling back, which returns the fight to ordinary arguing.

"The power in the exercise is put into the hands of the person who's powerless in the story." You can only give the *other* person the gun, and only *you* can cry and end the fight. He declares the crier the winner. These variations train actors out of protecting or winning for their character and out of being too polite onstage: "*you are not your character*. You are the *author* of your character." You can win the game by having your character lose the fight.

**From fight to music.** Musical improvisers need to listen and respond while making sound, to know that silence is okay, to have several ways of answering a partner (join them, repeat them, do something completely different), and to feel free to take the piece in a new direction. This is hard to teach directly with music, but arguing teaches it in about twenty minutes. Then tell them "a duet is just an argument except without anger," and the sounds no longer need to resemble language. "The best way to get people to communicate and collaborate on something beautiful is for them to start with a fight."

## Music and noise games

### The vowel drone
Ask a roomful of people to close their eyes and make and hold a vowel sound together. From outside it's "the most boring, unmusical sound you can imagine." From inside it can be very beautiful, because it awakens the underused pleasure of making sounds with other people.

### Converge / diverge
1. Everyone makes whatever sound they want, which produces a cacophony.
2. Converge gradually until everyone makes the same sound. Use listening only: no hand-waving or signaling, and no one person dominating while the rest join. Gradually take in other people's sounds. Resist rounding off the details. Where details disagree, "err on the side of more and sharper detail," especially timbre and emotion, and exaggerate them if needed.
3. In unison, perfect it: listen for points of disagreement and sharpen them. Hold the unison a long while; "there's a pretty deep pleasure in that unison ... especially when it took some work to get there."
4. Diverge so that it sounds gradual to a listener. If everyone makes a small change at once, it sounds enormous, so diverge at different times. One way to diverge gradually is not to diverge at all. Keep parts of the converged sound as you move out.
5. Improvise freely for a while. Before reconverging, push to even greater divergence: "listen to what is going on in the room and try to do the thing that is most opposite to what they hear."
6. Repeat.

"The resolution is unison and the tension is chaos." It's tension and resolution without pitch, and a group making a collective decision gradually, without a leader and with most communication removed.

### The conducting game (10 to 100 people)
Everyone walks around making whatever sounds they like. To be conducted, stand still and point at your head with your hand up. When you see someone waiting, conduct them; leaving them waiting is impolite. A piece lasts a minute or a minute and a half and ends when either person walks away, without saying goodbye.

- **Conductor's notes.** Be as specific, clear, deliberate and emphatic as possible, and fill every gesture with urgency and meaning. Trust that the person is great at this and will make great sounds. Expect your gestures to be read in ways you didn't intend; that's okay.
- **Notes for the person being conducted.** Trust that the conductor knows exactly what they're doing and that you're excited to work with them. Trust that you know "instinctively, immediately, and completely what every single gesture means," and that every sound you make is their responsibility. Respond to the emotional content, and "assume that every element of the conducting has meaning," from their facial expression to whether their fingers are curved.

The person being conducted feels controlled but authors every sound: "feeling not at all responsible while actually being completely responsible." The conductor inspires them and "gives them permission to do much more, by appearing to take away control while not really taking away control." Telling a group to make varied, interesting sounds rarely works, while a person making essentially meaningless gestures, called a conductor, unlocks amazing sounds quickly. The sounds inspire the conductor in turn.

### Conducting from the center of a circle
The group forms a circle with one person in the center, eyes closed. Closed eyes mark the audience, so this person is both audience and conductor, in a less authoritative role. Spin them a little and rearrange the ensemble so they lose track of who is where. They do whatever they like to evoke or change the sounds, thinking especially about timbre, the *kinds* of sound. "Conducting is when you make movements to alter a sound, and dancing is when you make movements in response to a sound." Here they do both. It's best when the conductor forgets that people they know are responding and gets lost in the soundscape.

### The rocks game (2 to 6 players)
Everyone gets some rocks. Players take turns either placing a rock on the floor or moving one already there, with no talking, facial expressions or pointing. Each move comments on the arrangement, approving or disapproving of a pattern, and aesthetics emerge fast ("*That ruins everything*"). It makes music improvisation concrete, because everyone can point at the same rocks. Players learn what it means to join a pattern, break one, start a new one or play counterpoint. Want the patterns exactly your way and the game is frustrating; have no attachment and it's boring.

### Listening as a role
In some games one role is to sit with your eyes closed and listen. Listeners are part of the piece, which keeps the game "a social thing among the people who are present, and ... not a rehearsal for some imagined future audience," and reminds the movers that the running around serves sound. The basic version, for ten people: four listeners sit with eyes closed in different parts of the room, four make sounds (often abstract, argument-like dialogues after the fighting games), and two are spectators on the couch, who are *not* part of the piece. The sound-makers soon play with the listeners by whispering in ears or sneaking up and making loud noises. The piece sounds different to each listener. Shuffle the roles and play again until everyone has heard it from each position.

## Endings

- **Find an ending.** Give a signal (ring a bell, or throw something at them) meaning "find an ending." At first people simply stop. Say: "*No, no, you can keep playing—you just have to end it.*" Soon they take more responsibility, try sudden new directions and risks, and play as if the next bit matters. "Telling people, *Find an ending*, is like saying, *Play a little bit better for a while, and then stop.*"
- **Get louder or quit.** This replaces the boring slow fade, and it works especially well with a large group. Every few seconds each player checks in with themselves and either drops out (lying down to listen, if standing) or continues, and continuing means getting louder. Fifty soft sounds become ten louder ones, then two people screaming. It discourages "lazy group behavior": a good improviser fights "the joining inclination" not to be the gazelle that strays from the herd.
