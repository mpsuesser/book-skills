# Life and art

What Glouberman says about personal questions and about what art is for.

## Friends

- **In a new city, making friends is a project.** In school, thousands of people your age are crammed together, all looking for friends at the same moment. Adult life isn't like that, and many people are shocked to find themselves isolated. Treat it as a project and its costs become acceptable: going to functions you don't feel like attending, making gestures that feel embarrassing or vulnerable, spending time with people who turn out to be dull.
- **"Friendship needs an activity associated with it."** Work out what you like doing and find people to do it with: a cooking class, drinking in bars, mocking TV together.
- **If you can, put yourself at the center.** Host a party every two weeks, or start a recurring night around your interest. New in Toronto, he liked bars and thinking about the Internet, so he started a biweekly bar night for all the lonely, isolated "web guys" scattered around the city. "Being a host—it's a really super-valuable service that a lot of people are disinclined to do."
- **"Friends are the people you play with."** His students grow warm and close through play without knowing much about each other's lives. A friend from Kelowna said Toronto friendships are built on talking, while in Kelowna you go swimming: "the people floating near you are your friends."
- **Parents, once a week.** "Visit your parents once a week. Agree on the day." That ends the negotiations about when, along with the expectations and guilt on either side.

## Decisions and uncertainty

**"A decision is a thing you make."** The career book that helped him laid out a decision in four steps:

1. Figure out what you want, meaning your priorities. This isn't mysterious, but it takes time and thinking.
2. Collect information from the world about your options and the likely outcomes of each.
3. Write it up, maybe in a chart.
4. "*Now once you've done all this, make the decision.*"

"You can't externalize the project of making a decision." No algorithm will rationalize it for you. Nervous people treat a decision as an intellectual puzzle with a correct answer waiting to be found. In fact "a decision is something you *create* ... an act of will, not an act of thought." Fear about the possibilities a choice rules out "is kind of the *definition* of choice."

**Gather data about yourself from friends.** Ask them what they think you're interested in, what you're good at and what you're not good at, rather than what you should do. His friends said he likes new things and gets bored easily (he had thought he liked routine), that he thinks best in real time with other people (unlike writers, who think alone), and that he can think on his feet. Teaching came up a lot. Some thought he already had a coherent career.

**Two ways out of painful uncertainty:** "increase your certainty," or "increase your tolerance for uncertainty." He had been trying to eliminate uncertainty with one perfect career decision, but "there's no way you're going to eliminate uncertainty." The converse also holds: "You don't have to accept more uncertainty than you have to. The fact that you can't know everything doesn't mean that you shouldn't try to know anything."

**Look at the world.** Science "is a genuine curiosity about what's true and isn't true, empirically, in the world." Whether talking about your childhood or gradual exposure cures a phobia is something you find out "by *looking at the world*." Even the claim that only something oblique and less solutions-oriented works is "an empirical question." Offering to help people with their problems while refusing to ask what works is irresponsible. Art may shake things up with uncertain outcomes; psychotherapy probably shouldn't.

**Good self-help books are underrated.** Other people have had your problem and thought hard about it, and a well-written book can keep you out of a lot of blind alleys.

## The crazy parts

Clinical psychology catalogues the intellectual mistakes people make when they're crazy, and knowing the catalogue is as useful as learning the fallacies in philosophy. Someone who washes their hands a thousand times a day is making a logical mistake: no amount of handwashing eliminates colds. Everyone is a little crazy in a million ways: irrationally berating themselves (as in depression), getting a thought stuck or working far past usefulness (as in OCD), overascribing meaning to random events (as in schizophrenia). A phobia counts as a phobia only if you recognize it as irrational; a rubber duck that unsettles you is a phobia, and a rubber duck you think will hurt you is a delusion. The skill is to spot which of your thoughts are irrational by stepping back from them "at a time when you're not emotionally engaged with them." His own: "to put more energy than is necessary into attaining certainty when certainty is actually impossible, or when the benefit of extra certainty would be quite low."

## Quitting smoking

He smoked over sixty cigarettes a day (two and a half packs, at twenty-five to a Canadian pack) for fifteen years. The months-long project that finally got him to quit, which he admits was completely crazy, included books, a 12-step-style group, a specialist therapist, switching to a brand he disliked, lighter cigarettes, and practicing working, drinking and talking to girls at parties without smoking before he quit.

The useful advice is simpler. "To quit smoking, all you need to do is make the decision that you will never smoke again." Smoking is unlike losing weight, or any decision that is a matter of degree. The decision means:

- **No exceptions.** Not once in a while, and not as a reward for not smoking. In college he took a couple of puffs a day and turned smoking into the reward for quitting smoking.
- **No crisis clause.** "There are always crises." His college relapse came with exam period. "*I'm not going to smoke again unless something bad happens*" means smoking again within ten years. "It's pretty easy to quit for a year, but the goal really is to not start again ever." His therapist pointed out that even in a best-case life both your parents die: "*You have to think about your parents dying and not starting smoking again when that happens.*"
- **Plan past day one, and give yourself rewards.** He planned his quit date months ahead but not the second day. He wore a suit to lunch on the first day, then kept wearing it for a month, the same suit, even at home, as his reward. The suit "gave me powers." (The expensive yo-yo went too far.)
- **Expect the negative space.** Quitting is "completely occupied by negative space," a project made entirely of *not* doing something, which makes it hard to know how much time to give it.

## Fraud and impostors

- **Feeling like a fraud is normal before real work.** Before every series he thinks, "*I have nothing to offer these people—they will realize that I am a fraud.*" He has had panic attacks "about a fucking charades class." "A lot of art is about creating the illusion of ease," and making anything involves fear and difficulty, plus covering them up.
- **Impostor syndrome may be accurate.** "There might be people who feel this way because they *are* impostors." The pressure to achieve and to sell yourself makes many people present a false version of themselves. "The solution ... may not be in getting people to accept that they really are who they say they are, but rather to stop pressuring people into lying so much."

## Love

- **Love is daily partnership.** Robert McKee teaches that Rick's choice in *Casablanca* to give up the Ingrid Bergman character shows the depth of his love. Glouberman calls this "the most destructively romantic understanding of love." "Love is what happens between people living their lives together, becoming close through contact and actual partnership, and it's what survives through difficulties and imperfections." An idealized faraway person in your heart: "That's a daydream." People either feel disappointed when love doesn't measure up to it or protect it from real life.
- **Two toxic ideas.** The Hollywood idea is that love is magic and you'll always be head over heels, so any other feeling means big trouble. The hippie idea is to always express everything and expect the relationship to always feel good, which ended savable marriages whose partners wouldn't endure the natural difficulty and sacrifice. He thinks couples have gotten much better at this over the past thirty years, and hopes people will soon talk about raising children with the same nuance.
- **Every system is flawed.** He once thought monogamy was a trick. But each person's desires about sex and intimacy contradict each other, and between two people "the number of contradictions ... is just squared. So anything you do is going to be fucked-up." Faults in the existing system are easy to see and faults in its replacement are hard to see: open relationships create problems as bad or worse.
- **The right partner may not fit your picture.** Margaux matched nothing he had imagined: "It's not like there was this Margaux-shaped hole in my life." What worked was "something new that came from the world."

## Happiness and difficulty

Happiness is "the *most* untrivial thing" to think about, since nearly everything people do, including the consumption that drives environmental damage, is done in pursuit of it, usually in pursuit of things that don't deliver it. His friends disagree hotly. Margaux thinks difficulty is everything: work when you *don't* feel like it, because resistance means you're going somewhere new, and walk up the mountain rather than driving. Edward believes in hedonism and imagines an easy, inspired creative life; Mark works on his book only when he feels like it. Darren blames every difficulty on an unjust society. Glouberman's view: "hedonism happens not to work." Lying on the grass feels wonderful after work, and wouldn't feel good all the time. "We're always meant to do *some* meaningless striving, or at least ... we're not happy without it." Even Buddhist monks work really hard at not striving.

Running a happiness course for a dozen friends gave his circle "a common language about what the hell we thought we were trying to do in our lives." Nobody did the reading, which was great.

## Culture, ritual and belief

- **Constructed is still real.** "Just because something's socially constructed, doesn't mean it's not real." Every society *has* standards of beauty and status, and a human outside any culture isn't more true. Of the gym, a fad of our time: "I *am* of our day. I don't have to see through everything."
- **Ritual without false belief.** It's good to stop believing superstitious falsehoods. People still crave connection to "something mysterious and bigger than ourselves," and that craving is real; it "you can't just reason out of existence." His music classes aim for ritual and the feeling of magic "without having to actually believe that the rules of science are being suspended."

## Filters and safeguards

From fighting eight hundred spam emails a day:

- **Measure both errors.** "The trick isn't to block the *most* spam, it's to block the most spam while blocking the fewest legitimate messages." Every new rule risks blocking good mail. An overzealous administrator's new filter silently deleted every email with *cock* in the subject line, and the bar the residents' association was fighting was the Cock and Tail.
- **Sort by confidence into tiers.** An "almost definitely spam" folder he never checks unless hunting for a specific message; clean mail in the inbox; suspect mail in the inbox with a tag. Score each message with many independent tests and act on the score, the way SpamAssassin does.
- **Count the measure's own damage.** "The damage that's often overlooked is the damage caused by the measures themselves": lockouts, forgotten passwords and lost accounts are real security costs.
- **Train on your own data.** A Bayesian filter learns what *your* mail looks like (your friends' names, your streets, your projects), and that is the one thing spammers can't learn.

## Work and money in cultural projects

- **Audiences rarely know the economics.** Artists assume they do, and it would help if audiences had more sense of patronage.
- **Patronage.** He buys a band's CD whether or not he wants it, as a vote to keep them active. "Being a bargain hunter and being a patron are sort of incompatible," and "a bargain for the fan is often a bad deal for the artist."
- **Mix projects.** Run many projects around getting groups of people to do things together, and let some (conferences, events) pay well enough to offset the ones that pay little or lose money. "If there's not going to be money in it, what other benefits might there be? You have to be a bit more aware of why you're doing these things."
- Artists who call themselves "the poor" are making a claim "too ridiculous to even merit serious consideration."

## What art and performance are for

- **"Art is communication made in the hope that interesting miscommunications will arise."** If you have a clear message, an editorial or a nonfiction book may serve it better.
- **Skill should be a means to an end.** A display of skill for its own sake is "the *most boring* kind of art", like juggling, acrobatics or being very tall. You go to hear a smart person for what they say, not to come away thinking they're smart.
- **Improvisation is the opposite of preparation.** Studying genre clichés so you're ready for any audience suggestion is preparation. The better response is "*How great! I've never thought about this before. I'm surprised by this suggestion!*" The feeling to cultivate is being "at once very comfortable and yet having no idea what's going to happen," and "a real joy in starting a sentence and not knowing how it's going to finish." He admits he worries, needs control and keeps a careful calendar, which is why spontaneity interests him.
- **Experimental work is pure research.** Take the *experimental* label seriously: it's the research-and-development wing, producing lots of results, some of which lead to useful music. Missy Elliott's "Work It", with its abrasive abstract noises and backwards vocals, is "the useful application of all those useless experiments," like the unbreakable dinner plates that came out of the space program.
- **"Music is not in the imperative mode."** Doing one thing doesn't mean opposing another. Trampoline Hall's amateur lecturers aren't a stand against experts, and "if you write a book that takes place in Paris, it's not a statement that no book should ever take place in New York."
- **Before asking an audience to "*shut up and listen*"**, Glouberman thinks you'd better be sure it's worthwhile. Margaux answers that nobody would make anything under that rule, and nobody is forced to listen. "I think she's probably right, but I can't help feeling this way."
