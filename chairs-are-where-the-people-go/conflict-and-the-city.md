# Conflict, negotiation and the city

Glouberman's material on disputes and civic life comes mostly from the Queen-Beaconsfield Residents' Association. He formed it in Toronto to fight the bar next door, which played loud music until two-thirty most nights with its doors open, and had applied for a 120-person patio facing a residential street, open until two in the morning.

## The residents' association campaign

Use this sequence for any fight with a business, landlord or institution.

1. **Try the other party directly, and keep track.** He called the bar every night for about three months. Staff politely promised to turn the music down and then didn't, or did for fifteen minutes. When he lost his temper, walked downstairs and turned the mixer knob himself, the room went silent and he nearly ended up in a fight. Expect the anger, and the shame, especially when the people closest to you aren't bothered: the noise bothered Margaux much less than it bothered him.
2. **Map the process, and expect it to be opaque.** Information from the City was nearly impossible to get: calls went unreturned, the websites didn't say, and sources conflicted. Public-input mechanisms can be shams. To object to a liquor license you had to give your name and contact details to the owners, whom you couldn't even identify, and licenses were never denied on the basis of public input. The patio poll covered only people within five hundred meters on the street the patio faced, so on a corner lot half the people affected had no say, including him, the next-door neighbor. The ballot came with no explanation. It passed about 7 to 5 before he found out it had happened.
3. **Find the others.** "In any public matter, the concerns of a large number of people are given more weight than the concerns of one person." So the first step with a noisy bar is to find out whether others are bothered too. Ask each person you find whether they know anyone else: five became ten, then twenty, then about forty.
4. **Learn who actually lives there.** Within two blocks there were about six distinct micro-neighborhoods, each unaware of the others: artists and young people renting over the storefronts, well-off Portuguese families, young professional couples, and a big apartment building of Vietnamese families that nobody noticed. Bar customers would say "*No one lives on Queen Street.*" "You only see what's relevant to you."
5. **Build the case.** Petition and knock on doors. On the block where the poll had passed 7 to 5, they got signatures from about 90 percent of addresses, over a hundred people, while only two or three people actively supported the patio. Get signed letters from people who voted on misinformation (four of the seven "yes" voters signed one). Dig for the rule nobody mentions: two days before the hearing he found a regulation forbidding a patio within twenty-five meters of a residence. Then the councillor, who had been "balancing the interests of business and the interests of residents", agreed to oppose the patio.
6. **When you've won, consider doing better than winning.** On the day of the hearing the councillor proposed a compromise. Everyone's first response was "*Fuck you, councillor*": the owners felt entitled, having cleared every hurdle, and the residents had already won. The councillor asked each side what it actually wanted. The neighbors wanted the loud late-night music with the doors open to stop, and didn't care about a patio open at dinnertime. The owners wanted "a strong dinner business." The deal was a smaller patio that closed at eleven, in exchange for keeping the music down all the time.
7. **Do the comparison honestly.** Blocking the patio would have left every existing problem in place, since the noise bylaw was unenforceable. The owners would have seen the residents as enemies with "nothing more to lose by pissing us off," and he'd have lived next door to people who hated him. A small early patio cost the residents almost nothing and bought conditions with an **enforcement mechanism**: break them and the bar loses its patio license.
8. **Write the conditions in detail, together.** About sixteen of them took about twenty minutes, because everyone had thought about the issue so much: no music with doors or windows open; music inaudible in the adjoining apartments; staff hired to stop customers yelling; regular meetings with the neighbors; and more.
9. **Expect enforcement to take months, then keep the relationship.** After a few months he never heard music from the bar again, and the patio did well. The owners had never wanted the bar that loud; their customers had complained too, and the real problem was controlling DJs who weren't bar staff. "Everyone prefers it this way." The association still exists and has become better at helping new bars figure out how to be good neighbors.

The lesson: "something can be *better than winning* in an apparently antagonistic situation," and it is easy to "mistake damage to the other party for advantage to yourself." Anger at the owners hid the fact that their dinner business was probably good for everyone.

## Sitting on the same side of the table

Society is proud of changing power without violence. "A more advanced version of that goal is to be able to do things without antagonism." Elections run as gladiator matches and legislatures run as debates are "atavistic," even though the opponents largely want the same things.

- *Getting to Yes*: "in a negotiation it helps to literally sit on the same side of the table." The aim is to turn a negotiation from two people pushing against each other into people working toward a common solution. Wanting different things doesn't make you enemies.
- **The trap** is "*Well, fuck 'em*," the reflex when the other side does something that feels dishonest or disrespectful, even when the retaliation leaves you worse off too. The battle approach is hardwired and produces lose-lose outcomes.
- **The battle mentality** shows up when "you end up forgetting what it is that you're after and focusing instead on stopping your opponent from getting what they want." At that point "you're no longer acting even to your own advantage." It happens among friends, at work and in politics.
- **Collaborative divorce** is the legal glimmer of this: lawyers and parties work together toward a fair division instead of going to war.
- Negotiation as a field combines "intellectual, analytical problem-solving skills, mixed in with very personal stuff about people's feelings." Glouberman considers it the key to large problems like climate change: "the most important thing is figuring out how people can get better at coming to agreement."

## Good-guy, bad-guy thinking

Once a group becomes the enemy, everything it does looks suspect and everything your own side does looks fine. His example: a video edited together from the most provocative interviews at McCain-Palin rallies drew unanimous outrage on MetaFilter, a forum of otherwise reasonable people. Every ideology has some stupid people. Run the **reverse test**: if someone made the same video of the most extreme supporters on your side, would you be furious at the videographer? We see our enemies' failings "*so clearly*", sometimes where none exist, and miss our friends'.

## Manners: agreement on arbitrary rules

Manners are "a tiny little subset of ethics": "not making yourself more important than other people." They are also about rules and communication. The rule for which side of the road to drive on is arbitrary, and everyone agreeing on it is what matters. If everyone brings wine to dinner, fine; if nobody does and the host provides everything, also fine. If half the circle brings wine, resentment follows. Noise works the same way: a culture where everyone plays music all night can work, but only if everyone agrees. Without agreement, people resent both the noise and being bound by their own side of the deal. Contemporary rules are "tacit and open-ended" and change from circle to circle, so to "*I think this, my friend thinks that, who's right?*" the answer is often that nobody is. Talk to the other people involved.

**Ask for it.** If you're exhausted and want a subway seat manners say you should give up, say: "*I've had a really long day and I'm exhausted and would it be okay if I sat down?*" It's direct, and it lets them give the seat to you: "to let someone give something to you, you have to let them own it first." Grabbing it makes you "some asshole" who stole a seat; asking makes them a good person who did a favor for a nice stranger.

## Neighborhoods and the city

- **A neighborhood is like an ecosystem.** Kensington Market activists imagined that banning cars would leave them with "Kensington Market as it is now, but just with no cars." Remove one important component and everything changes, hard to predict but guessable. The butchers and fruit and spice stores that depend on cars for deliveries and for suburban immigrant customers were the traditional backbone. Removing cars would speed the existing drift toward cafés, bistros, tourist shops and used-clothing stores, an entertainment destination rather than a functional neighborhood. People can't imagine the transformation: "*It's not like it's going to turn into Yorkville*," yet Yorkville, now the city's most upscale neighborhood, was once like Kensington.
- **Be honest about the direction.** Saying that neighborhoods change, that it's silly to try to preserve things, and that you don't want to turn a place "into a museum of itself" is a coherent position. Changing something drastically while claiming it will stay the same is not. "You can't pretend you're just *allowing* change to happen—you're pushing things in a specific direction."
- **Good neighborhoods feel like people live there.** A huge noisy street party every Sunday (a drum circle, an amplified performance poet) announces that the place belongs to visitors, not residents.
- **Look for the people not being heard.** In Kensington these were tenants and immigrant shopkeepers. Some didn't speak English, and some came from places where dissent gets you into trouble, so they assumed their views would lose to "enthusiastic, educated young environmentalists." Check that a proposed change isn't "just for the benefit of the most vocal groups ... while being damaging to the people who aren't as easily heard from." It helped when organizers later brought in neighbors who had disliked the event.
- **Class blindness.** Activists cast cyclists and pedestrians as the oppressed underdogs. But getting around on foot and by bike is often a privilege: you live downtown and don't commute to a crappy job. Many drivers are working people with families who can't afford to live downtown. "It's not like Kensington Market is full of sports cars and Mercedeses."
- **Your friends' city isn't automatically the better city.** Less advertising, more cycling, street parties and art in public spaces add up to a city "specifically well suited to people who are young and well educated and able-bodied ... In other words, people just like themselves." Pushing for your own interests is fine "but it's useful to have some recognition that this is what you're doing." His grandmother, born in a shtetl with a fifth-grade education, would have hated a city of posters and graffiti; her idea of beauty meant order, control and quiet. It's unfair "to pretend that the word *beauty* applies only to *my* image of beauty." A city is "*fundamentally* a place of commerce", so objecting to advertising rests on "an imaginary and high-minded understanding of what a city is."
- **Protective bubbles are okay.** "A city is a place where you can be alone in public, and where you have that right." People on the subway between a job full of people and a home full of people have earned their quiet. Projects that try to rouse them are fixing a problem that doesn't exist.

## Nimbyism: what you may and may not control

"Nimby" shouldn't be an insult: "it's not as if a general problem in our society is that we suffer from an excess of civic engagement." Bar monocultures overrun neighborhoods whose residents lack the pride, ownership or community to take charge. Laws that let businesses alone decide how a neighborhood evolves are the real problem. A city still needs nightlife, though, and the ugly uses of Nimbyism (opposing a homeless shelter, a halfway house, people different from yourself) show why residents' say must be limited. "The best results happen from balancing different people's desires."

To decide which is which, "distinguish the things you might not like in a neighborhood from the things over which you *oughtn't* to have control."

- **Yours to regulate, to a limited degree:** what kinds of businesses operate and what gets built. A nightclub next to a home "is in the business of selling off other people's peace and quiet." Like a slaughterhouse or an iron-smelting plant on a residential street, it makes an apartment effectively unusable.
- **Not yours:** who your neighbors are (yuppies from ad agencies, ethnic minorities, rich hipsters), a gay bookstore, the trendy souvenir shop or gift store you can't stand. "Your neighborhood preferences lose against the general principles of freedom," because "limits on our control are kind of fundamental to democracy."

Some change you simply put up with "if you don't want to be a crazy person."
