# Hosting: rooms, shows, conferences, parties, classes

Glouberman's practice for any gathering. It applies "the chairs are where the people go" to each kind of room.

## Seating

"Everything is better when you're closer to the stage!" How much people liked a show correlates directly with how close they sat.

The failure case is a literary reading in a large room: a few tables spaced far apart near the stage, a couple of rows of theater seating behind them, and standing room behind that. The tables waste the space up front, so most people end up far away. Nobody wants to stand through a reading. The people standing get bored, talk to each other, and make it worse for everyone.

Seat by what the event is:

| Event | Arrangement |
|---|---|
| Cocktail party | Few chairs, if any. Put down a lot and people won't mingle. |
| Music, or anything people can talk through | Mostly standing, so people can dance, talk and move around. |
| Play | Pack people fairly close on raked seating. Darkness is fine: they don't need to see each other, and the performers don't need to see them. |
| Show where interaction with the performers or each other matters | As many people as close to the stage as possible. Pack them tighter than you'd think; they won't mind. Scatter only a few tables for drinks, since each table takes the space of someone sitting. Put some light on the audience. Ideally seat them in a quarter-circle around an area extending from the stage, so they can see they're not alone watching. |
| Conference discussion group | A circle of chairs, no table. |
| Brunch with a big group | A circle or something like one. At a long table you talk to the same five people all meal. |

Setting up chairs is easy to delegate, and "people like to set up chairs." It's even easier to get them to put the chairs away. Ask helpers to do it when you want to get people involved in a project.

## Opening instructions: the contract

At the start of every Trampoline Hall show he gives the audience instructions on how best to enjoy it:

- Sit, and sit as close to the stage as possible.
- What the show will entail, so nobody gets antsy.
- A countdown from the stage ("starting in three minutes", then "two minutes"). People think he's joking. He isn't.
- How many acts there are, roughly how long each runs, and when the breaks are. "It's much easier to pay attention if you know you're going to have to pay attention for half an hour."
- Last, encourage them to shush anyone who talks, and offer techniques: the direct, aggressive way is to turn around and shush angrily; the passive-aggressive way is to cover your mouth so nobody knows who did it.

When someone talks mid-show, several audience members shush them. The talkers then understand that the quiet is "a collective desire" and stop. The performer is spared "the terrible indignity" of telling the room to simmer down every five minutes.

## Q&A: what a good question is

Trampoline Hall's Q&As work because he spends a long time at every show explaining what a good question is. The speech:

- "A good question has to be a question." A statement with the pitch raised at the end won't fool anyone.
- "There's no such thing as a two-part question." If you think you have one, you have two questions; pick the better one.
- Notice the feeling when a question comes on. Curiosity is a good one. It's even fine to feel a little angry and want to work that out by asking the lecturer something.
- Watch for pride. If you picture yourself "becoming enormous and floating God-like above the audience, and the lecturer getting smaller and smaller in the distance," the question probably isn't that great.
- "Bad questions are welcome; their good questions are just more welcome."

It is long, ridiculous and sounds like jokes, and it works. Its real effect is "to let the audience know that they're expected to ask good questions."

A related format is Darren O'Donnell's show *Q&A*, which Glouberman adapted as *Interviews with Harvard Alumni Who Feel Weird About Harvard*. Audience members are chosen at random, one at a time, to sit onstage, and anyone can interview them. The person onstage can refuse to answer any question, and the design encourages difficult, uncomfortable questions. Cameras project their faces on a big screen. "In five minutes they would say things they had not said in four whole years."

## Conferences

People come to a conference to meet others who share their interests, learn from each other and solve problems together. Then they sit in a room of fifty while one person reads a paper. That structure is "insanely atavistic". It "ignores the existence of the *printing press*." With ninety-nine smart, passionate people sitting silent and eighty of them bored, the potential goes to waste. A lecture is sometimes good, but it shouldn't be the only thing a conference does.

He runs **unconferences** (he dislikes the name). They are highly participatory, and the attendees have a great deal of agency and control. An **open space meeting** is the simplest self-organized structure; an unconference usually includes one alongside other structures.

### Open space meeting

1. Size a grid to the day: say 100 people, five time slots and six rooms make a five-by-six grid on the wall.
2. Ask: "*Who has something they want to talk about or learn about or work on?*" Each answer goes on the grid, so every item has at least one person who really cares about it.
3. Say: "*If there's something you really feel needs to be discussed today—if it's not up there, it's because you haven't put it there ... it's your job to put it there.*"
4. Give each topic a time slot and a room. People pick the rooms where the topics they care most about are being discussed. Someone alone in a room can think about the topic alone or join another room.
5. Recast the lonely session: if only one person comes to yours, "you guys have *found* each other." That is a huge success.

The groups stay small, so almost everyone talks, and nobody spends months on a committee guessing at needs it can't anticipate. "A conference full of smart, passionate delegates can do a lot more and make better choices than a small conference-planning committee can." He hears "*It won't work with this group*" constantly, "but it always works." It worked with frontline workers in a hierarchical health care organization going through cutbacks, people others assumed weren't initiative-takers.

### Seeding conversations

- **Random groups first.** "*Stand up and find four people you don't know.*" Letting people form their own groups works better than assigning them, and it energizes the room.
- **Give each group a personal question before the contested one.** At a copyright-and-art conference full of opinionated artists, open-source activists, lawyers and academics, the obvious opener was the legal debate of the moment. Instead, groups described a cultural product they really coveted as adolescents: a record they were dying to hear, a book, a show. That reminds people why the subject matters to them, and it builds personal intimacy before they find out the person across the table holds the opposite position on the bill.
- **Reshuffle and repeat.** Three conversations take about an hour. By then everyone has met a dozen people, everyone has talked, and people feel they know each other.
- **Let people sort themselves with minimal instruction.** At a conference of immigrants trained as professionals but unable to practice in Canada, he asked people to stand with others from the same continent, then to regroup by professional training. The room looked like a stock exchange floor. Fifteen minutes turned into an hour, because for many this was the first time in ten years they had stood with other architects and been identified as one. He had to reschedule everything he had planned.

### The "one over n" rule

The one discussion tip he gives: "*If you're in a group of five people, the natural amount of time for you to be talking is about a fifth of the time.*" People laugh because it's obvious. There are good reasons to talk more and good reasons to talk less, but "*if you are talking much more than that or much less than that, at the very least just stop and ask yourself if you have a good reason.*" Dominant people still dominate sometimes. Making the group aware of the goal still helps a lot.

### The cocktail-party mistake

Organizers spend their time on panelists, the dinner menu and the theme, and when they think about people meeting at all, they program a cocktail party. That is "a *terrible* way for people to meet each other." An outgoing person might meet ten people and still miss the ones they most need. His worst case was a conference of nothing but lectures and panels, with lunch left to attendees off campus, and the one social event held as a fund-raiser for an extra fifty dollars. "There was one thing in the whole conference that was *actually* about getting people to meet each other, and they put a barrier in front of it!" People assume the social side will work itself out. Design it.

## Parties

"People want to *get* things out of parties." That can be sex, getting drunk or high, dancing, new friends, work opportunities, or a chance to behave inappropriately. Anyone who says they go to parties for no reason, or who hosts but doesn't want guests trying to get something, "is lying, kidding themselves, or dumb." If you no longer want anything from parties, stop going; you won't have fun.

As a guest:

- You don't have to talk as much as you think. To be thought clever, "just say one or two smart things."
- In a conversation, you can listen. People always appreciate it.
- Standing alone feels awkward, but nobody is paying attention to you. (Realizing that drains some of the party's energy, which comes largely from "people's fear of being seen not talking to anyone.")
- "The last six hours of a party usually aren't much fun."
- Don't go out of obligation to the host. It's bad for the party, and there are less soul-draining ways to show affection.

### Bars and drinking

Events in bars are "in the drinks business." Late starts and long gaps between sets are boring, and the boredom sells drinks. His games night kept people engaged from arrival to departure, so they never bought drinks out of boredom and the bar made no money. The organizer of a bar event has to account for bar sales. Drunk people can't really play games: teams fight more and stop paying attention. For older people, "drugs and alcohol just give you greater tolerance for a boring time."

## Conversation versus storytelling

"A story isn't a conversation. It's a monologue." It can't be interrupted, and it has to end where the teller wants. "The best conversationalists are people who are hoping to end up somewhere they didn't expect." Rehearsing anecdotes to impress people at a party means "sacrificing the potential pleasure of real conversation in the interest of scoring points." "The most pleasing thing you can find yourself saying in a conversation is something you haven't said before." People can talk for a very long time about what they're doing without ever saying what they think they're up to. Ask about that.

## Running classes and series

- **Screen out people who would be disappointed.** The people you want are often exactly the ones nervous about coming. Send a questionnaire beforehand ("*Are you okay being in a situation where you're going to make and listen to a lot of discordant sounds?*") and ask what each person expects to get out of it. When someone's expectation doesn't fit (getting better at jazz, writing funny songs for improv comedy), phone them: "*I want to make sure that this class will provide what you're looking for.*" Point them to a video of fifteen people screaming in a room.
- **Price.** He wants money, a crowd and a mix of people all at once, so the price is "$360 or whatever you want." Tell well-off students that paying more subsidizes the artists and writers they enjoy having in the class. A surprising number pay full price, and some pay more. Be strict about the contract: no refunds for dropping out, and installments only by postdated checks given up front. He quietly refunds someone who hates the first class, but he doesn't advertise it.
- **Skip the round of introductions.** For a while, all people know is "*the person with the red hair who likes to make deep, guttural noises.*" That innocence is fragile and worth keeping. The bond comes from shared experience: how people use their bodies and voices, and when they're bossy.
- **Say "come anyhow."** Anticipation is dread (he feels it too, and secretly hopes nobody shows up) even by the sixth class of eight, like the first moment of getting into a lake. When someone calls in stressed or "not quite in the right mood," tell them to come anyway. They always get over the mood they diagnosed in themselves.
- **Expect split verdicts.** In a good class, nearly everything is someone's favorite and someone's least favorite thing. Don't imagine that the whole group loved or hated something. Tell students too: a disliked exercise feels different once you know someone else is enjoying it.
- **Keep what it's for unspoken.** He avoids saying what an exercise or a class is for, hoping people get something from it he didn't intend. Music classes are "for whatever music is for." A student who refused to treat the class as therapy for a bad mood pleased him.
- **Establish authority only when you need to.** The one time he did, at a charades class where he was nervous about his qualifications, he guessed every clue first on the first day, "like some old martial arts instructor."

## Letting the audience in

John Zorn's game piece *Cobra* puts players onstage making cryptic hand signals and waving cards, then tells the audience not to care how it works. Glouberman thought it would be more fun if the audience knew what was going on. At his games night with Joe Sorbara's band:

1. The band played *Cobra* the usual way, "in all its noisy, inaccessible mystery."
2. The band played demonstration rounds while his program, the Cobratron (half scoreboard, half educational flash cards), projected a live play-by-play explaining the game.
3. At intermission he said: "*Come back and we'll make some sounds together for a few minutes, and if you like that, you can stick around and learn Cobra ... If you don't like it, which is a really, really normal response, then you can leave, and that's okay.*"
4. About three-quarters came back and did noise exercises. A few more left. The rest learned the game in small groups and were playing by the end of the night, thrilled to be watching each other and applauding wildly.

People began finding beauty in what had sounded like cacophony and developing aesthetic preferences. "The game was a lot more interesting to the audience once they had some insight into what was going on." A permission to leave also builds a self-selected room.

## Value and money

Attention isn't money. Trampoline Hall was "a little phenomenon," but it ran once a month for about eighty people at five dollars a head and lost money. Its secret is that people sense they get great value: a handmade ticket, a program, three lecturers, the curator shaking their hands at the door, and a good chance the host acknowledges them individually. Projects like this work because the people making them want something other than money.
