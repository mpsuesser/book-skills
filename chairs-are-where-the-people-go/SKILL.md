# The Chairs Are Where the People Go

Misha Glouberman hosts the Trampoline Hall lecture series (amateurs lecture on random subjects in a bar, then he runs the Q&A). He teaches paid classes in charades, in theater improv without the theater, and in making noise music together, and he runs unconferences. He also founded a residents' association to fight the bar next door. Sheila Heti typed while he talked. His recurring subject is getting groups of people to do things together, and the thoughtfulness about people that this takes. He is opinionated and specific, and he always sees the other person's side. Advise in that register: name the concrete move, the exact words to say to a room, or the rule of the game, and quote his phrasings where they fit.

## How to use this skill

1. **Place the situation** in one of the branches under "Where to go" and read that file before answering. A situation that spans two branches (a class that is also an event, a dispute that is also a leadership problem) gets both files.
2. **Run the core ideas below** against the situation. Each one ends in a question. Answer every question that applies to the user's case.
3. **Answer with the book's move.** That means a procedure, a game, a script to say aloud or a distinction that settles the matter. Done when every recommendation is something Glouberman actually says, done concretely enough that the user could act on it tonight.

## Core ideas

These recur across the whole book. Use them as the lens on every situation.

### The chairs are where the people go

"There's a thoughtlessness in how people consider their audience that's reflected in how they set up chairs." Every structure (the seating, the schedule, the agenda, the price, the opening announcement) either serves the people who came or ignores them. Design starts from what they came for and what they will actually experience: standing at the back of a reading, they get bored and talk, and their bad time makes everyone's worse. Conferences get designed as if people travel across the country to hear a paper read aloud, when what they come for is to meet each other. Bars get designed to sell drinks, so the boredom is part of the business model. *Ask: what did these people come for, and does each arrangement help them get it?*

### Don't pretend there is no leader

People like to pretend they're not in charge when they are, especially in creative and activist groups, where the official story is collaboration but the founder writes the songs. The pretense is "born out of a bad politeness": the belief that leading is mean, or a fear of being responsible. The leader also misreads the group. Most people who join *want* someone to provide a vision and make the decisions; they don't want to make them all. A leader who hands over power and then waits for initiative ends up disappointed. The lie surfaces later, over who goes on tour and how the money gets split. Honest roles keep the room for objection, because "at least you know who to direct your objection to." "If you don't say the difficult thing early, it really fucks you up later on." Leaders do a service: they make the decisions, they're accountable, they take the blame, they do more of the work. *Ask: who is actually in charge here, and has anyone said so out loud?*

### It's a dialogue

From charades: "remember that the other person does not know what you're acting out." The common failure is to act something out in a way that would only make sense if you already knew the answer, then repeat the same gesture "but more exasperated." The fix is "an acceptance of the fact that the other person does *not* know." Both sides keep responding. The guesser throws out lots of guesses instead of waiting to be sure. The actor changes approach based on what's getting through. The same holds for conducting, where the conductor seems in control but both people are authors, and for any game where control is shared. *Ask: is each side responding to what the other is actually receiving?*

### Intention, open to surprise

True improvisation is "letting yourself be surprised and letting yourself be off-balance." The goal is to "develop skills so you can respond well to being off-balance, and especially so you can enjoy being off-balance." Being prepared for everything is the opposite. Want the outcome exactly your way and the game is frustrating; have no attachment to it and the game is boring. "The best thing is to have an intention, but also to be open to surprise." Fear of failure blocks surprise, because you rush back to the path you planned. Games help, since failure is built into them: "If you miss the ball playing baseball, it doesn't mean you're playing baseball wrong. It just means you're playing baseball." *Ask: where is this person protecting themselves from surprise?*

### Better than winning

From the residents' association fight: a compromise turned out to be "a *better* outcome than the victory we had imagined." Watch "the ease with which you can mistake damage to the other party for advantage to yourself." Negotiate as if you "literally sit on the same side of the table" (*Getting to Yes*), turning two people pushing against each other into people working toward a common solution. "Just because someone is your opponent doesn't mean that their defeat is always your victory." *Ask: what does each side actually want, and is anyone fighting just to stop the other side from getting something?*

### Say the agreement out loud

Many frictions come from people carrying different unstated expectations. Manners are mostly arbitrary rules, like which side of the road to drive on, and "mostly what matters is that there be agreement." So Glouberman tells an audience exactly what the show will entail, says when it will start, and tells them they may shush talkers. That is "a kind of contract." He sees his parents on an agreed day every week, so there is never a negotiation and nobody feels guilty. His class price is "$360 or whatever you want" with no refunds: "the contract is binding, it's just up to that person to choose what the terms of the contract are." *Ask: what expectation is unstated here, and who could state it?*

### Doing beats watching

The valuable thing in his college improv troupe "was the experience that people in the group were having," not what the audience got. Laughter in an improv class is the real thing, and a comedy show is "a bottled version" of it. So he builds events with no audience, where people make the sounds and play the games themselves. Displays of skill are "the *most boring* kind of art", because "virtuosity has only one thing to say ... *Look at how good I am.*" *Ask: could these people be doing the thing rather than watching it?*

### Think of it as a game

"A game has rules, it has goals, it has at least one player and a beginning and an end, there are elements of it that are arbitrary, and there has to be something about it that's not real." When frustrated, recast the situation as one: his neighborhood activism became a video game where he was trying to make certain patterns of email appear. Then you get less upset when you fail, you are more effective because you're not upset, and the opponent is not a bad person: "in a sport or a game, the other person is *necessarily* trying to do the opposite thing from you." *Ask: would this go better played as a game?*
