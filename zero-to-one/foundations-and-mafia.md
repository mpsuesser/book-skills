# Foundations, team and culture

## Thiel's law

**"A startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed."** Beginnings are qualitatively different from what follows: in its first microseconds the universe expanded by a factor of 10³⁰ under different physical laws; the compromises of the Constitutional Convention have been hard to change ever since (17 amendments after the Bill of Rights; California and Alaska still have equal Senate seats despite a more than 50-fold population gap). Companies are the same. Bad early decisions, such as the wrong partners or the wrong hires, are very hard to correct, and it may take a crisis on the order of bankruptcy before anyone tries. "As a founder, your first job is to get the first things right, because you cannot build a great company on a flawed foundation."

## Founding matrimony

"The first and most crucial decision you make is whom to start it with." Choosing a co-founder is like getting married, and founder conflict is as ugly as divorce. Optimism abounds at the start and it's unromantic to think soberly about what could go wrong, so people don't; if founders develop irreconcilable differences, the company is the victim. Luke Nosek's first startup paired a brilliant, eccentric thinker with an MBA type chasing the '90s gold rush, who had met at a networking event: "no better than marrying the first person you meet at the slot machines in Vegas." It blew up.

**Check a founding team**: technical abilities and complementary skill sets matter, but how well the founders know each other and how well they work together matter just as much. "Founders should share a prehistory before they start a company together—otherwise they're just rolling dice."

## Ownership, possession and control

Working alone guarantees alignment but limits what you can build ("It's very hard to go from 0 to 1 without a team"). Hiring just the right people and letting them flourish without structure ignores Madison's point that men aren't angels; that is why executives manage, directors govern, and founders' and investors' claims are formally defined. You need good people who get along and a structure that keeps them aligned for the long term.

To anticipate misalignment, separate three things:

- **Ownership**: who legally owns a company's equity?
- **Possession**: who actually runs the company on a day-to-day basis?
- **Control**: who formally governs the company's affairs?

A typical startup splits ownership among founders, employees and investors; managers and employees have possession; a board of founders and investors has control. In theory ownership rewards, possession empowers, and board oversight adds perspective. In practice every division multiplies opportunities for misalignment.

- **The DMV** is misalignment at its extreme: the citizens' ownership is fictional, the clerks enjoy real possession of their small-time powers, and the governor and legislature with nominal control can't change anything. "Accountable to nobody, the DMV is misaligned with everybody."
- **Big corporations** misalign ownership and possession. A General Motors CEO owns a trivial share of the stock, so he rewards himself through possession (salary, corporate jet), which good quarterly results protect. Stock paid for short-term performance makes cutting costs more lucrative than investing in a plan that creates more value far in the future.
- **Startups**: founders usually hold both ownership and possession, so most conflicts erupt between **ownership and control**, founders against investors on the board. The potential grows as interests diverge: a board member may want an early IPO to score a win for his venture firm while the founders want to stay private and grow.

## The board

"In the boardroom, less is more." A small board communicates, reaches consensus and oversees effectively, and for that same reason can forcefully oppose management in a conflict, so choose wisely: every member matters, and even one problem director causes pain and may jeopardize the company.

- **"A board of three is ideal."**
- **Never exceed five** unless the company is public (regulation effectively mandates larger public boards, averaging nine).
- **A huge board is the worst choice.** A nonprofit with dozens of directors looks well supported but has no effective oversight; it "merely provides cover for whatever microdictator actually runs the organization."

## On the bus or off the bus

Everyone you involve with your company should be involved **full-time**. Outside lawyers and accountants are the usual exceptions. "Anyone who doesn't own stock options or draw a regular salary from your company is fundamentally misaligned": at the margin they lean toward claiming value now rather than helping create more later. So consultants don't work, part-time employees don't work, and even remote work should be avoided, because "misalignment can creep in whenever colleagues aren't together full-time, in the same place, every day." The decision to bring someone on is binary. As Ken Kesey said, you're either on the bus or off the bus.

## Cash is not king

Ask any founder seeking investment how much he intends to pay himself. "A company does better the less it pays the CEO," one of the clearest patterns across hundreds of startups.

- **No CEO of an early-stage, venture-backed startup should take more than $150,000 a year in salary**, whatever he made at Google and whatever his mortgage and tuition bills.
- At $300,000 a CEO "risks becoming more like a politician than a founder": high pay makes him defend the status quo along with his salary instead of surfacing problems and fixing them aggressively. A cash-poor executive focuses on the value of the company as a whole.
- **Low CEO pay sets the standard.** Box's Aaron Levie paid himself less than anyone else; four years in he still lived two blocks from headquarters in a one-bedroom apartment furnished with a mattress, and every employee noticed and emulated the commitment. Alternatively, a CEO can take the highest salary, as long as it is still modest: it then sets a ceiling on cash compensation.
- Cash is pure optionality, and high cash pay teaches people to claim value from the company as it exists. A cash bonus is slightly better (it's contingent on a job well done), but even incentive pay encourages short-term thinking and value grabbing. "Any kind of cash is more about the present than the future."

## Vested interests

"Equity is the one form of compensation that can effectively orient people toward creating value in the future." Allocate it carefully so it creates commitment rather than conflict.

- **Equal shares are usually a mistake**: people differ in talents, responsibilities and opportunity costs, so equal amounts seem arbitrary. Unequal amounts seem unfair too. There is no formula that avoids resentment, and resentment at this stage can kill a company.
- **It gets harder over time.** Early employees take more risk and usually get the most equity, but later ones may be more crucial. An eBay secretary who joined in 1996 might have made 200 times more than her industry-veteran boss who joined in 1999; the graffiti artist who painted Facebook's walls in 2005 got stock worth $200 million, a talented engineer joining in 2010 perhaps $2 million.
- **Keep the details secret.** Perfect fairness is impossible; a company-wide email listing everyone's stake "would be like dropping a nuclear bomb on your office."
- **Equity reveals commitment.** Most people don't want it: it isn't liquid, it's tied to one company, and it's worthless if the company fails. (A PayPal consultant's one successful negotiation was his own $5,000 daily cash salary; he refused options.) These limits are what make it powerful: anyone who prefers part ownership to cash "reveals a preference for the long term and a commitment to increasing your company's value in the future."

## Extending the founding

The founding moment happens once: only at the start can you set the rules that align people toward future value. But there is a second sense of founding. The most valuable companies keep the openness to invention characteristic of beginnings, so the founding "lasts as long as a company is creating new things, and it ends when creation stops." Get the founding right and you can steer the company's distant future "toward the creation of new things instead of the stewardship of inherited success," and perhaps extend its founding indefinitely.

## Culture: the mechanics of mafia

Picture the ideal culture as a list of perks: beanbags, Ping-Pong tables, free massages, sushi chefs, yoga, pets. It has none of the substance, "and without substance perks don't work." You can't get anything meaningful from an interior decorator, an HR consultant or a branding specialist. "'Company culture' doesn't exist apart from the company itself: no company has a culture; every company is a culture. A startup is a team of people on a mission, and a good culture is just what that looks like on the inside."

### Beyond professionalism

The early PayPal team became the **"PayPal Mafia"**: after the sale to eBay for $1.5 billion in 2002, alumni founded or co-founded SpaceX, Tesla, LinkedIn, YouTube, Yelp, Yammer and Palantir, all worth more than $1 billion each. The culture was strong enough to transcend the company. It wasn't built by sorting résumés for the most talented people. At a New York law firm, Thiel saw impressive individuals with oddly thin relationships, spending all day together with little to say outside the office. A merely professional, transactional view of work "is worse than cold: it's not even rational." Time is your most valuable asset, so "If you can't count durable relationships among the fruits of your time at work, you haven't invested your time well—even in purely financial terms." PayPal hired people who would enjoy working together: talented, but even more, excited to work specifically with this team.

### Recruiting conspirators

Recruiting is a core competency and should never be outsourced. You need people who will work together cohesively after they're hired. The first four or five may come for large equity stakes or high-profile responsibilities. The question that matters: **"Why should the 20th employee join your company?"** More pointedly: why would someone join as your 20th engineer when she could go to Google for more money and prestige?

- **Bad answers**, because every company makes them: "Your stock options will be worth more here than elsewhere." "You'll get to work with the smartest people in the world." "You can help solve the world's most challenging problems."
- **Good answers** are specific to your company, and come in two kinds:
  - **Mission**: not why the mission is important in general, but "why you're doing something important that no one else is going to get done." At PayPal, a recruit excited by a new digital currency to replace the U.S. dollar was worth talking to; one who wasn't, wasn't a fit.
  - **Team**: the most engaged recruit will ask, "Are these the kind of people I want to work with?" Explain why the company is a unique match for him personally; if you can't, he's probably not the right match.
- **Don't fight the perk war.** Anyone swayed more by free laundry pickup or pet day care would be a bad addition. Cover the basics like health insurance, then promise what no one else can: "the opportunity to do irreplaceable work on a unique problem alongside great people." You can't be the Google of 2014 on pay and perks, but you can be the Google of 1999.

### Different in the same way

*From the outside, everyone in your company should be different in the same way.* Tech workers in branded T-shirts look the same as their co-workers and different from everyone else: "a tribe of like-minded people fiercely devoted to the company's mission." Max Levchin's rule: make the early staff as personally similar as possible, because small teams with limited resources must move fast, which is easier when everyone shares an understanding of the world. The early PayPal team were all the same kind of nerd (science fiction, *Cryptonomicon* as required reading, the capitalist *Star Wars* over the communist *Star Trek*) and, above all, all obsessed with a digital currency controlled by individuals instead of governments. Looks and nationality didn't matter; equal obsession did.

### Do one thing

*On the inside, every individual should be sharply distinguished by her work.* Matching talents to tasks as an optimization problem breaks down quickly, because roles change fast and because assignments are also about relationships between employees. Thiel's best move as a manager: **make every person responsible for doing just one thing**, each person's thing unique, and evaluate each person only on it. It began as a way to simplify managing, and turned out to reduce conflict: most fights inside a company happen when colleagues compete for the same responsibilities, a high risk in a startup where roles are fluid. Removing internal competition lets people build relationships beyond professionalism, and internal peace lets the startup survive at all. "Internal conflict is like an autoimmune disease: the technical cause of death may be pneumonia, but the real cause remains hidden from plain view."

### Of cults and consultants

In a cult, members spend time only with each other, abandon the outside world, and gain belonging and perhaps esoteric "truths." Take cultures of extreme dedication seriously: is a lukewarm attitude to work a sign of mental health? The opposite extreme is a consulting firm like Accenture, with no distinctive mission and consultants dropping in and out of companies they have no long-term connection to. Every culture sits on one spectrum:

**consultants (nihilism) ←→ cults (dogmatism)**, with 0 to 1 companies toward the cult end.

The best startups are slightly less extreme kinds of cults. The difference: cults are fanatically *wrong* about something important; people at a successful startup are fanatically *right* about something outsiders have missed. You won't learn those secrets from consultants, and it doesn't matter if your company makes no sense to conventional professionals. "Better to be called a cult—or even a mafia."
