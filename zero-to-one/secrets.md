# Secrets

## Conventions, secrets, mysteries

Every famous idea was once a secret: the relationship between a triangle's sides was unknown for millennia until Pythagoras worked it out (to learn it, you joined his vegetarian cult). Now it is a **convention**, a simple truth taught to grade schoolers: important, but it gives no edge.

Three zones on one line:

- **Conventions**: easy.
- **Secrets**: hard, but doable.
- **Mysteries**: impossible. Is string theory a hard secret or an impossible mystery? "You can achieve difficult things, but you can't achieve the impossible."

Contrarian thinking makes sense only if the world still has secrets left to give up. Every correct answer to "what valuable company is nobody building?" is necessarily a secret: "something important and unknown, something hard to do but doable." If many secrets remain, many world-changing companies remain to be started.

## Why people stop looking

The Unabomber's manifesto divided human goals into three: those satisfied with minimal effort, those satisfied with serious effort, and those that cannot be satisfied however hard one tries: the easy, the hard and the impossible. Kaczynski argued that modern people are depressed because all the hard problems are solved, leaving only the easy and the impossible, and wanted to destroy technology so people could start over. His loss of faith in the technological frontier is common. Hipster faux-vintage photography, handlebar mustaches and vinyl look back to a time of optimism: if everything worth doing has been done, "you may as well feign an allergy to achievement and become a barista." *Hipster or Unabomber?*

**Fundamentalists** of every kind allow no middle ground for hard questions: there are easy truths children rattle off and unexplainable mysteries, and the zone of hard truths between them is heresy. Religious fundamentalism; environmentalism (protect the environment; beyond that, Mother Nature knows best); free-market fundamentalism (anyone can look up a price, but whether it makes sense is not to be second-guessed).

Physical frontiers have receded (no blank spaces left on the map), and **four social trends** have rooted out belief in secrets:

1. **Incrementalism.** From childhood we're taught to proceed one small step at a time and get no credit for learning what's not on the test; academics chase large numbers of trivial publications instead of new frontiers.
2. **Risk aversion.** A secret hasn't been vetted by the mainstream, so seeking one means risking being wrong. Being lonely but right is hard; "The prospect of being lonely and wrong can be unbearable."
3. **Complacency.** Elites have the most freedom to explore but believe in secrets least, since they can collect rents on what's already been done. Elite schools tell entrants they are set for life, "the kind of thing that's true only if you don't believe it."
4. **"Flatness."** If the world is one homogeneous, highly competitive market, wouldn't someone in the faceless global talent pool have found it already? That doubt stops people from starting to look.

The optimistic description of the result: "today, you can't start a cult." Fewer crazy cults is a gain, bought at the price of "our sense of wonder at secrets left to be discovered."

## The world according to convention

To disbelieve in secrets is to believe all great questions are solved. That would mean a perfect understanding of justice: "a wrongful practice persists only when most people don't perceive it to be unjust," as slavery's evil was once a secret held by a small minority of abolitionists. No secrets would mean no hidden injustices. In economics, disbelief in secrets becomes faith in efficient markets, yet bubbles show extraordinary inefficiencies ("the more people believe in efficiency, the bigger the bubbles get"); in 2005 Greenspan saw only "signs of froth in local markets," and the 2008 crisis wiped out trillions.

**A company that stops believing in secrets: Hewlett-Packard.** HP was worth $9 billion in 1990, invented through the decade (the DeskJet 500C, the first affordable color printer, 1991; the OmniBook superportable laptop, 1993; the OfficeJet, the first all-in-one printer/fax/copier, 1994), and was worth $135 billion by mid-2000. From late 1999, while running a branding campaign around the imperative to "invent," it stopped inventing: HP Services (a glorified consulting and support shop) in 2001, the Compaq merger in 2002, and a $70 billion market cap by 2005. The board split between Tom Perkins, an engineer who thought the board should identify the most promising new technologies and have HP build them, and chairwoman Patricia Dunn, a banker who thought charting future technology beyond the board's competence and wanted a night watchman's role (was the accounting proper, were people following the rules?). Dunn won; leaks followed, then illegal wiretaps to find the leaker, and the board was disgraced. "Having abandoned the search for technological secrets, HP obsessed over gossip." By late 2012 it was worth $23 billion.

## The case for secrets

"You can't find secrets without looking for them." Fermat's Last Theorem resisted 358 years of effort; Andrew Wiles worked on it from 1986, kept it secret until 1993, and proved it in 1995. He needed brilliance and also faith in secrets: "If you think something hard is impossible, you'll never even start trying to achieve it. Belief in secrets is an effective truth."

Many secrets remain, but "they will yield only to relentless searchers": cures for cancer, dementia and the diseases of age; new energy that ends conflict over fossil fuels; faster travel; settling new frontiers off the planet. In business, "Great companies can be built on open but unsuspected secrets about how the world works." Airbnb saw untapped supply and unaddressed demand in unoccupied rooms where others saw nothing; Lyft and Uber saw a billion-dollar business in connecting people who want to go places with people willing to drive them, beyond the convention of licensed taxis and limousines. That such companies (Facebook too) are underestimated for their simplicity is itself an argument for secrets: if insights that look elementary in retrospect support valuable businesses, many great companies remain to be started.

## How to find secrets

Two kinds:

- **Secrets of nature** exist all around us; find them by studying some undiscovered aspect of the physical world.
- **Secrets about people** are things people don't know about themselves or hide because they don't want others to know.

So ask two questions: **"What secrets is nature not telling you? What secrets are people not telling you?"**

Natural secrets get overrated because their seekers sound authoritative; knowing the most fundamental truths, physics PhDs think they know all truths. (A physics PhD interviewing at PayPal cut Thiel off halfway through the first question: "Stop! I already know what you're going to ask!" He was wrong, and it was "the easiest no-hire decision I've ever made.") Secrets about people are underappreciated because you don't need years of education to ask the questions that uncover them: **"What are people not allowed to talk about? What is forbidden or taboo?"**

The two searches can reach the same truth. The monopoly secret (competition and capitalism are opposites) shows up empirically in a quantitative study of corporate profits, and humanly by asking what people running companies are not allowed to say: monopolists downplay their monopoly and competitors exaggerate their uniqueness.

**"The best place to look for secrets is where no one else is looking."** Schooling imparts conventional wisdom, so ask: **are there any fields that matter but haven't been standardized and institutionalized?** Physics matters but is set in its ways; astrology is unstandardized but doesn't matter. Nutrition matters to everybody, yet you can't major in it at Harvard, top scientists go elsewhere, the big studies are 30 or 40 years old and mostly flawed, and the low-fat, grain-heavy food pyramid was probably more lobbying than science. "We know more about the physics of faraway stars than we know about human nutrition": hard, but not obviously impossible, exactly the kind of field that could yield secrets.

## What to do with secrets

Tell anyone, or keep it? Some secrets are more dangerous than others; Faust warns that mankind has always crucified and burned the few who showed the crowd what they knew. "Unless you have perfectly conventional beliefs, it's rarely a good idea to tell everybody everything that you know."

Tell **whoever you need to, and no more.** The golden mean between telling nobody and telling everybody is a company. "Every great business is built around a secret that's hidden from the outside. A great company is a conspiracy to change the world; when you share your secret, the recipient becomes a fellow conspirator." The well-trodden road has no end in sight, but round the corner there may wait "A new road or a secret gate." Take the hidden paths.
