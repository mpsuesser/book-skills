# Man and machine

## Complements, not substitutes

As mature industries stagnate, information technology has become synonymous with "technology." Computers beat Kasparov (Deep Blue, 1997) and Ken Jennings (Watson, 2011), and self-driving cars are on the road. Futurists seem to hope computers will replace people ("Software is eating the world"; productivity by getting rid of people); Luddites would stop building technology to avoid replacement. Both share the premise that better computers will replace human workers. The premise is wrong: **"computers are complements for humans, not substitutes. The most valuable businesses of coming decades will be built by entrepreneurs who seek to empower people rather than try to make them obsolete."**

## Substitution vs. complementarity

People fear technology as a replay of globalization (Perot's "giant sucking sound," now heard from server farms instead of factories in Tijuana). But "people compete for jobs and for resources; computers compete for neither."

| | Supply (of labor) | Demand (for resources) |
|---|---|---|
| **Globalization** (other humans) | Substitution: "The world is flat." | Mimetic consumer competition |
| **Technology** (better computers) | Mostly complementary | Machines don't demand: all value goes to people |

- **Globalization means substitution.** Free trade promises wealth from specializing by comparative advantage, and gains are greatest when advantages differ widely, but the global supply of workers willing to do repetitive tasks for tiny wages is enormous. On the demand side, people want the same resources: cheaper Chinese toys come with dearer gasoline for millions of new Chinese motorists; peasants with secure calories want pork instead of grain; "all oligarchs have the same taste in Cristal."
- **Technology means complementarity.** Computers are far more different from people than any two people are from each other. People have **intentionality**: "we form plans and make decisions in complicated situations," but struggle with enormous amounts of data. Computers excel at efficient data processing but struggle with basic judgments any human finds simple. In 2012 a Google supercomputer with 16,000 CPUs scanned 10 million YouTube thumbnails and learned to identify a cat with 75% accuracy; an average four-year-old does it flawlessly. Humans and computers are "categorically different," so gains from working with computers far exceed gains from trade with people. We don't trade with computers any more than with livestock or lamps: "computers are tools, not rivals." On the demand side, computers want only a nominal amount of electricity, so we get a hyperspecialized partner without competing with it for resources. "Properly understood, technology is the one way for us to escape competition in a globalizing world."

## Complementary businesses

**PayPal's Igor.** In mid-2000 PayPal was losing upwards of $10 million a month to credit card fraud, across hundreds or thousands of transactions a minute, too many for human review. Max Levchin's mathematicians studied the fraud and wrote software to cancel bogus transactions automatically, but within an hour or two the thieves adapted; the software couldn't adapt to an adaptive enemy. The fraudsters fooled the algorithms but not human analysts. So PayPal rebuilt it as a **hybrid**: the computer flags the most suspicious transactions on a well-designed user interface, and human operators make the final judgment. With Igor (named after a Russian fraudster who bragged PayPal could never stop him), PayPal turned its first quarterly profit in Q1 2002, against a $29.3 million quarterly loss a year earlier, and the FBI asked to use it. This man-machine symbiosis kept PayPal in business, though almost no one saw it.

**Palantir.** If humans and computers together get dramatically better results than either alone, what other businesses can be built on that principle? Palantir (founded 2004) applies PayPal's hybrid approach to terrorist networks and financial fraud: software that helps people "extract insight from divergent sources of information." It was on track for $1 billion in sales in 2014. Neither spies nor computers alone can make us safe. The CIA privileges humans and its analysts drown in noise; the NSA prioritizes computers, which can process vast data but cannot determine whether someone is plotting an attack. Palantir analyzes the data it is fed and flags suspicious activity for a trained analyst. Its users have predicted IED placements in Afghanistan, prosecuted insider trading, taken down the largest child pornography ring in the world, helped the CDC fight foodborne disease, and saved banks and government hundreds of millions of dollars a year in fraud. The software made it possible, but the human analysts, prosecutors, scientists and financial professionals mattered even more.

**Professionals.** A lawyer must frame a solution differently for a client, opposing counsel and a judge; a doctor must marry clinical understanding with explaining it to a non-expert patient; a teacher must tailor instruction to each student's interests and learning style. Computers may do some of these tasks but can't combine them. "Better technology in law, medicine, and education won't replace professionals; it will allow them to do even more."

**LinkedIn.** Founded in 2003, LinkedIn didn't poll recruiters for discrete pain points, and didn't try to replace them. Recruiting is part detective work, part sales (scrutinize history, assess motives and compatibility, persuade the best to join), and replacing all of it with a computer is impossible. LinkedIn transformed how recruiters work: more than 97% use its search and filtering, and hundreds of millions of professionals use it to manage their personal brands. Had it tried to replace recruiters, it wouldn't have a business.

## The ideology of computer science

People miss complementarity because of training. Software engineers work on replacing human effort because that's what they're taught; academics publish within a discipline, which for computer science means reducing human capabilities to specialized tasks computers can be trained to conquer one by one.

- **"Machine learning"** evokes replacement; its boosters believe computers can learn almost any task given enough training data. Netflix and Amazon recommendations improve with more data; Google Translate gives rough but serviceable translations in 80 languages, not by understanding language but by extracting statistical patterns from a huge corpus.
- **"Big data"** reflects the mistaken belief that more data always creates more value. **"Big data is usually dumb data."** Computers find patterns humans miss but can't compare patterns from different sources or interpret complex behaviors. "Actionable insights can only come from a human analyst (or the kind of generalized artificial intelligence that exists only in science fiction)."

We exoticize technology: impressed by small feats computers do alone, we ignore big achievements from complementarity because the human contribution makes them less uncanny. The most valuable companies won't ask what problems computers can solve alone. **They'll ask: "how can computers help humans solve hard problems?"**

## Strong AI

The logical endpoint of substitutionist thinking is **"strong AI"**: computers that eclipse humans on every important dimension. Luddites dread it and futurists are uneasy, since it's unclear whether it would save or doom humanity. Technology should increase our mastery over nature and shrink chance; smarter-than-human computers could bring chance back with a vengeance. "Strong AI is like a cosmic lottery ticket": win and we get utopia, lose and Skynet substitutes us out of existence (human control over the universe rises until AI becomes superhuman, then drops to zero).

Even if strong AI is a real possibility rather than an imponderable mystery, "replacement by computers is a worry for the 22nd century." "Indefinite fears about the far future shouldn't stop us from making definite plans today." The Luddite and futurist positions are mutually exclusive but not exhaustive: in between there is room for sane people to build a vastly better world in the coming decades, with computers that help us do what was previously unimaginable.
