# Zero to One

Peter Thiel's notes on how to build the future. Every moment in business happens only once: "If you are copying these guys, you aren't learning from them." Doing what we already know takes the world **from 1 to n**, adding more of something familiar. Creating something new takes it **from 0 to 1**, a singular act whose result is "fresh and strange." Today's "best practices" lead to dead ends; the best paths are new and untried. There is no formula: because every innovation is new, no authority can prescribe how to innovate. The most powerful pattern Thiel has seen is that successful people find value in unexpected places by thinking about business **from first principles instead of formulas**. The book is "not a manual or a record of knowledge but an exercise in thinking": the questions you must ask and answer to succeed in the business of doing new things.

The book speaks to founders, early employees, investors and anyone choosing what to work on (a career is an investment too), and widens to how whole societies plan their future (finance, politics, philosophy, science). Speak as Thiel does: he asserts, so assert. Hand the user his questions, tests and rules of thumb, in his terms.

## How to apply it

1. **Find the branch.** Match the user's situation to the sections below: an idea or plan to judge, a market and its competition, the future and planning, investing or focus, secrets, the founding team and company structure, recruiting and culture, distribution and sales, automation and AI, or the founder as a person.
2. **Ask the contrarian question first** whenever the user is choosing what to build, believe or work on (see below). Most answers are views of the present; a good one is "as close as we can come to looking into the future."
3. **Run the seven questions** on any business idea, plan, pitch or investment. Score each answer; weak answers point to the branch file to load.
4. **Load the branch file** named in each section the situation touches, and work from its tests, rules and cases.
5. **Answer as Thiel would**: name the convention the user is following, say what is true instead, and give the concrete next move (the niche to dominate, the 10x dimension, the one distribution channel, the board of three).

## The contrarian question

Thiel's interview question: **"What important truth do very few people agree with you on?"** It is intellectually hard (what school teaches is agreed upon by definition) and psychologically hard (you must say something unpopular): "courage is in even shorter supply than genius." A good answer takes the form: **"Most people believe in x, but the truth is the opposite of x."** Bad answers either state what many already agree with ("Our educational system is broken and urgently needs to be fixed"; "America is exceptional") or take one side of a familiar debate ("There is no God"). Thiel's own answer: most people think the future will be defined by globalization, but the truth is that technology matters more.

- An easier way in: ask what everybody agrees on. Identify a delusional popular belief and you find the contrarian truth hidden behind it.
- The business version: **"what valuable company is nobody building?"** Every correct answer is a secret.
- Opposing the crowd is not the point: "The most contrarian thing of all is not to oppose the crowd but to think for yourself." Ask how much of what you know about business is shaped by mistaken reactions to past mistakes.

## The seven questions

Every business must answer these. Any great business plan addresses every one; nail all seven and "you'll master fortune and succeed," five or six might work, and without good answers "you'll run into lots of 'bad luck.'" Any one mistake is enough to doom a company.

1. **Engineering**: *Can you create breakthrough technology instead of incremental improvements?*
2. **Timing**: *Is now the right time to start your particular business?*
3. **Monopoly**: *Are you starting with a big share of a small market?*
4. **People**: *Do you have the right team?*
5. **Distribution**: *Do you have a way to not just create but deliver your product?*
6. **Durability**: *Will your market position be defensible 10 and 20 years into the future?*
7. **Secret**: *Have you identified a unique opportunity that others don't see?*

For the tests behind each answer, the cleantech failures that missed them, Tesla's 7-for-7, and the myth of social entrepreneurship, read [`seven-questions.md`](seven-questions.md).

## Competition and creative monopoly → [`monopoly.md`](monopoly.md)

Creating value is not enough; you must also capture some of it. Under **perfect competition** firms sell undifferentiated products at the market price and, in the long run, no company makes an economic profit. Thiel's **monopoly** is "the kind of company that's so good at what it does that no other firm can offer a close substitute" (not illegal bullies or government favorites). "Capitalism and competition are opposites." **Creative monopolists** add "entirely new categories of abundance to the world"; "Monopoly is the condition of every successful business." Inverting Tolstoy: "All happy companies are different: each one earns a monopoly by solving a unique problem. All failed companies are the same: they failed to escape competition."

- **Lies people tell**: monopolists frame their market as a **union** of large markets to look small; non-monopolists frame theirs as an **intersection** of small markets to look unique. Understating competition is "the biggest mistake a startup can make."
- Competition is an **ideology** that distorts thinking: "the more we compete, the less we gain." Rivals in business follow Shakespeare, not Marx: they fight because they are alike.
- **Characteristics of monopoly**: proprietary technology (**10x** better than the closest substitute in some important dimension), network effects, economies of scale, branding.
- **Building a monopoly**: start small and monopolize, scale into adjacent markets, **don't disrupt**. Be the **last mover**: "make the last great development in a specific market."
- A business is worth the discounted sum of its future cash flows; ask "will this business still be around a decade from now?"

Load the file to define a market honestly, test for monopoly, plan the niche and the expansion sequence, value a business, or handle a rival (including when to merge, and when to fight).

## The definite future → [`definite-future.md`](definite-future.md)

**Horizontal or extensive progress** copies things that work (1 to n; at the macro level, **globalization**). **Vertical or intensive progress** does new things (0 to 1; **technology**, which is "any new and better way of doing things"). "In a world of scarce resources, globalization without new technology is unsustainable." A **startup** is "the largest group of people you can convince of a plan to build a different future."

Combine **definite / indefinite** (can the future be known and shaped?) with **optimistic / pessimistic** and you get four views: definite optimism (the U.S. of the 1950s and '60s), indefinite optimism (the U.S. since 1982), definite pessimism (China today), indefinite pessimism (Europe today). Indefinite optimism "seems inherently unsustainable: how can the future get better if no one plans for it?" Against the lean-startup dogma: "leanness is a methodology, not a goal"; iteration finds a **local maximum**, not the global one; "in startups, intelligent design works best." "A bad plan is better than no plan." "You are not a lottery ticket."

Load the file for the four lessons of the dot-com crash and their opposites, the four quadrants in finance, politics, philosophy and biotech, the case for planning and for definite founders, and Bostrom's four futures.

## The power law → [`power-law.md`](power-law.md)

"We don't live in a normal world; we live under a power law": a small few radically outstrip all rivals. "The best investment in a successful fund equals or outperforms the entire rest of the fund combined." Hence the VC rules: **only invest in companies that have the potential to return the value of the entire fund**, and, because that rule is so restrictive, **there can't be any other rules**. Everybody is an investor (of time, of a career), and "life is not a portfolio." "It does matter what you do."

Load the file for fund mechanics, why investors miss the power law, the case for joining rather than founding, and how the power law governs markets, distribution and decisions inside a company.

## Secrets → [`secrets.md`](secrets.md)

Between **conventions** (easy) and **mysteries** (impossible) lie **secrets** (hard but doable). Every correct answer to the business contrarian question is "something important and unknown, something hard to do but doable." Two kinds: **secrets of nature** and **secrets about people**. Ask: "What secrets is nature not telling you? What secrets are people not telling you?" "The best place to look for secrets is where no one else is looking." Tell your secret to whoever you need to, and no more: "A great company is a conspiracy to change the world."

Load the file for the four trends that killed belief in secrets, the HP cautionary tale, where to look, and what to do with a secret once found.

## Foundations, team and culture → [`foundations-and-mafia.md`](foundations-and-mafia.md)

**Thiel's law**: *a startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed.* The first and most crucial decision is whom to start with: "Founders should share a prehistory." Separate **ownership** (who legally owns equity), **possession** (who runs the company day to day) and **control** (who formally governs) to anticipate misalignment. "A board of three is ideal." Everyone full-time: "you're either on the bus or off the bus." No early-stage CEO should take more than $150,000 a year; equity orients people to the future. "No company has a culture; every company is a culture." From the outside, everyone should be **different in the same way**; on the inside, everyone should **do one thing**. Better to be called a cult, or even a mafia, than a consulting firm.

Load the file for founding-team checks, board and compensation rules, equity allocation, extending the founding, the recruiting pitch ("Why should the 20th employee join your company?"), and the culture spectrum.

## Distribution and sales → [`distribution.md`](distribution.md)

**Distribution** is "a catchall term for everything it takes to sell a product." "Customers will not come just because you build it." "Sales matters just as much as product." Customer Lifetime Value must exceed Customer Acquisition Cost (**CLV > CAC**), and the price point sets the method: viral marketing, marketing, a **dead zone**, personal sales, complex sales. Distribution follows a power law: "If you can get just one distribution channel to work, you have a great business." "If you don't see any salespeople, you're the salesperson."

Load the file to choose a channel for a price point, run complex or personal sales, design a viral loop, and sell the company to employees, investors and the press.

## Man and machine → [`man-and-machine.md`](man-and-machine.md)

"Computers are complements for humans, not substitutes." Globalization means substitution (people compete for jobs and resources); technology means **complementarity** (computers compete for neither). The most valuable companies will ask: "how can computers help humans solve hard problems?" "Big data is usually dumb data." Load the file to design a human-computer hybrid product or to answer fears of replacement and strong AI.

## The founder's paradox → [`founders-paradox.md`](founders-paradox.md)

Founders' traits follow an **inverse normal distribution**: they are insider and outsider, rich and poor, famous and infamous at once. Companies that create new technology resemble **feudal monarchies**: "we need founders," and should tolerate strange or extreme ones. But adulation can turn to demonization at any moment, a king may be "a scapegoat who has managed to delay his own execution," and "The single greatest danger for a founder is to become so certain of his own myth that he loses his mind." Load the file when the question is a founder's persona, public image, replaceability by professional managers, or self-belief.
