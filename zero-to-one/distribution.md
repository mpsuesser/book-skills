# Distribution and sales

## Why distribution is underrated

**Distribution** is "a catchall term for everything it takes to sell a product." We underrate it from the bias of the *Hitchhiker's Guide* thinkers and workers who shipped the salespeople and consultants off on the B Ship: middlemen supposedly get in the way, and distribution should flow magically from a good product. This **Field of Dreams** conceit is strongest among engineers biased toward building cool stuff. "Customers will not come just because you build it."

**Nerds vs. salesmen.** U.S. advertising takes $150 billion a year and employs more than 600,000 people; U.S. sales is bigger at $450 billion, with 3.2 million Americans working in it. Nerds find advertising, marketing and sales superficial and irrational, "But advertising matters because it works. It works on nerds, and it works on you." Ads exist not to make you buy now but to embed impressions that drive sales later; anyone who can't admit its effect on himself "is doubly deceived." Engineers are used to transparency (a solution works or fails, and work is easy to judge); sales is "an orchestrated campaign to change surface appearances without changing the underlying reality," which looks trivial or dishonest to them. They overestimate the difficulty of science and engineering because those challenges are obvious. "What nerds miss is that it takes hard work to make sales look easy."

**Sales is hidden.** "All salesmen are actors: their priority is persuasion, not sincerity." We dislike only the awkward, obvious salesmen, the bad ones. Ability runs from novices through experts and masters to grandmasters, whose art is hidden in plain sight: Tom Sawyer getting friends to whitewash his fence was masterful; getting them to pay for the privilege was the move of a grandmaster. "Like acting, sales works best when hidden," which is why distribution jobs carry other titles: those who sell advertising are "account executives," who sell customers work in "business development," who sell companies are "investment bankers," who sell themselves are "politicians." Sales ability separates superstars from also-rans in every field: analysts aim to become dealmakers, law firms are led by rainmakers, professors envy the self-promoters who define their fields, and even the agenda of physics and cancer research results from persuasion. Businesspeople underestimate sales because of "the systematic effort to hide it at every level of every field in a world secretly driven by it."

**"It sells itself."** Anyone who says this about a real product is lying: either delusional (lying to himself) or selling something (contradicting himself). The opposite cliché, "the best product doesn't always win," gets explained as path dependence, but the operating systems and keyboard layouts we use were not imposed by mere chance. "It's better to think of distribution as something essential to the design of your product. If you've invented something new but you haven't invented an effective way to sell it, you have a bad business—no matter how good the product."

## How to sell a product

"Superior sales and distribution by itself can create a monopoly, even with no product differentiation. The converse is not true." However strong the product, even one that fits established habits and that anyone who tries it likes immediately, back it with a strong distribution plan.

**Two metrics set the limits.** **Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)**, the total net profit you earn on average over your relationship with a customer, must exceed **Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)**, what you spend on average to acquire one. The higher the price, the more you must spend to make a sale, and the more sense it makes to spend it.

The continuum of distribution methods, from cheapest to costliest per customer:

**Viral marketing (CAC: $1) → Marketing (CAC: $100) → Dead Zone → Sales (CAC: $10,000) → Complex sales (CAC: $10 million)**

The target runs along the same line, from consumers at the viral end, through small business in the middle, to big business and government at the complex-sales end.

### Complex sales

Average sale of seven figures or more. Every detail of every deal needs close personal attention; relationships take months; you might close once every year or two, then follow up through installation and service long after. It's hard, and it's the only way to sell some of the most valuable products.

- Politics matters as much as technical ingenuity. SpaceX (more than 3,000 people, mostly in California) persuaded NASA to sign billion-dollar contracts against an aerospace industry of more than 500,000 people spread through all 50 states, whose members of Congress protect their districts' funds. Because complex sales means only a few deals a year, a sales grandmaster like Musk can focus on the most crucial people and overcome political inertia.
- **"Complex sales works best when you don't have 'salesmen' at all."** Palantir employs no one separately tasked with selling. Its CEO, Alex Karp, spends 25 days a month on the road with clients and prospects; deals run from $1 million to $100 million, and at that price point buyers want to talk to the CEO, not the VP of Sales.
- **Growth expectations**: these businesses succeed with **50% to 100% year-over-year growth over a decade**. A 10x jump when customers learn of an obviously superior product almost never happens.
- **Start small**: a new customer may agree to become your biggest customer but will rarely sign a deal completely out of scale with what you've sold before. Build a pool of **reference customers** using the product successfully, then do the long, methodical work of hustling toward bigger deals.

### Personal sales

Average deals between $10,000 and $100,000; the CEO doesn't do all the selling. The challenge is not any one sale but "how to establish a process by which a sales team of modest size can move the product to a wide audience."

- **Land with the most acute pain, then expand.** In 2008 nobody knew they needed cloud storage. Box's reps started with small groups of users with the most acute file-sharing problems and built relationships with more and more users in each client. A small account sold to the Stanford Sleep Clinic in 2009 grew until the university offered a Box account to every student and faculty member and Stanford Hospital ran on Box. Pitching the university president an enterprise-wide solution first would have sold nothing; a complex-sales approach would have made Box a forgotten failure.
- **The product can itself be distribution.** ZocDoc charges doctors a few hundred dollars a month to join its booking network; with deals of a few thousand dollars it needs so many salespeople it has an internal team just to hire them. Each doctor signed makes the product more valuable to consumers, and more consumers make it more appealing to doctors.

### Distribution doldrums: the dead zone

Between personal sales (salespeople obviously required) and traditional advertising (none required) lies a **dead zone**. Software that helps convenience store owners track inventory, priced around $1,000, has a clear value proposition but no channel: advertising is too broad (no TV channel only convenience store owners watch) or too inefficient (one ad in *Convenience Store News* won't get anyone to part with $1,000 a year), and at that price you can't send a person to every prospect. That is why small and medium-sized businesses lack tools big firms take for granted: "distribution is the hidden bottleneck." Test any product priced in this band for a channel before building it.

### Marketing and advertising

For relatively low-priced products with mass appeal and no viral distribution. Procter & Gamble can't send salespeople door to door with detergent (it does send them to grocery chains, where one sale can mean 100,000 bottles), so it uses TV commercials, coupons and attention-getting boxes.

- **Startups should advertise only when CAC and CLV make every other channel uneconomical.** Warby Parker's glasses start around $100, so a customer buying a few pairs is worth a few hundred dollars: too little for personal attention, and hundred-dollar physical goods don't go viral. Quirky TV commercials put the product before millions of eyeglass wearers ("TV is a great big megaphone"); when you can spend only dozens of dollars per customer, you need the biggest megaphone you can find.
- **Don't try to outspend big companies** on memorable TV spots or elaborate PR stunts. PayPal's launch event with James Doohan (*Star Trek*'s Scotty) beaming money flopped; Priceline's William Shatner campaign worked, but Priceline was already a major player. No early-stage startup can match big companies' ad budgets.

### Viral marketing

"A product is viral if its core functionality encourages users to invite their friends to become users too": every Facebook share and PayPal payment invites more people in. It is cheap and fast. If every new user brings in more than one more, you get a chain reaction of exponential growth. Make the **viral loop** "as quick and frictionless as possible": funny videos spread in seconds because of their extremely short **cycle times**.

PayPal began with 24 users, all employees; banner ads cost too much. Paying people to sign up and more to refer friends cost $20 per customer but produced **7% daily growth**, nearly doubling the user base every 10 days. After four or five months it had hundreds of thousands of users and a business whose small transfer fees came to greatly exceed its acquisition cost.

**Dominate the most valuable segment first.** "Whoever is first to dominate the most important segment of a market with viral potential will be the last mover in the whole market." The obvious segment for email payments, emigrants wiring money home through Western Union, transacted too infrequently. PayPal needed a smaller niche with a higher **velocity of money** and found it in eBay's PowerSellers: professional vendors with multiple auctions ending each day who bought almost as much as they sold, a constant stream of payments. eBay's own payment solution was terrible, so they were enthusiastic early adopters. Once PayPal dominated them and became eBay's payments platform, there was no catching up.

## The power law of distribution

For any given business, one method is likely to be far more powerful than every other: distribution follows a power law of its own. Entrepreneurs assume more is more, but the **kitchen sink approach** (a few salespeople, some magazine ads, viral functionality bolted on as an afterthought) doesn't work. "Most businesses get zero distribution channels to work: poor sales rather than bad product is the most common cause of failure. If you can get just one distribution channel to work, you have a great business. If you try for several but don't nail one, you're finished."

## Selling to non-customers

You must sell the company, not just the product, to employees and investors. The recruiting version of "great products sell themselves" is "This company is so good that people will be clamoring to join it"; the fundraising version is "This company is so great that investors will be banging down our door to invest." Clamor and frenzy are real, but they rarely happen without calculated recruiting and pitching beneath the surface.

Selling to the media is part of selling to everyone else. Nerds who mistrust the media try to ignore it, but just as no one buys a superior product on its obvious merits alone, "you should never assume that people will admire your company without a public relations strategy." Even with viral customer acquisition, the press helps attract investors and employees, and any prospective hire worth having will google you: what he finds or doesn't find is critical.

## Everybody sells

Everybody has a product to sell, whether employee, founder or investor, even in a company of one person and a computer. "Look around. If you don't see any salespeople, you're the salesperson."
