# Competition and creative monopoly

## Value created vs value captured

A company can create a lot of value without becoming very valuable itself, so "even very big businesses can be bad businesses." U.S. airlines took $160 billion in revenue in 2012 and made 37 cents per passenger trip on an average one-way fare of $178. Google took $50 billion and kept 21% as profit, more than 100 times the airlines' margin, and was worth three times all U.S. airlines combined. The airlines compete; Google stands alone.

- **Perfect competition** (the ideal and default of Economics 101): undifferentiated firms selling homogeneous products, no market power, everyone sells at the market price. Profits attract entrants who drive prices down; losses drive firms out. "Under perfect competition, in the long run no company makes an economic profit."
- **Monopoly**: owns its market and sets its own prices, producing at the quantity and price that maximize profit. Thiel means "the kind of company that's so good at what it does that no other firm can offer a close substitute," not illegal bullies or government favorites.
- "Capitalism and competition are opposites": capitalism is premised on accumulating capital, but under perfect competition all profits get competed away. "If you want to create and capture lasting value, don't build an undifferentiated commodity business."

## Lies people tell

To an outsider firms look alike, sitting side by side in the middle of a line from perfect competition to monopoly. In reality the difference is binary and deep: most businesses sit much closer to one extreme than people realize. The confusion comes from a universal bias to describe market conditions in self-serving ways.

**Monopoly lies.** Monopolists conceal their monopoly to avoid being audited, scrutinized and attacked, usually by exaggerating the power of their (nonexistent) competition. They frame their market as the **union** of several large markets:

> search engine ∪ mobile phones ∪ wearable computers ∪ self-driving cars

Google as a search engine (as of May 2014) held about 68% of search, against Microsoft's 19% and Yahoo!'s 10%, and "google" is a verb in the Oxford English Dictionary. Framed as an advertising company, it becomes small: U.S. search advertising ($17 billion) sits inside U.S. online advertising ($37 billion), inside all U.S. advertising ($150 billion), inside global advertising ($495 billion), so even total control of U.S. search ads would be 3.4% of the global market. Framed as a technology company, it is under 0.24% of the $964 billion global consumer tech market, though 95% of its revenue comes from search advertising. Eric Schmidt's 2011 testimony ("We face an extremely competitive landscape in which consumers have a multitude of options to access information") translates to: "Google is a small fish in a big pond."

**Competitive lies.** Non-monopolists say "we're in a league of our own." "The fatal temptation is to describe your market extremely narrowly so that you dominate it by definition." They frame their market as the **intersection** of smaller markets:

> British food ∩ restaurant ∩ Palo Alto
>
> Rap star ∩ hackers ∩ sharks

The only British restaurant in Palo Alto owns its market only if the market is British food. The real market may be all Palo Alto restaurants, or those of nearby towns too. Founders have an incentive not to ask: told that most new restaurants fail within one or two years, they invent a story of why theirs is different instead of asking whether there are people who would rather eat British food above all else. Trivial differentiation (a great-grandmother's naan recipe) will not save a business in a competitive market. The same holds for a screenplay that "will combine various exciting elements in entirely new ways": Jay-Z joining teen hackers to catch the shark that killed his friend has never been done, and maybe that's a good thing.

**Test a market definition** by widening it one ring at a time and recomputing your share, as with the solar company that holds 100 MW: 10.53% of U.S. solar (950 MW), under 1% of global solar (18 GW), 0.02% of global renewables (420 GW), a drop in the ocean of global power generation (15,000 GW). A submarket that exists only by rhetorical shrinking is fictional, and "you can't dominate a submarket if it's fictional, and huge markets are highly competitive, not highly attainable."

## Why monopoly matters

- **Ruthless people.** A competitive business must squeeze every efficiency (minimum wages, Grandma at the register, the kids washing dishes). "The competitive ecosystem pushes people toward ruthlessness or death." A monopoly has latitude to care about its workers, its products and its impact on the world; it can "take ethics seriously without jeopardizing its own existence." "In business, money is either an important thing or it is everything." A firm in perfect competition is so focused on today's margins that it cannot plan for a long-term future. "Only one thing can allow a business to transcend the daily brute struggle for survival: monopoly profits."
- **Monopoly capitalism.** In a static world a monopolist is just a rent collector, like a board game where deeds change hands but the board never changes. In the real, dynamic world creative monopolists invent new and better things. Apple's iPhone profits were "the reward for creating greater abundance, not artificial scarcity." Old monopolies do not strangle innovation, because new ones displace them: IBM's hardware monopoly fell to Microsoft's software, Microsoft's fell to mobile led by iOS, AT&T's to cheap cell plans. "The history of progress is a history of better monopoly businesses replacing incumbents." The promise of years of monopoly profits is the incentive to innovate, and the profits fund long-term plans and ambitious research.
- **Equilibrium is death.** Economists copied 19th-century physics and treat firms as interchangeable atoms; perfect competition is what is easy to model. Physics' long-run equilibrium is the heat death of the universe: "in business, equilibrium means stasis, and stasis means death." In a competitive equilibrium your firm's death won't matter to the world. "Every new creation takes place far from equilibrium," and "every business is successful exactly to the extent that it does something others cannot."

## The ideology of competition

Competition is "an ideology—*the* ideology—that pervades our society and distorts our thinking." Schools rank students by grades in the same subjects taught the same way; elite students climb until competition beats their dreams out of them, then pay large sums "for the privilege of being turned into conformists" and fight over conventional careers such as management consulting and investment banking. Thiel came close to a Supreme Court clerkship and lost; a friend later asked, "So, Peter, aren't you glad you didn't get that clerkship?" Winning would have cost him the chance to create anything new. "All Rhodes Scholars had a great future in their past."

**Marx vs Shakespeare.** Two models of conflict. To Marx, people fight because they are different; the greater the difference, the greater the conflict. To Shakespeare, combatants are alike ("Two households, both alike in dignity"), grow more alike as the feud escalates, and lose sight of why they began. In business, Shakespeare is the better guide: people inside a firm obsess over rivals for advancement, firms obsess over rivals in the market, and everyone loses sight of what matters. Microsoft and Google came from different places but fought across Windows vs. Chrome OS, Bing vs. Google Search, Explorer vs. Chrome, Office vs. Docs and Surface vs. Nexus, and Apple overtook them both (in January 2013 Apple was worth $500 billion against $467 billion for Google and Microsoft combined; three years earlier each had been worth more than Apple). It's competition, not business, that is like war: "allegedly necessary, supposedly valiant, but ultimately destructive."

Failure modes of rivalry:

- **Imitation.** Rivalry makes people "overemphasize old opportunities and slavishly copy what has worked in the past." After Square's square card reader came a half-moon reader, a cylindrical one and PayPal's triangle: the saga "won't end until the apes run out of shapes." People less sensitive to social cues (an Asperger's-like social ineptitude) are less likely to imitate and less likely to give up their convictions to join crowds competing for obvious prizes.
- **Hallucinated opportunities.** Pets.com, PetStore.com and Petopia.com fought over chew-toy prices and Super Bowl ads and never asked whether online pet supply was the right space; $300 million vanished with Pets.com. "Everybody loses when the war isn't one worth fighting."
- **Personal distraction.** Ellison and Siebel spent years sabotaging each other; Ellison kept enemies "large enough to appear threatening" but not to threaten. Informix's CEO aimed billboards personally at Ellison while his company imploded in an accounting scandal.

**What to do with a rival.**

- If you can't beat a rival, it may be better to merge. PayPal and Elon Musk's X.com, four blocks apart with feature-for-feature products, were at all-out war (100-hour weeks aimed at beating X.com, not at productivity) until fear of the bursting bubble brought them to a café equidistant from both offices and a 50-50 merger in March 2000. Together they rode out the crash.
- "Sometimes you do have to fight. Where that's true, you should fight and win. There is no middle ground: either don't throw any punches, or strike hard and end it quickly."
- Pride gets in the way. Hamlet's greatness is to "greatly to find quarrel in a straw / When honor's at the stake," fighting for things as thin as an eggshell. That is disastrous in business. "If you can recognize competition as a destructive force instead of a sign of value, you're already more sane than most."

## Last mover advantage: durability and value

A monopoly is a great business only if it endures. When Twitter went public in 2013 it was valued at $24 billion, more than 12 times the New York Times Company, though the Times earned $133 million in 2012 and Twitter lost money. The difference is future cash flow.

- **Value**: "the value of a business today is the sum of all the money it will make in the future," discounted to present worth.
- **Low-growth businesses** hold most of their value in the near term. A newspaper holds its value if it keeps its cash flows for five or six years; nightclubs and restaurants, with close substitutes, see cash flows dwindle as customers move to trendier alternatives, so present value falls year by year.
- **Technology companies** often lose money at first, and "Most of a tech company's value will come at least 10 to 15 years in the future." Their present-value cash flows rise for years before peaking. In March 2001 PayPal, 27 months old and unprofitable but growing revenue 100% a year, had 75% of its present value in profits from 2011 and beyond, and that proved an underestimate. LinkedIn in early 2014 was worth $24.5 billion on under $1 billion in revenue and $21.6 million of 2012 net income, which makes sense on projected future cash flows.
- **Measurement mania.** Growth is easy to measure and durability isn't, so founders obsess over weekly active users, monthly revenue targets and quarterly earnings, and miss deeper threats. Zynga's "psychometric engine" could not escape the Hollywood problem of producing constant hits for a fickle audience; Groupon could not turn trial merchants into repeat customers. Ask: **"will this business still be around a decade from now?"** Numbers won't answer it; think critically about the business's qualitative characteristics.

## Characteristics of monopoly

Monopolies usually share some combination of four. "This isn't a list of boxes to check": there is no shortcut to monopoly, but analyzing a business by these helps you make it durable.

1. **Proprietary technology**, the most substantive advantage, because it makes the product difficult or impossible to replicate (Google's search algorithms, plus fast page loads and accurate autocompletion). Rule of thumb: **at least 10 times better than its closest substitute in some important dimension**. Anything less than an order of magnitude "will probably be perceived as a marginal improvement and will be hard to sell, especially in an already crowded market." Three routes to 10x:
   - *Invent something completely new*: where there was nothing before, the increase in value is theoretically infinite (a drug that safely eliminates the need for sleep, a cure for baldness).
   - *Radically improve an existing solution*: PayPal made buying and selling on eBay at least 10 times better than mailing a check that took 7 to 10 days, with funds known to be good. Amazon offered at least 10 times as many books as any store (a retail bookstore might stock 100,000) because it held no inventory and ordered from suppliers; Barnes & Noble sued three days before its IPO, calling it a "book broker."
   - *Superior integrated design*: tablets from Windows XP Tablet PC Edition (2002) and Nokia's Internet Tablet (2005) were a pain; the iPad took them "from unusable to useful."
2. **Network effects**: the product grows more useful as more people use it. You reap them only if the product is valuable to its very first users while the network is small. Xanadu (from 1960) needed every computer to join at once and folded after three decades. So network businesses "must start with especially small markets" (Facebook began with Harvard), which is why MBA types rarely start them: the first markets are so small they don't look like business opportunities.
3. **Economies of scale**: fixed costs (engineering, management, office space) spread over ever more sales; software's marginal cost is near zero. Service businesses rarely become monopolies (a yoga studio can add instructors and locations but never lets a small core group serve millions). "A good startup should have the potential for great scale built into its first design."
4. **Branding**: a company has a monopoly on its own brand by definition. Apple's surface (looks, materials, stores, ads, premium pricing, Jobs's charisma) can be imitated, but it works only over substance: proprietary hardware and software, scale that dominates materials pricing, and network effects between developers and users. "Beginning with brand rather than substance is dangerous": Marissa Mayer's Yahoo! chain of "people then products then traffic then revenue" (new logo, Tumblr, star power) left open what products it would make. Jobs, on his return, slashed product lines to focus on a handful of 10x opportunities. "No technology company can be built on branding alone."

## Building a monopoly

Choose the market carefully and expand deliberately.

**Start small and monopolize.** Every startup is small at the start and every monopoly dominates a large share of its market, so every startup should start with a very small market. "Always err on the side of starting too small." "If you think your initial market might be too big, it almost certainly is."

- **Small doesn't mean nonexistent.** PayPal's first product beamed money between PalmPilots; their millions of users were scattered, had little in common and used the devices only episodically. Nobody needed it. eBay's high-volume "PowerSellers" were a few thousand people who really needed PayPal, and after three months PayPal served 25% of them.
- **The perfect target market** is "a small group of particular people concentrated together and served by few or no competitors." Any big market is a bad choice; a big market already served by competitors is worse.
- **Red flag**: a founder who talks of getting 1% of a $100 billion market. A large market either lacks a good starting point or is open to competition, so the 1% never comes, and a foothold there earns zero profits.

**Scaling up.** Once you dominate a niche, expand gradually into related and slightly broader markets.

- Amazon aimed at all online retail but started with books: millions to catalog, all roughly the same shape, easy to ship, and the rarest titles drew the most enthusiastic customers. It became the answer for anyone far from a bookstore or seeking something unusual, then chose adjacent markets over growing the number of readers, starting with the most similar (CDs, videos, software), and added categories until it was the world's general store.
- eBay began with intense interest groups like Beanie Baby obsessives and kept serving hobbyists rather than jumping to sports cars or industrial surplus. Watch for hidden obstacles: auctions suit individually distinctive goods (coins, stamps), not commodities (pencils, Kleenex), so eBay ended smaller than people expected in 2004.
- "Sequencing markets correctly is underrated." The most successful companies make the core progression (dominate a niche, then scale to adjacent markets) part of their founding narrative.

**Don't disrupt.** "Disruption" was a term of art: a firm uses new technology to bring a low-end product at low prices, improves it, and overtakes incumbents' premium products (PCs over mainframes; perhaps mobile over PCs). As a buzzword it distorts self-understanding in a competitive way: it makes startups see themselves through older firms' eyes, as insurgents fixated on obstacles. "If your company can be summed up by its opposition to already existing firms, it can't be completely new and it's probably not going to become a monopoly." Disruptors look for trouble and find it: Napster threatened the recording industry in 1999, made the cover of Time the next year, and was in bankruptcy court a year and a half later. PayPal took some business from Visa but expanded the payments market and gave Visa more than it took, a net-positive dynamic instead of a negative-sum fight. "As you craft a plan to expand to adjacent markets, don't disrupt: avoid competition as much as possible."

**The last will be first.** "Moving first is a tactic, not a goal." First mover advantage is worthless if someone unseats you. Aim to be the **last mover**: "make the last great development in a specific market and enjoy years or even decades of monopoly profits," by dominating a small niche and scaling toward an ambitious long-term vision. As Capablanca put it, "you must study the endgame before everything else."
