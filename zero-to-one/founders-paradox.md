# The founder's paradox

## Unusual people

Of the six people who started PayPal, four had built bombs in high school, five were 23 or younger, four were born outside the U.S., and three had escaped communist countries. One had signed up for cryonics; one was proud to be without a country; one had escaped a trailer park to a top math and science magnet school; the lone Eagle Scout took a third of the salary a big bank had offered him. Are founders unusual, or do we remember and exaggerate what is most unusual about them? Which traits matter? It is "more powerful but at the same time more dangerous for a company to be led by a distinctive individual instead of an interchangeable manager."

## The difference engine

Most people's traits fall on a **bell curve**, peaking at average, with paired extremes at either end: weak/nerd vs. strong/athlete, idiot savant vs. polymath, disagreeable vs. charismatic, outsider vs. insider, poor vs. rich, villain vs. hero, infamous vs. famous. Founders seem extreme, so you might expect a **fat-tailed** distribution, more people at both ends.

The stranger fact: opposite traits, normally mutually exclusive, coexist in founders. Startup CEOs are cash poor and paper millionaires; they swing between sullen jerkiness and appealing charisma; "almost all successful entrepreneurs are simultaneously insiders and outsiders"; success brings fame and infamy together. Founders' traits follow an **inverse normal distribution**: high at both extremes and empty at average.

Where do the traits come from? Nature, nurture, founders strategically exaggerating, others exaggerating them: all can operate at once, and they reinforce each other in a cycle that starts with unusual people and makes them act and seem more unusual:

**actually different → develop extreme traits → they exaggerate → others exaggerate → actually different**

- **Richard Branson** started his first business at 16 and Virgin Records at 22, but no one is born with that lion's mane. The media crowned him "The Virgin King," "The King of Branding" and, after ice cubes shaped like his face, "The Ice King." A normal businessman lionized by good PR, or a born branding genius rightly singled out by the journalists he manipulates so well? Maybe both.
- **Sean Parker** began with the ultimate outsider status, criminal: his father pulled his keyboard mid-hack, he couldn't log out, and the FBI arrested him at 16. Three years later he co-founded Napster (10 million users in its first year, shut down by court order 20 months after opening), then became Facebook's founding president, stepped down amid drug allegations, which only enhanced his notoriety, and was played by Justin Timberlake; people visiting Silicon Valley now ask Timberlake if he's Sean Parker.
- **Celebrities** found personal brands the way founders found companies. Lady Gaga claims she was "born this way," but no one is born looking like a zombie with horns, so she must be a self-manufactured myth. Then again, nobody normal would do that to herself, so perhaps she was born that way.

## Where kings come from

Myth is full of extreme founders. **Oedipus**, abandoned as an infant and raised in a foreign land, became a brilliant king who solved the Sphinx's riddle: the paradigmatic insider/outsider. **Romulus**, of royal blood but abandoned, killed Remus for crossing the line he had drawn as Rome's edge ("So perish every one that shall hereafter leap over my wall"): law-maker and law-breaker, outlaw and king.

The famous and infamous are vessels for public sentiment, praised in prosperity and blamed for misfortune. Primitive societies would be torn apart by conflict without a way to stop it, so when plague, disaster or violent rivalry threatened the peace, they placed the entire blame on one person everyone could agree on: a **scapegoat**. Like founders, scapegoats are extreme and contradictory: weak, since powerless to stop their own victimization, and the most powerful member of the community, since they defuse conflict by taking the blame. Before execution they were often worshipped as gods (the Aztecs feasted their victims royally until their brief reign ended). These are the roots of monarchy: "every king was a living god, and every god a murdered king. Perhaps every modern king is just a scapegoat who has managed to delay his own execution."

## American royalty

We crown celebrities (Elvis the king of rock, Michael Jackson the king of pop, Britney Spears the pop princess) until they fall, and we revel in the fall. For some, early death brings resurrection: the "27 Club." We alternately worship and despise technology founders the same way.

- **Howard Hughes** built Houston's first radio transmitter at 11, made nine successful movies by 30, and designed, built and flew record-setting planes. He didn't show up for his Congressional Gold Medal in 1939 (Truman later mailed it to him). His third and worst crash, in 1946, began his end: obsessive-compulsive and addicted to painkillers, he spent his last 30 years in self-imposed solitary confinement. He had always acted a little crazy so fewer people would bother him; "when his crazy act turned into a crazy life, he became an object of pity as much as awe."
- **Bill Gates** embodied the founder archetype, an awkward, nerdy college-dropout outsider and the world's wealthiest insider. With Windows holding 90% of operating systems in 2000, Peter Jennings could ask who mattered more, Clinton or Gates. The Justice Department sued Microsoft for "anticompetitive conduct," and in June 2000 a court ordered it broken up; Gates had stepped down as CEO six months earlier, consumed by legal threats instead of building technology. The breakup was overturned and the case settled in 2001, but his enemies had already deprived Microsoft of its founder's full engagement, and it entered relative stagnation. "Highly visible success can attract highly focused attacks."

## The return of the king

Jobs and Gates were opposites (artist vs. businessman, closed vs. open systems, great products vs. running the world), but both were insider/outsiders who pushed their companies to achievements no one else could have matched. Jobs, a barefoot college dropout, was also the insider of his own personality cult, charismatic or crazy by mood or by calculation. In 1985 Apple's board effectively ousted him after he clashed with the professional CEO brought in for "adult supervision." By 1997 impeccably credentialed executives had steered Apple nearly to bankruptcy (Michael Dell: "I'd shut it down and give the money back to the shareholders"). Jobs returned as interim CEO and introduced the iPod (2001), iPhone (2007) and iPad (2010); by 2012 Apple was the most valuable company in the world. His return shows that "the most important task in business—the creation of new value—cannot be reduced to a formula and applied by professionals."

**Companies that create new technology resemble feudal monarchies** more than supposedly modern organizations. A unique founder "can make authoritative decisions, inspire strong personal loyalty, and plan ahead for decades." Impersonal bureaucracies of trained professionals outlast any lifetime but usually act with short time horizons.

## Lessons

- **For business: we need founders.** Be more tolerant of founders who seem strange or extreme; "we need unusual individuals to lead companies beyond mere incrementalism."
- **For founders:** individual prominence and adulation can be enjoyed only on the condition that they may be exchanged for notoriety and demonization at any moment, so be careful.
- **Above all, don't overestimate your own power as an individual.** Founders matter not because only their work has value but because "a great founder can bring out the best work from everybody at his company." Needing peculiar founders doesn't mean worshipping Ayn Randian "prime movers" who claim independence from everyone around them. Rand's "villains were real, but her heroes were fake. There is no Galt's Gulch. There is no secession from society." Believing yourself divinely self-sufficient marks someone who has "mistaken the crowd's worship—or jeering—for the truth."
- **The two dangers.** "The single greatest danger for a founder is to become so certain of his own myth that he loses his mind. But an equally insidious danger for every business is to lose all sense of myth and mistake disenchantment for wisdom."
