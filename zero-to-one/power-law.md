# The power law

## The law of the universe

Small minorities achieve disproportionate results. Pareto found in 1906 that 20% of the people owned 80% of the land in Italy, as 20% of the peapods in his garden produced 80% of the peas. The most destructive earthquakes outweigh all smaller ones combined; the biggest cities dwarf all towns put together; monopoly businesses capture more value than millions of undifferentiated competitors. The **power law** ("so named because exponential equations describe severely unequal distributions") "is the law of the universe," so pervasive we usually don't see it. The one thing everyone needs to know, which even venture capitalists struggle to understand: **"we don't live in a normal world; we live under a power law."** (Einstein never said compound interest was "the eighth wonder of the world"; that he keeps getting credit for it shows the same compounding.)

## The power law of venture capital

VCs raise money from institutions and wealthy people, pool it in a fund, invest in early-stage technology companies, and usually take 20% of the returns. Funds usually run 10 years, because successful companies take time to grow and exit through an IPO or acquisition. Most venture-backed companies fail, usually soon after they start, so a fund typically loses money first and, if it succeeds, rises steeply later when its winners hit their exponential growth spurts: the **J-curve**. For most funds, takeoff never comes.

**The error** is expecting returns to be normally distributed: bad companies fail, mediocre ones stay flat, good ones return 2x or even 4x. Investors who assume this build diversified portfolios and hope winners offset losers. This **"spray and pray"** approach "usually produces an entire portfolio of flops, with no hits at all." Real returns follow a power law: a small handful of companies radically outperform all the others, and the perceived gently declining curve is in reality a steep plunge from one outlier to a long flat tail below 1x. "If you focus on diversification instead of single-minded pursuit of the very few companies that can become overwhelmingly valuable, you'll miss those rare companies in the first place."

At Founders Fund, Facebook, the best investment in the 2005 fund, returned more than all the others combined; Palantir, the second best, is set to return more than everything else except Facebook. The pattern holds across all its funds. **"The biggest secret in venture capital is that the best investment in a successful fund equals or outperforms the entire rest of the fund combined."**

**Two rules for VCs:**

1. **Only invest in companies that have the potential to return the value of the entire fund.** This eliminates the vast majority of possible investments, since even quite successful companies usually succeed on a humbler scale.
2. **Because rule number one is so restrictive, there can't be any other rules.**

Breaking rule one: Andreessen Horowitz put $250,000 into Instagram in 2010 and netted $78 million when Facebook bought it for $1 billion two years later, a 312x return. But against a $1.5 billion fund, $250,000 checks would need 19 Instagrams just to break even. That is why investors put much more into any company worth funding: find the handful that will go from 0 to 1 "and then back them with every resource." Nobody knows the winners in advance, so even the best firms hold a portfolio, but every company in it must have the potential to succeed at vast scale. Founders Fund focuses on five to seven companies per fund, each of which it thinks could become a multibillion-dollar business on its unique fundamentals. "Whenever you shift from the substance of a business to the financial question of whether or not it fits into a diversified hedging strategy, venture investing starts to look a lot like buying lottery tickets. And once you think that you're playing the lottery, you've already psychologically prepared yourself to lose."

## Why people don't see the power law

- **It shows only over time.** At the start of a fund, 10 companies with monopoly potential show no divergence in returns. Mid-fund, valuations diverge but exponential and linear growth are hard to tell apart. After 10 years the portfolio is split "between one dominant investment and everything else."
- **Daily experience hides it.** Investors spend most of their time on new investments and early-stage companies, which are by definition average. The differences they see every day are between relative levels of success, not between exponential dominance and failure. And since nobody wants to give up, VCs spend more time on their most problematic companies than on their most obviously successful ones.
- **It hides in plain sight in the economy.** Under 1% of new U.S. businesses get venture funding each year, and VC investment is under 0.2% of GDP. Yet venture-backed companies create 11% of private-sector jobs and generate annual revenues equal to 21% of GDP. The dozen largest tech companies were all venture-backed, and together are worth more than $2 trillion, more than all other tech companies combined.

## What to do with the power law

**Everybody is an investor.** A founder invests her time in a startup; anyone choosing a career bets that the work will be valuable decades from now. Folk wisdom and financial convention answer "Don't put all your eggs in one basket," treating dabbling as a hedge. Investors who understand the power law "make as few investments as possible." And **"life is not a portfolio"**: a founder cannot run dozens of companies and hope one works, and an individual cannot diversify a life by keeping dozens of equally possible careers in reserve.

Schools teach the opposite: 45-minute periods whatever the subject, everyone at the same pace, model students hedging with "a suite of exotic and minor skills," and course catalogs that seem to say "it doesn't matter what you do, as long as you do it well." "That is completely false. It does matter what you do. You should focus relentlessly on something you're good at doing, but before that you must think hard about whether it will be valuable in the future."

**Join or found?** Don't necessarily start your own company, even if you are extraordinarily talented; too many people are starting companies. Those who understand the power law hesitate more, knowing how successful they could become by joining the very best company while it's growing fast. "Differences between companies will dwarf the differences in roles inside companies." 100% of a failed venture is 100% of nothing; 0.01% of Google was worth more than $35 million.

**Operating under the power law.** The most important things are singular:

- One market will probably be better than all others (see [`monopoly.md`](monopoly.md)).
- One distribution strategy usually dominates all others (see [`distribution.md`](distribution.md)).
- Time and decision-making follow a power law: some moments, above all the founding, matter far more than others (see [`foundations-and-mafia.md`](foundations-and-mafia.md)).

A world that denies the power law won't frame your decisions accurately, so what matters most is rarely obvious and might even be secret. "In a power law world, you can't afford not to think hard about where your actions will fall on the curve."
