# The definite future

## Two kinds of progress

The future is not just moments yet to come; it is "a time when the world looks different from today." If nothing changes for 100 years, the future is 100 years away. It will be different, and it must be rooted in today's world.

- **Horizontal or extensive progress**: copying things that work, going from 1 to n. Easy to imagine because we know what it looks like. One typewriter built into 100 typewriters. At the macro level its word is **globalization**: making what works somewhere work everywhere. China is the paradigm, its 20-year plan being to become like the U.S. today, copying railroads, air conditioning and whole cities (sometimes skipping steps, like going straight to wireless).
- **Vertical or intensive progress**: doing new things, going from 0 to 1. Hard to imagine because it requires doing what nobody has done. A typewriter turned into a word processor. Its word is **technology**: "any new and better way of doing things," not just computers.

The two modes are independent: 1815 to 1914 had both; from the First World War to Kissinger's 1971 trip to China, technology without much globalization; since 1971, rapid globalization with limited technology, mostly in IT. Dividing the world into "developed" and developing nations implies a technological end of history in which poor nations need only catch up. Thiel's contrarian answer: technology matters more. If China doubles its energy production with today's tools it doubles its air pollution; if India's households lived as Americans do with today's tools the result would be environmentally catastrophic. "In a world of scarce resources, globalization without new technology is unsustainable."

Relentless progress ran from the steam engine of the 1760s to about 1970; since midcentury only computers and communications have improved dramatically. Our parents expected a four-day workweek, energy too cheap to meter and vacations on the moon. They were wrong only to expect progress to be automatic.

## Startup thinking

New technology tends to come from small groups bound by a mission (the Founding Fathers, the Royal Society, Fairchild's "traitorous eight"). Big bureaucracies move slowly and shy from risk; in the most dysfunctional ones, "signaling that work is being done becomes a better strategy for career advancement than actually doing work (if this describes your company, you should quit now)." A lone genius can make a work of art but not an industry. A startup is the middle path: work with others, but stay small enough that you can.

**A startup is "the largest group of people you can convince of a plan to build a different future."** Its most important strength is new thinking; "even more important than nimbleness, small size affords space to think."

## The dot-com lessons and their opposites

"The first step to thinking clearly is to question what we think we know about the past." Conventional beliefs only look arbitrary in retrospect; when one collapses we call it a bubble, but its distortions outlive it. The 1990s were mostly malaise (recession, slow recovery, anxiety about globalization); amid crises in East Asia, Russia, LTCM and the euro, the internet looked like the only way forward, so tech investors were exuberant without being clearly irrational. The mania lasted 18 months, September 1998 to March 2000, when "irrationality was rational given that appending '.com' to your name could double your value overnight." The NASDAQ peaked at 5,048 on March 10, 2000, fell to 3,321 by mid-April, and bottomed at 1,114 in October 2002. Afterward, everyone learned to treat the future as indefinite and to call anyone with plans measured in years rather than quarters an extremist; investors fled to bricks (housing) and BRICs (globalization), inflating the real-estate bubble.

The survivors took four lessons that became startup dogma:

1. **Make incremental advances.** Grand visions inflated the bubble; small steps are the only safe path.
2. **Stay lean and flexible.** "Lean" is "code for 'unplanned'": iterate, and treat entrepreneurship as agnostic experimentation.
3. **Improve on the competition.** Start with an existing customer and improve on recognizable products.
4. **Focus on product, not sales.** A product that needs advertising or salespeople isn't good enough; only viral growth is sustainable.

The opposite principles are probably more correct:

1. **"It is better to risk boldness than triviality."**
2. **"A bad plan is better than no plan."**
3. **"Competitive markets destroy profits."**
4. **"Sales matters just as much as product."**

March 2000 was a peak of insanity and also "a peak of clarity": people looked far ahead and judged themselves capable of building what was needed. Rejecting the post-crash dogmas does not make their opposites automatically true: "you can't escape the madness of crowds by dogmatically rejecting them."

## Luck or design?

Successful people often credit luck (Gladwell's "patchwork of lucky breaks and arbitrary advantages," Buffett's "ovarian lottery," Bezos's "half luck, half good timing, and the rest brains"). Serial entrepreneurs who build several billion-dollar companies (Jobs, Dorsey, Musk) argue against luck as the main cause. The debate can't be settled objectively: you can't rewind to 2004 and start Facebook in 1,000 copies of the world, and "Statistics doesn't work when the sample size is one." From the Renaissance to the mid-20th century, luck was something to master: "Shallow men believe in luck, believe in circumstances.... Strong men believe in cause and effect" (Emerson); "Victory awaits him who has everything in order—luck, people call it" (Amundsen). The important question is about the future: chance or design?

## Definite vs indefinite

- A **definite** view treats the future as knowable and shapeable: understand it in advance and work to shape it. It favors firm convictions: a definite person "determines the one best thing to do and then does it," striving to be great at something substantive, "a monopoly of one."
- An **indefinite** view treats the future as ruled by randomness, so you give up trying to master it. "Process trumps substance": lacking concrete plans, people use formal rules to assemble a portfolio of options. Students hoard extracurriculars and curate omnicompetent résumés, pursuing "many-sided mediocrity" and calling it "well-roundedness," ready "for nothing in particular."

Cross definite/indefinite with optimistic/pessimistic (does the future get better or worse?):

|---|---|---|
| **Optimistic** | U.S., 1950s–1960s | U.S., 1982–present |
| **Pessimistic** | China, present | Europe, present |

- **Indefinite pessimism**: a bleak future and no idea what to do. Europe since the early 1970s, in undirected bureaucratic drift; the ECB "might as well print 'Kick the Can Down the Road' on the euro." People react to events, hope things don't get worse, and eat, drink and be merry meanwhile (hence the vacation mania).
- **Definite pessimism**: the future can be known but will be bleak, so prepare. China, growing about 10% a year since 2000 by relentlessly copying the West, burning coal for factories and skyscrapers, knowing its living standards cannot catch up given resource limits. "China is the only country afraid that it won't" take over the world. Leaders who lived through famine, the rich moving money abroad and the poor saving everything: every class takes the future deadly seriously.
- **Definite optimism**: the future will be better if you plan and work to make it better. The West from the 17th century through the 1950s and '60s: the Thames tunnel (1843), Suez (1869), Panama (1914), the Empire State Building (1929–1931), the Golden Gate Bridge (1933–1937), the Manhattan Project (1941–1945), the Interstate system (begun 1956, 20,000 miles open by 1965), Apollo (1961–1972, 12 men on the moon). Bold plans weren't reserved for officials: John Reber, a schoolteacher and self-taught engineer, proposed dams across San Francisco Bay, freshwater lakes and 20,000 reclaimed acres, and was taken seriously enough for congressional hearings and a 1.5-acre Army Corps scale model (which revealed flaws, so it wasn't built). Then, people welcomed big plans and asked whether they would work; today the same plan from a schoolteacher is crankery and from anyone powerful is hubris. "In the 1950s, Americans thought big plans for the future were too important to be left to experts."
- **Indefinite optimism**: the future will be better, but he doesn't know how, so he makes no specific plans; "He expects to profit from the future but sees no reason to design it concretely." Dominant in America since 1982, when a long bull market began and finance eclipsed engineering. Indefinite optimists rearrange what already exists: bankers rearrange capital structures, lawyers resolve disputes over old things, private equity and consultants squeeze efficiency from old businesses. These fields draw "Ivy League optionality chasers" to careers that "keep options open." Baby Boomers saw life improve every year without doing anything, and the elite among them kept improving after progress stalled in the 1970s, so they expect tracked careers to work for their children. Gladwell's luck thesis reflects a generation that learned "to overrate the power of chance and underrate the importance of planning."

### The indefinite world

- **Indefinite finance.** "Finance epitomizes indefinite thinking because it's the only way to make money when you have no idea how to create wealth." Its tenet is that the market is random, so diversification is supreme. After an exit, founders give money to a bank, the bank spreads it across institutional investors, they spread it across stocks, companies return free cash flow as dividends and buybacks, and the cycle repeats: no one in the chain knows what to do with money in the real economy. In an indefinite world "money is more valuable than anything you could possibly do with it. Only in a definite future is money a means to an end, not the end itself."
- **Indefinite politics.** Polling lets politicians match preexisting opinion; we care more about forecasts of what the country will think in a few weeks than visions of what it will look like in 10 or 20 years. Government once coordinated atomic weapons and lunar exploration; now it mainly provides insurance (entitlement spending has exceeded discretionary every year since 1975). Discretionary spending needs definite plans to solve specific problems.
- **Indefinite philosophy.** The ancients were pessimists (definite: Plato, Aristotle; indefinite: Epicurus, Lucretius). The 19th century's Spencer, Hegel and Marx were definite optimists. Rawls and Nozick are indefinite optimists with no specific vision of the future, both focused on process (Rawls's "veil of ignorance"; Nozick against "patterned" justice). "Arguing over process has become a way to endlessly defer making concrete plans for a better future."
- **Indefinite life.** Renaissance minds treated death as something to defeat (Bacon called "the prolongation of life" the noblest branch of medicine). Nineteenth-century life tables reduced death to a probability, and we traded the search for longevity's secrets for better insurance: "death is both inevitable and random." Drug discovery tries to amplify Fleming's accidental penicillin by random search. **Eroom's law** (Moore's law backward): the number of new drugs approved per billion dollars of R&D has halved every nine years since 1950.

| | Biotech startups | Software startups |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Uncontrollable organisms | Perfectly determinate code |
| Environment | Poorly understood, natural | Well understood, artificial |
| Approach | Indefinite, random | Definite, engineering |
| Regulation | Heavily regulated | Basically unregulated |
| Cost | Expensive (> $1B per drug) | Cheap (a little seed money) |
| Team | High-salaried, unaligned lab drones | Committed entrepreneurial hackers |

Biology's real difficulty may have become an excuse for an indefinite approach to business: professors become part-time consultants even to startups built on their own research, and everyone imitates them. Heavy regulation holds biotech back, but "indefinite optimism may pose an even greater challenge."

## Is indefinite optimism possible?

U.S. households save almost nothing and U.S. companies pile up cash without investing because they have no concrete plans. In the quadrant chart, investment runs low on the indefinite side and high on the definite side; savings run low in the optimistic row and high in the pessimistic row. The other three views can work: definite optimism builds the future it envisions; definite pessimism builds what can be copied without expecting anything new; indefinite pessimism is self-fulfilling ("if you're a slacker with low expectations, they'll probably be met"). Indefinite optimism "seems inherently unsustainable: how can the future get better if no one plans for it?"

Its answer is evolution, progress without planning, and pseudo-Darwinian metaphors fill business writing ("Digital Darwinism," "Survival of the Clickiest"). Silicon Valley's version is the **lean startup** that adapts and evolves, listens to what customers say they want, ships a "minimum viable product" and iterates. But **"leanness is a methodology, not a goal."** Small changes to what exists can reach a **local maximum**, never the **global maximum**: you could build the best app for ordering toilet paper from an iPhone, but "iteration without a bold plan won't take you from 0 to 1." A company is the strangest place for indefinite optimism: why expect your business to succeed without a plan? "Darwinism may be a fine theory in other contexts, but in startups, intelligent design works best."

## The return of design

"Every great entrepreneur is first and foremost a designer," but the lesson of Jobs is not aesthetics: "The greatest thing Jobs designed was his business." Apple imagined and executed definite multi-year plans to create new products and distribute them effectively. "Forget 'minimum viable products'": you can change the world through careful planning, not focus-group feedback or copying others. Analysts saw the first iPod (October 2001) as "a nice feature for Macintosh users"; Jobs planned it as the first of a generation of post-PC devices, a secret invisible to most people, and Apple's stock over the following decade shows the harvest.

**Planning and valuation.** A big company bidding for a successful startup almost always offers too much or too little. Founders sell only when they have no more concrete visions, so the buyer overpaid; definite founders with robust plans don't sell, so the offer was too low. When Yahoo! offered $1 billion for Facebook in July 2006, Zuckerberg told the board: "We're obviously not going to sell here." "A business with a good definite plan will always be underrated in a world where people see the future as random."

## You are not a lottery ticket

Returning to a definite future takes nothing short of a cultural revolution, and philosophy departments, bestseller lists and politics are crowded fields that are hard to change. But "a startup is the largest endeavor over which you can have definite mastery," agency over a small and important part of the world. "It begins by rejecting the unjust tyranny of Chance. You are not a lottery ticket."

## Stagnation or singularity

Even the most farsighted founders cannot plan beyond the next 20 to 30 years, but the far future's broad contours are visible. Nick Bostrom's four patterns:

- **Recurrent collapse**: endless alternation of prosperity and ruin, as the ancients saw history. Unlikely now: knowledge is so widespread that complete annihilation is more probable than a dark age followed by recovery.
- **Plateau**: the world converges on the life of today's richest countries and the future looks like the present. This is what most people expect, which means they expect no future at all. Even at best, competition would be fiercer than ever for every person and firm, and with competition for scarce resources, "without new technology to relieve competitive pressures, stagnation is likely to erupt into conflict." Global conflict collapses stagnation into extinction.
- **Extinction**: a collapse so devastating we don't survive it.
- **Takeoff**: accelerating progress toward a much better future, so different as to defy description. Its most dramatic form is the **Singularity**; Ray Kurzweil projects exponential trends and says "the Singularity is near" and inevitable.

"No matter how many trends can be traced, the future won't happen on its own." The stark choice is between the two most likely scenarios, "nothing or something. It's up to us." We cannot take for granted that the future will be better, so we must work to create it today, starting with the unique opportunities to do new things in our own working lives.
