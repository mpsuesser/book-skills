# The seven questions

Early in the 21st century everyone agreed clean technology was the next big thing: thousands of cleantech companies, more than $50 billion invested, and a bubble (Solyndra; more than 40 solar manufacturers out of business or bankrupt in 2012 alone; the renewable-energy index rose to a peak in late 2007, crashed in 2008 and slid further through 2012). The failure was more than government's. Most cleantech companies crashed because they neglected one or more of the seven questions every business must answer, and many started with zero good answers, "and that meant hoping for a miracle." Use cleantech's losing scorecard and Tesla's 7-for-7 as the worked examples when you run the questions on any plan.

## 1. The Engineering Question

*Can you create breakthrough technology instead of incremental improvements?*

- **Test**: proprietary technology an order of magnitude (10x) better than its nearest substitute. Cleantech rarely produced 2x, and sometimes was worse than what it replaced: Solyndra's cylindrical solar cells were, to a first approximation, only as efficient as flat ones while catching less direct sunlight, and mirrors couldn't fix it. "It's hard to recover from a radically inferior starting point."
- **Why 10x**: incremental improvements often mean no improvement for the end user. A wind turbine 20% more efficient in the lab won't cover the real-world costs and risks of a new product, and even a true 20% net gain meets skepticism from customers used to exaggerated claims. "Only when your product is 10x better can you offer the customer transparent superiority."

## 2. The Timing Question

*Is now the right time to start your particular business?*

- **Test**: is the underlying technology advancing in a way that suggests liftoff? SpectraWatt's CEO likened solar in 2008 to microprocessors in the late 1970s. But microprocessors advanced exponentially from the first one in 1970 (Intel: 4-bit 4004 in 1971, 8-bit 8008 in 1972, 16-bit 8086 in 1978, 32-bit iAPX 432 in 1981), while solar efficiency crept up linearly from Bell Labs' first silicon cell of 1954 (about 6%) to field efficiencies still under 25%. Little in the mid-2000s suggested impending liftoff.
- **Rule**: "Entering a slow-moving market can be a good strategy, but only if you have a definite and realistic plan to take it over."

## 3. The Monopoly Question

*Are you starting with a big share of a small market?*

- **Test the pitch's first slide.** A trillion-dollar market is a warning, not a selling point: "huge, trillion-dollar markets mean ruthless, bloody competition." When John Doerr announced that "green is the new red, white and blue," he could have stopped at "red."
- **Test claims of an edge.** MiaSolé's CEO told Congress his company was one of several "very strong" thin-film startups, then predicted it would become the world's largest thin-film producer within a year; thin-film is one of more than a dozen kinds of solar cell. "Customers won't care about any particular technology unless it solves a particular problem in a superior way." MiaSolé sold in 2013 for hundreds of millions less than its investors had put in.
- **Test the market definition** by widening it ring by ring and recomputing share (100 MW is 10.53% of U.S. solar, under 1% of global solar, 0.02% of global renewables). Cleantech founders shrank their market to seem differentiated, then asked to be valued on huge markets. "You can't dominate a submarket if it's fictional, and huge markets are highly competitive, not highly attainable." For the full method see [`monopoly.md`](monopoly.md).

## 4. The People Question

*Do you have the right team?*

- **Test**: does a technical problem have a technical team? Energy problems are engineering problems, yet the failed cleantech companies were run by "shockingly nontechnical" salesman-executives, good at raising capital and winning subsidies, less good at building products customers wanted.
- **Founders Fund's shortcut**: pass on any company whose founders dressed up for pitch meetings. "Never invest in a tech CEO that wears a suit." The best sales is hidden: "There's nothing wrong with a CEO who can sell, but if he actually *looks* like a salesman, he's probably bad at sales and worse at tech."

## 5. The Distribution Question

*Do you have a way to not just create but deliver your product?*

- **Test**: is it easy for the customer to know what they're buying and to buy it? "The world is not a laboratory: selling and delivering a product is at least as important as the product itself." Cleantech courted governments and investors and forgot customers.
- **Better Place** raised and spent more than $800 million (2007–2012) on swappable batteries and charging stations, sold 1,000 cars, and went bankrupt. Buyers couldn't tell whether they were buying an electric Renault or a Better Place, then had to win the company's approval by proving they lived near a swap station and would follow predictable routes, sign a fueling subscription, and learn to stop and swap batteries. It thought its technology spoke for itself and didn't market clearly; one customer asked why there was no billboard comparing a Prius at 160,000 shekels with this car at 160,000 plus four years of fuel. Its board's epitaph: "The technical challenges we overcame successfully, but the other obstacles we were not able to overcome." For channel choice see [`distribution.md`](distribution.md).

## 6. The Durability Question

*Will your market position be defensible 10 and 20 years into the future?*

- **Ask**: "what will the world look like 10 and 20 years from now, and how will my business fit in?" Plan to be the last mover in your market.
- **Name the specific threat and ask what stops it.** Cleantech's obituaries read alike, blaming Chinese competitors' subsidies and "aggressive pricing," and some sued them. But Chinese competition was predictable; the right question was "what will stop China from wiping out my business?"
- **Test your assumptions about the wider market.** An industry premised on the twilight of fossil fuels was blindsided by fracking: shale gas went from 1.7% of U.S. natural gas in 2000 to 4.1% in 2005 to 34% in 2013, and gas prices fell more than 70% from 2008, devastating renewable business models.

## 7. The Secret Question

*Have you identified a unique opportunity that others don't see?*

- **Test**: is the company's justification a convention everyone shares? Cleantech companies justified themselves with conventional truths about the need for a cleaner world, mistaking "an overwhelming social need for alternative energy solutions" for an overwhelming business opportunity. By 2006 a president, investors and executives were all bullish on solar; one CEO spoke of "a gold rush feeling." Q-Cells, Evergreen Solar, SpectraWatt and Energy Innovations went from promising beginnings to bankruptcy court. "Great companies have secrets: specific reasons for success that other people don't see." For finding them see [`secrets.md`](secrets.md).

## The myth of social entrepreneurship

Cleantech was the biggest phenomenon, and biggest flop, of **social entrepreneurship**, which aims to combine corporate power with nonprofit purpose and "do well by doing good." "Usually they end up doing neither." The ambiguity of "social" is the deeper problem: is something socially good because it's good for society, or merely seen as good by society? "Whatever is good enough to receive applause from all audiences can only be conventional, like the general idea of green energy." Progress isn't held back by a difference between corporate greed and nonprofit goodness but by the sameness of both: corporations copy each other, nonprofits push the same priorities, and cleantech produced "hundreds of undifferentiated products all in the name of one overbroad goal." "Doing something different is what's truly good for society—and it's also what allows a business to profit by monopolizing a new market." "The best problems to work on are often the ones nobody else even tries to solve."

## Tesla: 7 for 7

Tesla rode cleantech's social buzz better than anyone and got all seven right.

1. **Technology**: good enough that other carmakers rely on it (Daimler uses its battery packs, Mercedes-Benz a powertrain, Toyota a motor), and its greatest achievement is integration: the Model S, designed end to end, is more than the sum of its parts (Consumer Reports rated it higher than any car it had reviewed; Motor Trend and Automobile named it 2013 Car of the Year).
2. **Timing**: where others saw subsidies flowing indefinitely, Musk saw a one-time-only opportunity, securing a $465 million Department of Energy loan in January 2010, about a year and a half before Solyndra's collapse politicized subsidies. It was unthinkable before and after.
3. **Monopoly**: started with a tiny submarket it could dominate, high-end electric sports cars (about 3,000 Roadsters at $109,000), which funded the R&D for the Model S; it then owned the luxury electric sedan market (more than 20,000 sold in 2013), positioned to expand further.
4. **Team**: the CEO is "the consummate engineer *and* salesman," with a team strong at both; Musk likens working at Tesla to choosing Special Forces over the regular army.
5. **Distribution**: owns the whole chain, selling and servicing cars in its own stores instead of through independent dealerships. Higher up-front cost, but control of the customer experience, a stronger brand and long-run savings.
6. **Durability**: a head start plus moving faster than anyone means a widening lead; a coveted brand in a category where trust is hard to win (a car is one of the biggest purchases people make); and the founder is still in charge.
7. **Secret**: fashion drove interest in cleantech. The rich wanted to look green, even in a boxy Prius or clunky Honda Insight that looked cool only by association with eco-conscious stars. Tesla built cars that made drivers look cool, period. The secret: "cleantech was even more of a social phenomenon than an environmental imperative."

## Macro need, micro plan

Nothing was inherently wrong with cleantech's big idea: the world will need new energy, and "Energy is the master resource." But indefinitely optimistic investors funding companies with no specific plans produced a bubble with the same shape as the NASDAQ's a decade earlier. The '90s had one big idea (the internet will be big), and too many companies had that idea and no other. "An entrepreneur can't benefit from macro-scale insight unless his own plans begin at the micro-scale." "No sector will ever be so important that merely participating in it will be enough to build a great company."

The dream of the '90s proved right, and energy startups could follow as Web 2.0 followed the dot-coms, but only by finding a niche and dominating a small market, as Facebook began with one campus. Thiel's examples of small energy markets: replacing diesel as a power source for remote islands, or modular reactors for quick deployment at military installations in hostile territory. "Paradoxically, the challenge for the entrepreneurs who will create Energy 2.0 is to think small."
