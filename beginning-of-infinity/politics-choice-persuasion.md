# Politics, choice and persuasion

Deutsch's account of political institutions, collective and individual decision-making, and how ideas pass between minds. *Popper's criterion* and the *principle of optimism* are defined in `SKILL.md`.

## From "who should rule?" to Popper's criterion

- **Replace the question of sources.** Popper (in "Knowledge without Authority") replaced the question of where knowledge comes from with "How can we hope to detect and eliminate error?" The answer is a tradition of criticism that seeks good explanations of what has gone wrong, what would be better, and what policies will do. Policies "cannot be *derived* from anything. They are conjectures," chosen "not on the basis of their origin, but according to how good they are as explanations: how hard to vary." Objective progress is possible in politics.
- **"Who should rule?" is the political form of empiricism.** It looks for a source from which the right ruler can be derived or justified: inheritance, the majority, education. Blind optimism, blind pessimism, induction, instrumentalism and Lamarckism all expect *explanationless progress*: "knowledge to be created by fiat with few errors, and not by a process of variation and selection that is making a continual stream of errors and correcting them." The question "begs for violent, authoritarian answers": tyranny and revolution.
- **Popper's question**: "*how can we rid ourselves of bad governments without violence?*" "Systems of government are to be judged not for their prophetic ability to choose and install good leaders and policies, but for their ability to remove bad ones that are already there." The ideal is "not that nothing unexpected will go wrong, but that when it does it will be an opportunity for further progress."
- **Why bother removing bad rulers?** Without an expectation of progress the answer is "better the devil you know". That makes a closed loop: if knowledge cannot grow, the precautionary principle is true, and if the precautionary principle is followed, knowledge cannot grow. Only societies that expect knowledge to grow, unpredictably and helpfully, meet Popper's criterion. Hereditary monarchy embodies the precautionary principle; revolutionary utopians are blind optimists.
- **Check for anti-rational conditions.** Examine laws, customs and institutions "with an eye to whether they set up conditions for anti-rational memes to evolve" (see `evolution-and-memes.md`). "Avoiding such conditions is the essence of Popper's criterion."

## The epistemology of persuasion (Socrates and Hermes)

Deutsch's dialogue between Socrates and the god Hermes states this epistemology. It is "largely that of… Karl Popper, together with some addenda of my own".

- **Customs.** Athenians defy anyone seeking their submission, and give a hearing to honest criticism that seeks to persuade freely: "two sides of the same valuable coin".
- **No source is privileged.** A just person must be persuaded of even a god's moral rightness by their own standards. "Situations can easily arise in which you disbelieve your closest friend and believe your worst enemy. What matters in all cases is the explanation you create, within your own mind." "I have no need to trust the source if the argument itself is persuasive. And no way of using *any* source unless I also have a persuasive argument." Even revealed knowledge is "just as fallible as ever". "It all comes from within, from conjecture and criticism."
- **Nothing is easy to see.** Eyelids, darkness, dazzle, mirrors, mirages, crumpled clothes, dreams and conjurers all deceive, and you cannot certify that viewing conditions are good without circularity. "*Nothing* is easy to see without prior knowledge." Yet one can still "conceive of *knowing*". Certainty is unwanted: it is boring, and useful only "to provide a semblance of an argument when one doesn't have a real one".
- **Communication is guesswork.** "When we hear something being said, we *guess* what it means, without realizing what we are doing." If you misunderstand a captain's directions, the wrong idea came from within you. "We habitually underestimate the difficulty of communication." The communication of new ideas, "even mundane ones like directions", depends on guesswork on both sides, and "the default assumption should be that misunderstandings are ubiquitous and that neither intelligence nor the intention to be accurate is any guarantee against them."
- **Senses check guesses; they do not supply them.** Sensory input is needed not in the source of our ideas but in their criticism. Tested against the senses, the guess becomes "a waking dream that *corresponds* to reality", and what we understand we then control. "It is like magic, only real."
- **Converge on the truth to converge with each other.** "People who converge upon the truth converge with each other." Spartans agree through enforced conformity, Athenians through critical debate. Scientists do not read Einstein's originals, because "the original sources of scientific theories are almost never good sources". Learners criticizing their initial guesses about a good theory "will arrive at the same theory as the originator. *That* is how the theory manages to be passed faithfully." "The way to converge with each other is to converge upon the truth."
- **Xenophanes.** "The gods did not reveal, from the beginning, / All things to us; but in the course of time, / Through seeking we may learn and know things better." And: "all is but a woven web of guesses."

**Sparta's trap, and the deadliest error.**

- Educating children to hold ideas immune from criticism is wrong, and that follows from epistemology alone. Sparta's way of life traps its people because "one of their mistaken beliefs is that they must take no steps to prevent their way of life from misleading and trapping them!"
- There is an asymmetry. If an unchanging Sparta were not trapped, it would have to be already perfect, right about everything. But "our very idea that improvement is possible implies that there *must* be errors and inadequacies in our current ideas."
- No idea is immune from criticism: not "2+2=4", nor fallibilism itself, which a true fallibilist criticizes too. "My doubt *improved* my knowledge."
- Banning debate, philosophy or elections bans persuasion. "This is a rare and deadly sort of error: it prevents itself from being undone."
- Socrates asks: "Could it be that the moral imperative *not to destroy the means of correcting mistakes* is the only moral imperative? That all other moral truths follow from it?"

**The thievery thought experiment.** A city legalizes theft. Its citizens first conclude there is not yet *enough* thievery, then meet setbacks, which are problems. Some suspect the law, find a better explanation and persuade the majority. "Salvation would come about through persuasion… Thought, explanation and persuasion."

**Athens vs Sparta.** The single source of all their differences is *improvement* vs *stasis*. Athens's laws accommodate rival ideas of perfection, criticize them and winnow them, so "myriad individuals who can conceive of no improvement of themselves nevertheless add up to a *city* that relentlessly seeks nothing else." "We can improve because we are constantly striving to; they hardly ever improve, because they are trying *not* to!"

## Choices: why "the will of the people" is incoherent

**The apportionment problem.** How many House seats should each US state get?

- A state's ***quota*** is its exact proportional share. An *apportionment rule* rounds quotas. A rule *stays within the quota* if no state's seats ever differ from its quota by a whole seat.
- Deutsch's first guess, to round each state's quota "to the nearest whole number", violates quota. Take ten seats and four states, one with just under 85 per cent of the population and three with just over 5 per cent each. Rounding gives 8 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 11 seats, and 85 per cent of 11 is 9.35, so the large state is short by more than a seat.
- *Reallocation schemes* stay within quota but produce *apportionment paradoxes*:
  - The ***population paradox***: a state whose population grew loses a seat to one whose population shrank.
  - The ***Alabama paradox***: adding seats to the House makes a state lose one.
- Washington's first presidential veto (1792) blocked Hamilton's rule, and Congress has tinkered ever since. The issue went twice to the National Academy of Sciences, with different recommendations each time, and no one ever accused a predecessor of a mathematical error. That "ought to have warned everyone that this problem is not really about mathematics."
- ***Balinski and Young's Theorem*** (1975): "Every apportionment rule that stays within the quota suffers from the population paradox."
- The paradoxes have the "infuriating property that, no matter how firmly they are kicked out of the front door, they instantly come in again at the back." Every "why don't they just…?" fix (round, reallocate, add seats, tune the House size until two rules agree, majority-vote among rules) fails, and a combined scheme is just another scheme. "If it was not *designed* to be good, why should it be?"

**Arrow's theorem** (1951). Kenneth Arrow laid down five axioms that any rule defining the preferences of a group should satisfy:

1. The group's preferences are defined only in terms of its members' preferences.
2. No dictator: the rule may not simply adopt one person's views regardless of the others.
3. If the members are unanimous about something, the group has that preference.
4. If members who disagreed with a group preference switch to it, the group keeps it. (This is akin to ruling out the population paradox.)
5. If members change their minds about something else, such as strawberries vs raspberries, the group's preference between pizza and hamburger is unchanged.

They are logically inconsistent. "There is no such thing as 'the will of the people'." No way of regarding "society" as a decision-maker with self-consistent preferences works. Weighting preferences by intensity changes nothing. Such systems also reward lying about one's preferences.

**Weighing is the wrong model of deciding, for groups and for individuals.**

- Arrow applies inside one mind if a person "weighs" evidence, with each consideration a voter. Then all thinking is irrational, unless some internal dictator overrides the rest, which is an infinite regress.
- The error is to model decision as "selecting from existing options according to a fixed formula". That is only the end, the perspiration phase. "Decision-making is problem-solving… At the heart of decision-making is the creation of new options and the abandonment or modification of existing ones."
- "To choose an option, rationally, is to choose the associated explanation. Therefore, rational decision-making consists not of weighing evidence but of explaining it." Rival explanations are "not outweighed, but out-argued, refuted and abandoned." The struggle is usually to create even one good explanation.
- **No splitting the difference.** A good explanation is hard to mix with a rival. "Something halfway between them is usually worse than either." Good explanations are discrete, separated by bad ones, and mixing two into a better one takes a further act of creativity.
- **Perspiration comes last.** An architect asked how tall a skyscraper can be begins creatively, with design ideas, after the clients have creatively decided their own priorities. A voter can weight issues only after working out a political philosophy.
- **Social-choice theory makes Zeno's mistake.** Its "decision" is choosing a checkbox from fixed options by fixed preferences, so it mistakes an abstract process it has named decision-making for the real one. The no-go theorems are true; it is their model that is false. Including creativity in the model is impossible for now: "Such a model would *be* a creative process: an AI."
- **Arrow's "dictator" is technical.** It means any agent given sole say over some decision: your body under laws against rape or involuntary surgery, your possessions, your own ballot. Freedom of thought and speech, and self-determination, all require "dictatorships" in Arrow's sense.
- **Regretting the theorems is itself the error.** Webster wrote "that which cannot be done perfectly must be done in a manner as near perfection as can be." But "what sort of 'perfection' is a *logical contradiction*?" "If your conception of justice conflicts with the demands of logic or rationality then it is unjust. If your conception of rationality conflicts with a mathematical theorem… then your conception of rationality is irrational." "Wishing for something that is logically impossible is a sign that there is something better to wish for." The better thing is Popper's criterion.

## Judging institutions by Popper's criterion

- **The question to ask.** Instead of entrenching the most self-evident of the mutually inconsistent criteria of fairness, judge institutions "according to how well they promote the removal of bad rulers and bad policies." They must embody traditions of peaceful, critical discussion.
- **Fairness concerns input; rationality concerns removal.** Conditions of "fairness" are about the *input*: who participates and how opinions are combined. Like empiricism, that is the wrong focus. Non-discrimination still matters, not so everyone contributes to the answer, but because discrimination "entrenches in the system a preference among their potential *criticisms*." Progress requires "to *exclude* ideas that fail to survive criticism."
- **What elections are for.** "The essence of democratic decision-making is not the choice made by the system at elections, but the ideas created between elections." Voters are not "a fount of wisdom from which the right policies can be empirically 'derived'"; they are fallibly seeking the truth. "Society is not a zero-sum game." In elections voters "are choosing which experiments are to be attempted next, and (principally) which are to be abandoned… The politicians, and their policies, are those experiments." The no-go models wrongly assume no one persuades anyone. In reality "neither the preferences nor the options need be the same at the end of a decision-making process as they were at the beginning."
- **Compromise policies** have "an undeservedly high reputation". They are better than violence, but "if a policy is no one's idea of what will work, then why should it work?" Their key defect: "when one of them is implemented and fails, no one learns anything because no one ever agreed with it." That shields the underlying explanations from criticism.
- **Plurality voting vs proportional representation.**
  - *Plurality* ("first-past-the-post") over-represents the two largest parties and can suffer paradoxes. But it usually gives one party sole charge and removes the losers entirely. Every party must persuade a large share of voters or face powerlessness. Winning policies are tested unmixed, winners cannot pass the blame, and "a small change in the vote produces a relatively large change (in the same direction!)".
  - In Manitoba in 1926 the Conservatives won more than twice the votes of any other party and none of seventeen seats. At the next election a 3 per cent rise in their vote brought a 17 per cent rise in seats, restoring rough proportionality.
  - *Proportional representation* gives disproportionate power to the third-largest party, which decides who governs. In Germany from 1949 to 1998 the FDP never exceeded 12.8 per cent of the vote, yet twice switched sides, three times installed the less popular large party, and for the last twenty-nine years Germany had only two weeks without an FDP foreign minister. Under PR power can move against public opinion, small parties proliferate, and even proportionality fails: in Israel's 1992 election the right won most votes and the left most seats. An analysis of eight Danish elections found paradoxes in every one.
  - Deutsch's verdict: "in the advanced political cultures of the Enlightenment tradition… the idea that representative government depends on proportionate representation in the legislature is unequivocally a mistake." This is not a claim that plurality voting is "the One True System" for every polity.
- **Convergence.** Scientists who keep disagreeing still reach near-unanimity on a steady stream of issues, because they converge on objective truth. Political cultures do too: on slavery, women working, autopsies, and promotion regardless of skin colour. Broad consensus is "the one state of public opinion that is not subject to decision-theoretic paradoxes and where 'the will of the people' makes sense." Women's suffrage dwarfed every apportionment injustice.

"The purpose of deferring to the majority in democratic systems should be to approach unanimity in the future, by giving all concerned the incentive to abandon bad ideas and to conjecture better ones. Creatively *changing the options* is what allows people in real life to cooperate in ways that no-go theorems seem to say are impossible; and it is what allows individual minds to choose at all." Human beings "will never disagree any less than they do now, and that is a very good thing": we advance "from misconception to ever better misconception."
