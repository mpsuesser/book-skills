# Morality and beauty

Deutsch's case that there are objective truths in morality and aesthetics, reached the same way as truths in science: by conjecture, criticism and the search for good explanations.

## Objective morality

- **The claim.** "There is an objective difference between right and wrong". Abstractions exist objectively though not physically, and causation and the laws of physics are themselves abstractions. Morality is more autonomous than physics: the laws of physics could themselves be *immoral*.
- ***Moral philosophy*** "addresses the problem of what sort of life to want": it is "basically about the problem of what to do next – and, more generally, what sort of life to lead, and what sort of world to want." Imagine being the last human on Earth: "do whatever pleases me most" gives little guidance, because what pleases depends on moral judgement.
- **Ought and is.** Hume's "you can't derive an *ought* from an *is*" led to dogmatic despair. "Certainly you can't derive an *ought* from an *is*, but you can't derive a *factual* theory from an *is* either." "Although factual evidence and moral maxims are logically independent, factual and moral *explanations* are not. Thus factual knowledge can be useful in criticizing moral explanations." A slave who writes a bestseller can ruin the explanation that Providence intends some people to be slaves. Immoral doctrines carry factual falsehoods, as 9/11 conspiracy theories do; they "bear the imprint of moral wrongness just as clearly as a fossil". Bronowski argued that science entails values: truth, openness, tolerance, integrity, open debate. "No true explanation is entirely disconnected from any other… morally right values are connected… with true factual theories, and morally wrong values with false theories."
- ***Utilitarianism*** defines right and wrong by usefulness in meeting people's preferences. But preferences are shaped by moral explanations. It played a role like empiricism's: liberating, with little truth.
- **How to criticize a moral claim.** Treat it as an explanation. Is it hard to vary? What factual claims does it entail, and are they true? Does it fit our other best explanations? Experience in philosophy supplies *problems*, by bringing existing ideas into conflict. "Good explanations about philosophical issues are as hard to find as in science – and criticism is correspondingly effective."
- **The candidate first principle.** In the Socrates–Hermes dialogue Socrates asks: "Could it be that the moral imperative *not to destroy the means of correcting mistakes* is the only moral imperative? That all other moral truths follow from it?"
- **Mechanical accounts of people are immoral.** "Mechanical reinterpretations of human affairs not only lack explanatory power, they are morally wrong as well", because they deny the participants' humanity. Behaviourism in psychology legitimizes dehumanizing theories of the mind as a non-creative automaton.
- **Cultural relativism** "has a very hard time explaining what people are doing when they think they are improving a tradition."

## Objective beauty

- **Hard to vary, again.** Peter Shaffer's play *Amadeus* describes Mozart's music this way: "Displace one note and there would be diminishment. Displace one phrase and the structure would fall." John Wheeler asked of a deep theory "how could it have been otherwise?" Both describe the same attribute: "being hard to vary while still doing the job".
- **Denial is a relic of empiricism.** The prevailing view is that there are no objective artistic standards: beauty is in the eye of the beholder, a matter of taste. That relies on the empiricist claim that philosophical knowledge cannot exist. Aesthetic values cannot be *deduced* from facts, but aesthetic truths are linked to physical facts through explanations. Facts can criticize aesthetic theories, and experience provides artistic problems.
- **Beauty is in the form.** The equivalent of instrumentalism treats art as no more than a means to non-artistic ends. Artistic creations can deliver information, "But their beauty is not primarily in that content. It is in the form." Of two photographs of a screw and a nut with much the same content, one can have greater aesthetic value: "In its composition, framing, cropping, lighting, focus – it has the *appearance of design* by the photographer." Yet "Unlike Paley's watch, it does not seem to have a function", which is the puzzle of what beauty is.
- ***Elegance***: beauty in explanations, mathematical formulae and so on. It is not the same as truth. Keats's "Beauty is truth, truth beauty" is refuted by Huxley's "the slaying of a beautiful hypothesis by an ugly fact". But "elegance is, at least, a useful heuristic when searching for fundamental truths", and a slain beautiful hypothesis is usually replaced by a more beautiful one, a regularity that itself needs explaining.
- **Creation takes errors.** Feynman's tools were paper, pencil and a waste-paper basket. Beethoven agonized over drafts; Mozart and Ramanujan did their discarding invisibly, in their heads. Was Beethoven fooling himself that his discarded sheets were mistakes, as with fashions in hemlines? If beauty is objective, new art adds something irreducibly new, and "real progress is difficult and involves many errors for every success".
- **Human taste is not inborn.** Animal taste is predictable and repetitive. Humans, as universal explainers, override their genes: fasting, abstinence, and skydiving, where an inborn aversion is reinterpreted into an attraction. That people can learn to find beetles beautiful does not show beauty is cultural, since people also learn false theories.

## Why flowers are beautiful

1. **The observation.** Flowers co-evolved with insects: the criteria and the means of meeting them evolved together. Yet flowers also attract humans, and that is amazing. Most species look hideous to us, tigers are striped for camouflage, and peacocks are a rare anomaly. Flowers are *reliably* beautiful, even ones never seen before, across cultures; their roots and leaves are not.
2. **The problem.** Flowers needed an inter-species code that the right insects recognize easily and that other flower species cannot forge cheaply, since nectar costs energy. So both the criterion and the means of meeting it had to be hard to vary. Signalling within a species can draw on shared genetic knowledge, as in sexual selection. Signalling across species starts from scratch.
3. **The guess.** "The easiest way to signal across such a gap with hard-to-forge patterns designed to be recognized by hard-to-emulate pattern-matching algorithms is to use objective standards of beauty." Honey is different: its appeal rests on a biochemical heritage with sugar that insects and humans share.
4. **Objections answered.** Is it just bright colours, contrast and symmetry? There are white flowers, a spider in the bath is symmetrical, and orchids are asymmetrical. Night skies, waterfalls and sunsets are attractive but lack the appearance of design, like the sun as a timekeeper. "Displace even one petal, and there would be diminishment." It is like an archaeologist meeting inscriptions in an unknown language.

Dawkins's daughter was partly right that flowers are there "to make the world pretty": prettiness is what they specifically evolved to have.

## Two kinds of beauty, two kinds of art

- **Parochial vs universal.** There is parochial attractiveness, local to a species, culture or individual, and universal beauty, "as objective as the laws of physics", which requires knowledge with universal reach. Flowers are not great art.
- **Why humans seek objective beauty.** Signalling between two humans is like signalling between species, since each human, in knowledge and creative individuality, is like a species. "To have any chance of communicating, we had better strive to rise above parochialism and seek universal truths." That may be the proximate reason humans began to. A corollary: human appearance, shaped by sexual selection, may be objectively more beautiful than apes'.
- **Applied vs pure.** *Applied* art solves signalling problems, usually with parochial beauty: interface design, songs for cohesion, politics or advertising drinks. *Pure* art seeks beauty for its own sake, including improved criteria and new styles. It is the analogue of pure scientific research, with the same states of mind, and both seek universal objective truth. "Artistic values are not subordinate to, or derived from, anything else." Utilitarian theories of art deprecate pure art.
- **Art and explanation.** A good story has a good explanation of its fictional events. Music's explanation is ***inexplicit***, not yet expressed in words or symbols, though one day it may be. Poetry and mathematics both develop variants of ordinary language. In appreciation the pure and applied, universal and parochial are mixed, but "only in the objective direction that we can expect to make unlimited progress. The other directions are inherently finite."
- **Against self-expression.** Expression conveys what is already there, and the self is subjective. Spontaneous or mechanical acts, such as throwing paint or pickling sheep, lack a means of progress.
- **The future of art** is unlimited. It may include new senses and new qualia. Answering Nagel's "What is it like to be a bat?" by giving the experience is a task for technological art.

**To judge a work or design**, ask whether it is hard to vary while still doing its job. Ask whether its appeal is parochial (to this audience, now) or would reach across a gap of shared assumptions. Ask what problem it solves, pure or applied.
