# People and universality

Deutsch's account of what people are, why they matter cosmically, how systems jump to universality, and what that implies for AI. *People*, *parochialism* and "everything not forbidden… is achievable" are defined in `SKILL.md`.

## Two misconceptions about human significance

- ***Anthropocentric***: centred on humans, or on people. The anthropocentric approach "never yielded any good explanations beyond the realm of human affairs". A phenomenon is ***significant*** or ***fundamental*** if it "plays a necessary role in the explanation of many phenomena, or whose distinctive features require distinctive explanation in terms of fundamental theories."
- ***The Principle of Mediocrity***: "there is nothing significant about humans (in the cosmic scheme of things)". Hawking's version: humans are "just a chemical scum on the surface of a typical planet".
- ***Spaceship Earth***: "The biosphere is a life-support system for humans." It pictures the Earth as a generation ship: self-sufficient, finite in capacity, with no second chances.
- Both oppose arrogance and carry a moral tone. Deutsch's truth is their negation: "People *are* significant in the cosmic scheme of things; and The Earth's biosphere is *incapable* of supporting human life."

**Against the Principle of Mediocrity.**

- We are atypical. About 80 per cent of matter is dark matter; ordinary matter mostly glows, and plasma is its commonest form. The background is 2.7 K, and the coldest places in the universe may be laboratory refrigerators. A typical place is intergalactic space, with less than one atom per cubic metre: "cold, dark and empty."
- The principle is itself anthropocentric, using value-laden terms such as "arrogance" and "scum".
- The ancients' error was parochial, not arrogant: "they were not arrogant *enough*: they assumed far too easily that the world was fundamentally incomprehensible to them."

**Against Spaceship Earth.**

- An Oxford winter kills an unprotected human. The Great Rift Valley, where we evolved, was "a death trap for humans rather than a life-support system". Populations live close to the edge: any improvement makes them grow until they are balanced again. The biosphere stays stable by "continually neglecting, harming, disabling and killing individuals". About 99.9 per cent of species are extinct. There are "very few fossils of old people".
- Tools, fire and clothing were transmitted culturally, not genetically. "Almost the entire capacity of the Earth's 'life-support system for humans' has been provided not *for* us but *by* us." "A heap of raw materials is not the same thing as a life-support system. It takes knowledge to convert the one into the other." "We have never been merely its passengers, nor… its stewards, nor even its maintenance crew: we are its designers and builders."
- The metaphor casts humans as ungrateful for gifts never received. Like Golden Age and Fall-from-Grace myths, it deems a Fall imminent.

**The shared bubble.** Both ideas picture "a tiny, human-friendly bubble embedded in the alien and uncooperative universe": physical for Spaceship Earth, conceptual for the Dawkins–Haldane argument that brains evolved for a narrow class of problems, so the world may be "queerer than we *can* suppose". But "any assumption that the world is *in*explicable can lead only to extremely bad explanations": nothing beats "Zeus rules there". And because the outside affects the inside, the inside would not be explicable either. The real question is why one adaptation should have unlimited reach, and "all reach has an explanation."

## Why people are significant

- **Universal constructors.** Every cell is a chemical factory. Humans differ in the kind of knowledge they use (explanatory, not rules of thumb) and in how it is created (conjecture and criticism, not variation and selection of genes). Human bodies are "factories for transforming *anything into anything* that the laws of nature allow. They are 'universal constructors'." A ***constructor*** is "a device capable of causing other objects to undergo transformations without undergoing any net change itself"; a ***universal constructor*** can cause any raw materials to undergo any physically possible transformation, given the right information.
- **One reach.** All entities with universal reach have the same reach. Apes' nut-cracking culture is not explanatory, so the Haldane–Dawkins argument is valid for apes. Two thumbs and a bigger brain are irrelevant, because people build robots and computers. Speed and memory are supplied by computers: Einstein said "My pencil and I are more clever than I." To claim some qualitative thing humans can never understand is to claim the world inexplicable, an appeal to the supernatural. "The only uniquely significant thing about humans… is our ability to create new explanations." Genetic alteration of humans is therefore no more a betrayal than clothes or telescopes.
- **Perspiration can be automated.** "All technological knowledge can eventually be implemented in automated devices", so "the 'perspiration' phase can be automated", and "the more advanced technology becomes, the shorter is the gap between inspiration and automation."
- **What an environment needs.** "An environment is within human reach if it is possible to create an open-ended stream of explanatory knowledge there." "Matter, energy and evidence are the only requirements". Take a cube of intergalactic space the size of the solar system. It holds over a billion tonnes of ionized hydrogen for fusion power and transmutation, and its faint light carries evidence enough to map the nearest galaxies. "Not only is the universe full of evidence, it is full of the same evidence everywhere." "Knowledge-friendliness is the rule, not the exception": person-friendliness "*to people who have the relevant knowledge*. Death is the rule for those who do not." Primed with knowledge, the cube grows more massive and concentrated, produces energy, creates evidence and corrects its errors: "it would resist!"
- **People are cosmically significant.** Predicting a star requires taking a position about the knowledge near it, since people could prevent a supernova, so "astrophysics is incomplete without a theory of people". An astrophysicist's brain contains a model of a quasar jet embodying "the same mathematical relationships and causal structure. That is scientific knowledge." Predicting whether champagne is opened at the Arecibo SETI telescope requires knowing about distant people. "People and knowledge… are by far the most significant phenomena in nature – the only ones whose behaviour cannot be understood without understanding everything of fundamental importance." Like "an explosive awaiting a spark", the universe awaits knowledge: "If we want to, we could be that spark."
- **No final safety.** "The condition of having to solve problems… is permanent." An asteroid strike has about a 1-in-1000 chance per century. Space colonies are a good hedge.
- **Perfectibility** has two senses: attaining a perfect state, as in utopias, or every attainable state being indefinitely improvable. Fallibilism entails the second. "We shall always be at the *beginning* of infinity." Deutsch contrasts the utopian Continental Enlightenment (problems soluble but not inevitable, leading to dogmatism and the Reign of Terror) with the fallibilist British Enlightenment ("enthusiastic for small improvements, unbounded in the future"). These classify ideas, not nations: Condorcet was French but "British", Popper Austrian. "The" Enlightenment is the British one.

## The jump to universality

***The jump to universality***: "the tendency of gradually improving systems to undergo a sudden large increase in functionality, becoming universal in some domain." Typically "a small change in a system to meet a parochial purpose just happened to make the system universal as well."

**Writing.**

- Pictograms plus a rule that a word sounding like two or more other words in sequence is written with their pictograms ("tree" then "sun" for "treason") brought in words no one had explicitly added. A new pictogram is useless until every reader has been taught it; a word written by the rule is understood even by a reader who has never seen it written. That is reach, and "reach always has an explanation": words are built from a few dozen elementary sounds.
- A rule that uses each picture for its initial sound made writing *universal* for the language: the origin of alphabets.
- Universality by rule beats a completed list. Rules are simpler and encode regularities, so they contain more knowledge. They cover every *possible* word, and anyone can coin new words without a central authority.
- The ancients rarely used this power. Alphabetic writing was conceived once, without vowels, which the Greeks added. The innovators cared only about specific, parochial problems.

**Numbers.**

- Tally marks are universal for tallying but impractical. There is a hierarchy of universality.
- Roman numerals have rules for combining symbols, so VII and VIII yield XV and "an abstract truth has been discovered, and proved… without anyone having counted": it was "the *system of numerals* that performed arithmetic", "using *us*". But the highest symbol stood for a thousand, so the system was not universal.
- The positional system came from India, with 0 as a placeholder, and Europe was slow to adopt it. The Greeks multiplied digits written above M (a myriad) by a myriad; allowing that rule to generate multi-tier numerals would have made their system universal, but they never did. Archimedes' own system petered out after 10^800,000,000. Need was not the reason, for he went on to a system with that base and again capped its powers. Apollonius's system fell short for the same reason. It was "as though everyone in the ancient world was avoiding universality on purpose."
- "The largest benefits of any universality… come from its being useful for further innovation. And innovation is unpredictable." Valuing it requires valuing abstract knowledge or expecting unforeseeable benefits, which is unnatural in a static society. The Enlightenment reversed this. It began to regard "all arbitrary exceptions and limitations" as "inherently problematic", including laws treating aristocrat and commoner, master and slave, or man and woman differently. Universality came to be sought deliberately, even as a sign that an idea is true.

**Printing and machines.**

- The typical transition: "before the jump, one has to make specialized objects for each document…; after the jump, one customizes (or specializes, or programs) a universal object". Movable type and the Jacquard loom (1801) both made it.
- Babbage's Difference Engine (built in 1991 by Doron Swade) computed analytic functions by additions and multiplications. His Analytical Engine could choose its next punched card, and that jump made it universal, a universal simulator. It might have led to a Victorian Internet.
- ENIAC jumped to universality and was used for weather forecasting and H-bomb design. With the microprocessor (about 1970) a washing machine could, in principle, do astrophysics. All universal computers have an identical repertoire.

***Lady Lovelace's objection*** (Turing's name for it) is that "the Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything". It fails to appreciate the universality of the laws of physics. Searle compares the brain to earlier technologies such as steam engines and telephone exchanges, but "a steam engine is not a universal simulator. But a computer is". Lovelace and Hofstadter share the premise that low-level steps cannot add up to an "I", and take opposite horns.

**Error-correction and the digital.**

- "There is no such thing as a universal analogue computer", because of error-correction. "Without error-correction all information processing, and hence all knowledge-creation, is necessarily bounded."
- Analogue tallying, such as lengths of string for goats, accumulates errors, and in an analogue system every value could be correct. Taking a median does not help.
- Digital systems round to discrete values: Babbage's cogs click into ten positions. Such a system "takes for granted that errors will occur, but *corrects* them once they do – a case of 'problems are inevitable, but they are soluble' at the lowest level". "Information that cannot be reliably retrieved is not really being stored."
- So "*all* jumps to universality occur in digital systems." Speech is built from finitely many sounds. Even musical volume is a handful of discrete levels (p, mf, f).

**The genetic code.**

- Life began with replicators, such as a catalyst that catalysed its own formation. Groups of them formed rudimentary organisms, like the Roman-numeral stage.
- "Genes are replicators that can be interpreted as instructions in a genetic code. Genomes are groups of genes that are dependent on each other for replication. The process of copying a genome is called a living organism."
- The code stopped evolving while organisms kept evolving, which shows its phenomenal reach. But for over a billion years it built only bacteria, and what universality it has is unknown.

**The most significant universality.** People are "universal explainers, which makes them universal constructors as well". It is "the only kind of universality capable of transcending its parochial origins… Only people can rely on themselves into the unbounded future."

## Artificial intelligence

(Deutsch's "AI" is what is sometimes called AGI.)

- **The Turing test** (1950) asks whether a judge can tell a program from a human through a textual medium. Eliza (Weizenbaum, 1964) used keyword templates ("I hate my job" → "What do you hate most about your job?") and stock questions, and fooled people. Hofstadter's 1983 hoax produced lines like "Ears are auditory organs found on animals." After half a dozen exchanges luck "becomes a very bad explanation": "the probability that the outputs of such templates will continue to resemble the products of human thought diminishes exponentially with the number of utterances."
- **No progress.** Chatbots, including Loebner-prize winners such as Elbot (2008), are no better than Eliza: in 58 years, nil. Opponents of AI are unsurprised for the wrong reason, because they do not appreciate universality; enthusiasts do not appreciate the failure. Turing expected the task to need about 100 MB and thinking by 2000. A laptop now has a thousand times the memory and a million times the speed, "But with what program?" If anyone knew how to write the program, there would be no need to wait for speed.
- ***Qualia***: "the subjective aspect of a sensation." A colour-blind biochemist about to be cured can predict that they will call the colour "blue", but not "*what blue will look like*". Dennett denies qualia exist. But "simply to deny their existence is a bad explanation: anything at all could be denied by that method." "One day, we shall" explain them. "Problems are soluble."
- **What intelligence is not.** Self-awareness by the mirror test, tool use, signalling language and emotional responses are not intelligence. Rule of thumb: "if it can already be programmed, it has nothing to do with intelligence in Turing's sense." Test: "*if you can't program it, you haven't understood it*."
- **The test that matters.**
  - The Turing test is rooted in the empiricist mistake of a purely behavioural criterion. "Judging whether something is a genuine AI will always depend on explanations of how it works."
  - Ask, as Paley would, "who designed the AI's *utterances*: who adapted its utterances to be meaningful – who created the knowledge in them? If it was the designer, then the program is not an AI. If it was the program itself, then it is an AI."
  - "Without a good explanation of how an entity's utterances were created, observing them tells us nothing about that." A good explanation of how a program creates knowledge would show it is an AI without any output.
  - The same joke can be evidence for or against thinking, depending on the best explanation. Judges should seek a hard-to-vary explanation on which the utterances "*cannot* come from any human but only from the AI". It is like checking a patient's consent by rephrasing the question and seeing whether the reply changes. "If the program really is thinking… it will *explain itself*".
- ***Behaviourism***: instrumentalism applied to psychology. "A non-AI program cannot fake AI. The path to AI cannot be through ever better tricks". "Becoming better at pretending to think is not the same as coming closer to being able to think." A chatbot's trick is like muscle-strengthening, which uses knowledge already present; creative thought is like evolution.
- **Artificial evolution.** A student evolves a walking robot by an evolutionary algorithm after first creating a subroutine language for it. Deutsch doubts any knowledge is created: "a much more obvious explanation of their abilities" is "the creativity of the programmer." Improvement stops once the aim is achieved. His proposed experiment: use a real robot, replace the program with *random numbers*, and let the mutations be the processor's natural errors. "If the robot ever walks better than it did originally, then I am mistaken. If it continues to improve after that, then I am very much mistaken."
- **Every AI is a person.** "My guess is that every AI is a person: a general-purpose explainer." Consciousness and creativity probably arrived in one jump. AGI has made no progress "because there is an unsolved philosophical problem at its heart: we do not understand how creativity works. Once that has been solved, programming it will not be difficult."
- **The Singularity** (Vinge, 1993). There is no sign of the theoretical progress it requires, and no reason to single out AI: "we already have billions of humans."
  - By universality there are no superhuman minds as such, only automation: more speed, more working memory, "perspiration" delegated to non-AI automata, brain add-ons. "Universality implies that, in every important sense, humans and AIs will never be other than equal."
  - The Singularity as an incomprehensible upheaval is a parochial misconception, because the capacity to cope grows too: "if everyone were suddenly able to think a million times as fast, no one would feel hurried".
  - Robin Hanson counts several earlier singularities: agricultural, industrial, and the early Enlightenment.
- **Open questions Deutsch names.** Is simulating suffering a crime? Are two identical instances of a quale one experience or two? Is a quantum-randomized computer running all possible AI programs the worst crime ever, or trivial? What distinguishes a simulation, which is a person, from a recording, which is not? Expect the answers to come from the explanatory theory from which AI will follow.
