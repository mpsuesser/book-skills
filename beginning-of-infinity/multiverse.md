# The multiverse

Deutsch's account of quantum theory, "the deepest explanation known to science", as describing a multiverse, and of how bad philosophy kept physicists from saying so. The many-universes view is a minority one, and so is the view that science seeks explanations at all.

## Vocabulary

- ***The world***: the whole of physical reality. ***Multiverse***: "a unified physical entity that contains more than one universe"; the world according to quantum theory.
- ***Universe***: a quasi-autonomous region of the multiverse. "A universe is not a receptacle containing physical objects: it *is* those objects."
- ***History***: a set of fungible universes over time; "a sequence of events happening to objects and possibly their identical counterparts". ***Instances***: the counterparts of an object in different universes.
- ***Fungible***: "identical in literally every way except that there are two of them". The term is legal: dollars in a bank account and barrels of oil are fungible, horses are not. Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles is wrong, since photons in a laser are fungible. Fungibility "allows radically new types of *motion* and *information flow*". Fungible universes can become different under deterministic, symmetric laws (a voltage surge in "half" of them); merely identical ones could not. That gives ***diversity within fungibility***.
- ***Parallel universes***: somewhat misleading. Histories are not perfectly parallel, and the multiverse has more structure: fungibility, entanglement and measures.
- ***Quantum***: the smallest possible change in a discrete physical variable. Quantum theory favours discrete variables, so in any interaction most atoms are unchanged and a few change by one quantum.
- ***Entanglement***: information in each multiversal object determining which of its instances can affect which instances of other objects.
- ***Decoherence***: the process of it becoming infeasible to undo the effect of a *wave of differentiation*, which spreads at up to the speed of light.
- ***Quantum interference***: phenomena caused by non-fungible instances becoming fungible again, when histories rejoin.
- ***Quantum computation***: computation in which the flow of information is not confined to a single history.

## How the multiverse works

- **Splitting.** When an object is affected differently in different instances, a wave of differentiation spreads out from it. Effects usually diminish with distance, except for the creation of knowledge, which "can aim itself at a target, travel vast distances having scarcely any effect, and then utterly transform the destination." There are uncountably many fungible instances, so a surge "in half the universes" makes sense.
- **Measure gives probability.** Proportions of histories have a measure: one outcome a half, two others a quarter each. Outcomes are *subjectively random* though objectively determined. This is the "origin of quantum-mechanical randomness and probability in real physics: it is due to the measure", and it is tested by repetition.
- **Three sources of unpredictability.**
  1. Fundamentally random variables are excluded: there are "no such variables in real physics".
  2. Deterministic processes that are unknown or too complex, especially the creation of knowledge.
  3. "Two or more initially fungible instances of the observer become different".
- **Interference is the evidence.** If histories never rejoined, the multiverse would make the same predictions as a single random universe and would be a bad explanation. But histories do rejoin, and interference provides evidence of other histories *without* communication between them. Write splitting as X ⇒ X/Y (an object with value X comes to have X in some histories and Y in others) and interference as X/Y ⇒ X.
  - *The transporter*, the fictional device that introduces the anomalous voltage Y. Run it twice in quick succession, X ⇒ X/Y ⇒ X, and the outcome is always X. Were the intermediate value randomly X or Y, the outcome would sometimes be Y.
  - *The Mach–Zehnder interferometer.* A semi-silvered mirror sends a photon rightwards (X) in half the universes and downwards (Y) in the other half: X ⇒ X/Y. When both instances strike a second semi-silvered mirror at the same time, it performs X/Y ⇒ X. A photon sent in always emerges rightwards, never downwards, while one introduced on either path after the first mirror emerges randomly. Detectors on the paths find only one photon per history, so both intermediate paths happen: "the path that was not taken affects the one that was."
- **Entanglement blocks interference.** Rejoining occurs only if no wave of differentiation has left the object. Once X and Y have affected the rest of the world differently, the object is entangled with it, and no operation on the object alone can make X and Y interfere: there is "no interference, just splitting". Decoherence is usually rapid, so splitting predominates; interference is ubiquitous microscopically but hard to show in a laboratory.
- **Particles are multiversal.** No instance of a particle stays "the same" over time, and atoms collide like semi-silvered mirrors. The ***uncertainty principle*** (badly misnamed) says any fungible collection of instances must have some diverse attributes. An electron spreads like an ink blot, and a hydrogen atom is that spread balanced by the proton's attraction, not a little solar system. "Uncertainty" has nothing to do with anyone being uncertain, and it is no "principle" but a deduction, as there is no principle of eclipses. "Particles and waves at the same time" is a misconception: an electron is an irreducibly multiversal object.
- **Histories are emergent.** Universes, histories and particles are approximate, emergent phenomena. A history is like a geological stratum, a channel of information flow that is approximately autonomous. Large objects are nearly classical because entanglement suppresses interference in them. "Quantum jumps" are a myth: transitions are continuous changes in the proportion of instances in each state, like money changing owner. A "tiny effect" in quantum terms is a tiny change in *proportions* of instances with discrete attributes. Time itself is an entanglement phenomenon, as Don Page and William Wootters first understood in 1983: "different times are a special case of different universes".
- **Fiction and fact.** A cosmic-ray particle's ink-blot can be wider than the Earth, so lightning strikes, cancers and wars differ across histories. "A great deal of fiction is therefore close to a fact somewhere in the multiverse", but not fiction that needs different laws or constants: "All fiction that does not violate the laws of physics is fact." Somewhere magic appears to have worked, with the history beginning at the transformation, but it never works again. A lottery winner cannot explain the win without the many losers: "In the multiverse, the losers are other instances of oneself."
- **Explaining without causing.** Dawkins's case of a TV psychic is a model. Ten million viewers, half wearing watches, over 100,000 five-minute periods a year: about 25 watches stop each minute, and a quarter of those owners ring in, so the psychic gets calls. Circumstances can *explain* events without *causing* them. The audience's error is parochialism: seeing a phenomenon without the wider unobserved phenomenon essential to its explanation.
- **Quantum computers** protect information-carrying variables from entanglement and exploit ***quantum parallelism***. A register of *n* qubits has 2^*n* instances. Splitting gives intermediate results Y₁…Y_many that depend on the input X and are processed separately in autonomous histories of the same few atoms; interference then combines them into a single history holding the output f(X), and all of them are needed to compute it efficiently. A few hundred qubits would exceed the atoms in the visible universe. Only certain computations benefit.
- **Knowledge across histories.** We are "channels of information flow". Since there are many more ways of being wrong than right, knowledge-creating entities become *more alike* across histories, not less.
- **Inter-universe communication** does not exist in real quantum physics. But Deutsch's thought experiment about it teaches that an idea from another universe is at least as fallible as your own, and that "you can only know that if you have a good explanatory theory". Better to exchange theories themselves: "you can see for yourself that it is a good explanation even if you have no way of knowing how your doppelgänger arrived at it."

**Good fiction is hard to vary.** "Since a wizard could equally well have been said to conjure *any* events, in *any* story, it is a bad explanation". "A good plot always rests… on good explanations of how and why events happen, given its fictional premises". The "phantom zone" premise fails because seeing requires absorbing light. "Good fictional science is hard to invent: it is a variant of real science, and real scientific knowledge is very hard to vary."

## How physics lost the explanation

- **The retreat.** Heisenberg (matrices) and Schrödinger (waves in a higher-dimensional space) created quantum theory in 1925–27. A rule of thumb was adopted: "whenever a measurement is made, all the histories but one cease to exist", the survivor chosen at random by measure. Then came the retreat into instrumentalism, the ***shut-up-and-calculate interpretation***. It ignored three things:
  1. The rule of thumb is grossly inconsistent with both theories, and usable only where quantum effects are too small to matter, which includes measurement, because of decoherence.
  2. It is not self-consistent for an observer measuring another observer.
  3. The theories clearly described *some* process that brings about the outcomes.

  Students were trained not to wonder, which counteracted the tradition of criticism.
- ***The Copenhagen interpretation***: "Bohr's combination of instrumentalism, anthropocentrism and studied ambiguity", used to avoid understanding quantum theory as being about reality. Its parts are the paradox of Wigner's friend; the *principle of complementarity*, which permits only classical language and forbids certain questions; *particle–wave duality* (ironically true in the multiverse sense, though not meant so); Heisenberg's "potentialities"; and consciousness causing the transition. "If you think you've understood quantum mechanics then you don't." Inconsistency was defended as "complementarity", and parochialism hailed as sophistication. Claiming to stand outside the jurisdiction of criticism is the hallmark of bad philosophy, and it opened the door to quackery and mysticism.
- **Dissenters.** Einstein never accepted it. Bohm's theory is the multiverse in heavy disguise. Schrödinger, in Dublin in 1952, warned that what he would say might "seem lunatic": the histories were "not alternatives but all really happen simultaneously". That is the earliest known reference to the multiverse. Everett's version, five years later, is endorsed by a small minority.
- ***Interpretation***: "the explanatory part of a scientific theory, supposedly distinct from its predictive or instrumental part". The split makes no sense; the full criticism, with the dinosaurs-and-fossils case, is in `explanation-and-science.md`.

**When someone treats quantum theory as only a calculating device**, ask what process produces the outcomes it predicts. Point to the Mach–Zehnder experiment or a quantum computation. Ask whether they claim to be made of something other than atoms.
