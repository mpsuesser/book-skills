# Explanation and science

How Deutsch judges theories, studies, measurements and the philosophies of knowledge behind them. The core tests (hard to vary, reach, testability second, who created the knowledge) are in `SKILL.md`; this file holds the misconceptions they answer, the worked examples, and the bad philosophy and explanationless science that follow when explanation is dropped.

## The misconceptions about where knowledge comes from

- ***Empiricism***: the misconception that we "derive" all our knowledge from sensory experience (Locke's "white paper", the "Book of Nature"), with the discoverer passive. In reality theories are conjectured and experience only chooses between them. Empiricism set up two false authorities: sensory experience, and a fictitious process of "derivation", such as extrapolation, generalization or induction. It did early good by defending against dogma and giving experiment a central role, albeit the wrong one.
- ***Theory-laden***: "There is no such thing as 'raw' experience." All observation is interpretation: "we perceive nothing as what it really is. It is all theoretical interpretation: conjecture." Sherlock Holmes's "it is a capital mistake to theorize before one has data" is itself a capital mistake. The word "data" ("givens") misleads for the same reason.
- ***Inductivism***: the misconception that theories are obtained by generalizing or extrapolating repeated experiences, under a *principle of induction* that "the future will resemble the past". It makes two mistakes:
  - Most knowledge is not predictions about experience at all. It is explanations of what is there.
  - The future is often unlike the past: powered flight, fission bombs, a sunrise every ninety minutes from orbit. Saying the "same laws" keep holding is empty, because only an explanation says which laws, and what counts as "the same".
  - Example: the calendar's leading "19" repeated for a century and then became "20" (the Y2K bug).
  - Even recognizing a repetition is theory: "the very idea that an experience has been repeated is not itself a sensory experience, but a theory."
  - *Induction* is a "non-existent process."
- ***Justificationism***: the misconception that knowledge can be genuine or reliable only if justified by some source or criterion: knowledge as *justified, true belief*. It turns "how do we know…?" into "by what authority do we claim…?", "a chimera". It converts the quest for truth into a quest for certainty (a feeling) or for endorsement (a social status). Justified belief is "*unattainable* to humans except in the form of self-deception; it is *unnecessary* for any good purpose; and it is *undesired* by the wisest". Beliefs can be justified only relative to other beliefs, fallibly, so the quest ends in infinite regress.
- ***Instrumentalism***: the misconception that science only predicts the outcomes of observations and says nothing about reality. In statistics "explanation" has come to mean prediction. ***Realism***, the commonsense and true doctrine, holds that the physical world really exists and knowledge of it can exist too. ***Relativism***: the misconception that statements cannot be objectively true or false, only relative to a cultural standard.

Taking no one's word for it (the Royal Society's *Nullius in verba*) was necessary but not enough. Usually "the whole point of a tradition was to keep things the same"; what the Enlightenment added was a tradition of criticism.

## Rules of thumb and the conjuring trick

*Background knowledge* is "familiar and currently uncontroversial knowledge"; a *rule of thumb* is a "purely predictive theory" whose explanatory content is all background knowledge. Rules that seem explanationless only seem so: there is always an explanation of why they work, and they fail parochially. A rule predicting where the ball ends up in a cups-and-balls trick fails when the conjurer uses candles. Only explanations let us abstract "cups" and "balls" rather than "red" and "blue". "Appearances are not self-explanatory." An explanation is "a statement of the reality that accounts for the appearance."

The prophet who says the sun will go out next Tuesday, and the gambler on a "lucky night", have testable theories. When they fail they reinterpret the omens and pick a new date. Easily variable explanations "insulate" their holders from evidence as thoroughly as untestable ones. Feynman: "Science is what we have learned about how to keep from fooling ourselves."

**The Aristarchus exercise.** Hand someone the axis-tilt explanation of seasons and ask them to modify it to dodge an awkward prediction, such as six-month days at the poles, no seasons in the tropics, or the southern hemisphere out of phase. "You will fail. That is what a good explanation will do for you." Patching it ("everywhere else on Earth, they *also* happen at those times of year") makes it no longer an explanation but a rule of thumb.

**The gold test.** Gold on Earth comes from supernovae, or from an intelligent being with an explanation. Finding the explanation is how we know which.

## Testing, error and measurement

- **The essence of an experimental test**: at least two viable theories making conflicting predictions that an experiment can distinguish. We test only the few theories that are already good explanations.
- **Popper's *criterion of demarcation*** is testability: a theory is *testable* if it makes predictions which, if false, could be contradicted by some possible observation. Galileo called experiments *cimenti*, "trials by ordeal". Testability is not sufficient: myths can be testable and still bad.
- **An error is a mistake about a cause.** "An error in experimental science *is* a mistake about the cause of something. Like an accurate observation, it is a matter of theory." An apparent galaxy on a photographic plate turned out to be a flaw in the emulsion.
- **Observation is attribution.** "To say that we have genuinely observed any given thing is to say that we have accurately attributed our evidence… to that thing." Scientific truth is this correspondence between theories and reality. Instruments take us closer to reality while putting us physically further from it, through longer chains of interpretation, each link more theory. They are "like conjuring tricks in reverse": they "fool our senses into seeing what is really there." Jocelyn Bell's pulsar was an ink line on a chart.
- ***Proxy***: "a physical variable which can be measured as a way of measuring another variable. (All scientific measurements involve chains of proxies.)" Each link adds error, and we avoid fooling ourselves only by criticizing the theory of each link. Hence the measurement rule: "one can claim to have measured a quantity only when one has an explanatory theory of how and why the measurement procedure should reveal its value, and with what accuracy." Reporting a result without an accuracy estimate "makes your report strictly meaningless."
- No amount of observation corrects a misconception until someone thinks of a better idea. With the right idea one can explain the data even when the data contain large errors.
- The Edison quotation ("one per cent inspiration and ninety-nine per cent perspiration") misleads. "A repetitive experiment is not repetitive if one is thinking about the ideas that it is testing." "A trial that fails is still fun."
- "Feeling insignificant because the universe is large has exactly the same logic as feeling inadequate for not being a cow." The universe "is our home, and our resource. The bigger the better."

## Emergence, abstractions and levels of explanation

- ***Emergence***: high-level phenomena that are explicable in their own terms, quasi-autonomously from their constituents. A kettle's molecules are intractable, but its boiling time is predictable from its mass and power. *Levels of emergence* are "sets of phenomena that can be explained well in terms of each other without analysing them into their constituent entities." Emergent phenomena are a tiny minority of all phenomena, yet they are what we care about, and "all knowledge-creation depends on, and physically consists of, emergent phenomena." Emergence is why discoveries can be made in steps, each theory explaining one layer.
- ***Reductionism***: the misconception that science must always explain by analysing into components, so higher-level explanations cannot be fundamental. ***Holism***: the misconception that all significant explanations are of components in terms of wholes. Both are irrational for the same reason as instrumentalism and the Principle of Mediocrity: "they advocate accepting or rejecting theories on grounds other than whether they are good explanations."
- **The Churchill copper atom.** Why is a particular copper atom in the nose of a statue of Churchill in London? Only high-level explanations answer: ideas, leadership, war, tradition. A low-level prediction of that atom's path would explain nothing.
- **Hofstadter's dominoes.** A chain of spring-loaded dominoes computes whether 641 is prime; the output domino never falls. Why not?
  - "Because its predecessor never falls" is true but passes the buck: it answers a predictive question at the wrong level of emergence.
  - "Because 641 is prime" is "the best explanation, perhaps even the only explanation".
  - An abstraction is causing a physical effect, and reductionism about abstractions is refuted.
- **Causation is abstract.** "The very idea of a *cause* is emergent and abstract." Hume saw we cannot perceive causation, and at the micro level the laws make cause and effect interchangeable. "There is no inconsistency in having multiple explanations of the same phenomenon, at different levels of emergence. Regarding micro-physical explanations as more fundamental than emergent ones is arbitrary and fallacious."
- **Abstractions are real.** "If an entity is referred to by our best explanation in the relevant field, we must regard it as really existing." When a computer chess program beats you, "it is really the *program* that has beaten you": the knowledge runs in a chain of substrates, and its content is the whole story. We know abstractions as we know everything else, by conjecture, criticism and seeking good explanations. Plato's mistake was to make them supernatural.
- **Theories live on.** Kepler's, Newton's and Einstein's theories each deny their predecessor's explanatory entities: general relativity says there is no force of gravity. But "sweeping away the entities through which a theory makes its explanation is not the same as sweeping away the whole of the explanation." Where Newton predicted truly, it was because he expressed a truth that lives on in its successor. Einstein: "There could be no fairer destiny for any physical theory than that it should point the way to a more comprehensive theory in which it lives on as a limiting case." Newton is fine for bridges, slightly off for satellite navigation, and hopelessly wrong for pulsars.
- "It is the explanations in existing theories, not the predictions, that have to be creatively varied in order to conjecture the next theory." Scientists can pass through intermediate ideas that are not viable at all. "This is the fundamental reason that explanatory ideas are able to escape from parochialism, while biological evolution, and rules of thumb, cannot." In Popper's words: "We can let our theories die in our place."
- Experiment is only one mode of criticism. The Enlightenment brought criticism to non-scientific fields, where "good explanations about philosophical issues are as hard to find as in science – and criticism is correspondingly effective." In philosophy, experience supplies problems.

## Bad philosophy

***Bad philosophy*** is "philosophy that is not merely false, but actively prevents the growth of other knowledge." Its hallmark is a claim to stand outside the jurisdiction of criticism. "Error is the normal state of our knowledge, and is no disgrace. There is nothing bad about *false* philosophy." After the Enlightenment good philosophy grew, but bad philosophy became *worse*. Deutsch traces the descent:

- **Empiricism** was merely false and largely harmless while nobody took it literally: scientists conjectured, tested, and only afterwards claimed induction.
- ***Positivism*** tried to eliminate everything not "derived from observation". Since nothing is, what got eliminated depended on whim. Ernst Mach spurred Einstein to drop the assumption of universal time, but also opposed relativity and denied that atoms exist. He vilified Boltzmann, whose despair may have contributed to his suicide. "The role of philosophy should have been to laugh at it *then*."
- ***Logical positivism*** held that statements not verifiable by observation are meaningless, which makes logical positivism itself meaningless. Wittgenstein accepted that and declared all philosophy meaningless.
- Then came linguistic philosophy, attacks on truth and reason themselves, and **postmodernism**. Postmodernism says that because ideas cannot be justified they are arbitrary "narratives", and truth is endorsement by a group. Its charges are true of itself: it is a narrative that resists criticism, with authority-based criteria. A good explanation is hard to create "because there is an objective reality that does not meet *anyone's* prior expectations."
- **The Copenhagen interpretation and shut-up-and-calculate** in quantum theory: see `multiverse.md`.

**The "interpretation" split.** The most damaging legacy is the idea that a scientific theory divides into predictive rules of thumb and an optional *interpretation*. Without an explanation one cannot even recognize when a rule applies, and in fundamental physics the predicted outcome is itself an unobserved physical process. In palaeontology dinosaurs are "*the explanation* of fossils", not an interpretation. Rival "interpretations" make the same predictions: dinosaurs as palaeontologists' sensations, fossils coming into existence on extraction, dinosaurs as relationships between experiences. They are ruled out not empirically but as "general-purpose means of denying anything." "Since explanationless prediction is actually impossible, the methodology of excluding explanation from a science is just a way of holding one's explanations immune from criticism."

**How bad philosophy is defeated.** It holds itself immune to argument, so it "can be countered by *progress*." Mach would have accepted atoms after seeing them under a microscope. He could still have said "I'm only seeing a video monitor," but would have tired of that general-purpose bad explanation once a better game was available. "Bad philosophy is philosophy that denies the possibility, desirability or existence of progress."

## Explanationless science: how to spot it

The method: check whether a study has an explanatory theory linking its proxy to the quantity it names, and of the errors in between. Deutsch's two worked cases follow.

**The happiness-heritability study** (behaviourism, which is instrumentalism applied to psychology, in action).

1. People rate their happiness on a scale. Ratings are not commensurable: a pessimist at 8 may be miserable, and a chanting-craze devotee at 3 happier than most.
2. A gene correlates with higher ratings. It might code for less reluctance to *quantify* happiness, or for good looks that get better treatment. "There is an infinity of possible explanations. But the study is not seeking explanations." Watching "happy behaviour" instead adds another layer, since some people act happy as a remedy for unhappiness.
3. "Heritability" is defined as correlation with genetic relatedness, a nonexplanatory definition. By it, slavery was once highly heritable in America.
4. The headline reads "Happiness 50% Genetically Determined". Then "heritable" becomes "genetically determined", and a correct but uninteresting result is turned against an explanatory theory. That theory is "Happiness is a state of continually solving one's problems," with unhappiness caused by being "chronically baulked", which is a special case of the principle of optimism. Yet how genes affect happiness may itself depend on knowledge, such as cultural standards of looks, or a persuasive book.
5. The study may have followed every formal rule (randomizing, controls, statistics), but "explanationless theories can do no more than entrench existing, bad explanations." A theory that predicts how happy people will be cannot take account of knowledge creation, so to that extent it is prophecy and "biased towards pessimism". Refusing to theorize about the mind as a cause is the equivalent of regarding it as "a non-creative automaton": behaviourist studies lead to dehumanizing theories.

There are legitimate proxy studies. A poll asking whether voters are "happy" with a politician is tested at the election. A drug trial tests whether patients *say* they are happier without side effects. Whether they are really happier is inaccessible to science until there is a testable explanatory theory of happiness. The same goes for animal suffering: two commissioned studies of hunted stags, measuring "stress" chemistry, had no "bearing on the issue in question", because there is no explanatory theory of qualia. That is "bad philosophy disguised as science", and it suppresses the moral debate.

**The City Museum count.** Assistants tally people entering each door. To claim the total equals "visitors", you need theories: which doors are entrances, whether the café counts, whether staff count, whether people re-enter. Counting exits as well gives an accuracy estimate, but only given a theory of the unseen interior: empty at night, no other exits, visitors never created or destroyed. Explanationless science ("really… science with unstated, uncriticized explanations") instead names the daily difference the "spontaneous-human-creation count" and reports anomalies "inconsistent with conventional physics". The more incompetent the counting, the more anomalies. It concedes teleportation cannot be ruled out, and answers the headlines with "not conclusive, merely suggestive… more studies are needed." No claim in it is false, since definitions cannot be false, but "explanation *of errors* constitutes most of the content of the design of any nontrivial scientific experiment."

**The instability.** "The bigger the errors you make… the more exciting the results are, *if true*." Without error-correction, which depends on explanatory theories, "false results drown out the true." This is how eminent physicists were fooled by conjurers into believing in "paranormal" phenomena: they judged only whether a prediction came true.
