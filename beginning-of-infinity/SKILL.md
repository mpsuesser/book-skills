# The Beginning of Infinity

Deutsch's thesis: rapid, stable progress has happened only once in history, and all of it, in science, technology, morality, politics and art, comes from "a single human activity: the quest for what I call good explanations." That progress is unbounded: the *beginning of infinity*. "There is indeed an objective difference between a false explanation and a true one," between wrong and right, ugly and beautiful, suffering and its alleviation. Use this skill to handle a situation the way Deutsch would: find the explanation at stake, criticize it, and look for what would let errors be corrected.

## The vocabulary

Use these as leading words, in Deutsch's sense.

- ***Explanation***: a "statement about what is there, what it does, and how and why." All predictions are deduced from explanations of how things are.
- ***Good explanation / bad explanation***: one that is hard / easy to vary while still accounting for what it purports to account for. "If an explanation could easily explain anything in the given field, then it actually explains nothing."
- ***Reach***: the ability of some explanations "to solve problems beyond those that they were created to solve."
- ***Problem***: exists "when a conflict between ideas is experienced." There are no contradictions in reality, so every problem signals flawed or inadequate knowledge. Problems are not bad: a problem is something to be curious about and solved. An unproblematic state "is a state without creative thought. Its other name is death."
- ***Knowledge***: information which, once physically embodied in a suitable environment, tends to cause itself to remain so. It arises only through error-correcting processes: evolution (in genes) or conjecture and criticism (in minds).
- ***Conjecture and criticism***: "the real source of our knowledge is conjecture alternating with criticism." Theories are "guesses – bold conjectures," made by rearranging, combining, altering and adding to existing ideas. Experience does not supply them; its "main use is to choose between theories that have already been guessed."
- ***Fallibilism***: "there are no authoritative sources of knowledge, nor any reliable means of justifying ideas as being true or probable." Fallibilists expect even their best explanations to contain misconceptions. "It is fallibilism, not mere rejection of authority, that is essential for the initiation of unlimited knowledge growth."
- ***Tradition of criticism***: the Enlightenment's real innovation. Rejecting authority alone just installs new authorities; what lets knowledge grow is a lasting custom of criticizing existing ideas and seeking better ones. A short-lived one is a *mini-enlightenment*.
- ***Rational***: "attempting to solve problems by seeking good explanations; actively pursuing error-correction by creating criticisms of both existing ideas and new proposals."
- ***Error-correction***: "Error-correction is the beginning of infinity." Every unbounded process, from digital computation to science to politics, works by taking for granted that errors will occur and correcting them.
- ***Parochialism***: "mistaking appearance for reality, or local regularities for universal laws."
- ***People***: "entities that can create explanatory knowledge": *universal explainers*, and therefore *universal constructors*. Human, artificial or extraterrestrial, there is only one kind of person.
- ***Everything not forbidden by laws of nature is achievable, given the right knowledge.*** Every putative transformation is either impossible because a law of nature forbids it, or achievable with the right knowledge. There is no third option: one would be a regularity with no explanation, which is belief in the supernatural.
- ***Problems are inevitable. Problems are soluble.*** These are the two truths Deutsch would carve in stone. "It is inevitable that we face problems, but no particular problem is inevitable." Soluble means "the right knowledge would solve them," not that we already have it.
- ***The principle of optimism***: "All evils are caused by insufficient knowledge." It is "a way of explaining failure, not prophesying success."
- ***Prediction vs prophecy***: a prediction is a conclusion about future events "that follow[s] from good explanations"; a prophecy is "anything that purports to know what is not yet knowable." No good explanation can predict the outcome, or the probability, of anything whose course will be significantly affected by knowledge not yet created.
- ***Popper's criterion***: "Good political institutions are those that make it as easy as possible to detect whether a ruler or policy is a mistake, and to remove rulers or policies without violence when they are." It replaces the question "who should rule?".
- ***Criterion for reality***: "a particular thing is real if and only if it figures in our best explanation of something." If our best explanation denies that something exists, stop assuming it does.

## The method: conjecture and criticism

This is the sequence for any problem: a scientific puzzle, a decision, a disagreement, a failing project. It is the procedure Deutsch gives in his dialogue between Socrates and Hermes.

1. **State the problem as a conflict between ideas.** Name the ideas that conflict, or the explanation that fails to meet a criterion. Curiosity is the experience of a best explanation failing to meet a criterion. Done when you can say which ideas clash.
2. **Guess.** Create candidate explanations by varying, combining and altering existing ideas. The source is irrelevant (a dream, a rival, an authority, a god): "What matters in all cases is the explanation you create, within your own mind." Treat even what someone tells you as a guess about what they meant.
3. **Criticize every candidate, including your favourite.** Run the tests below. Accept nothing because an authority endorsed it or because you want it true. Experiment is only one mode of criticism; logic, consistency with other good explanations, and the hard-to-vary test do most of the work.
4. **Remedy what fails.** Alter or drop the idea in favour of others, which are themselves guesses and get the same criticism.
5. **Provisionally accept** "only when we fail in these attempts either to reject or to improve an idea." Hold it as the current best misconception. It will throw up new problems, and better problems are the mark of progress. Done when you hold one explanation you cannot vary without losing what it explains, you can say what would refute it, and you have named the new problem it creates.

Being willing to drop a refuted theory is not enough: "one must also be seeking a better explanation." That is the scientific frame of mind.

## Tests for an explanation

- **Hard-to-vary test.** Try changing its details. If variants account for the evidence equally well, it is a bad explanation, and preferring any one variant is irrational. The Greek myth of Persephone explains winter by Demeter's grief, and its details can be swapped at will, including to fit any new observation. The axis-tilt explanation of the seasons cannot be varied: every detail plays a functional role. If it were refuted, "its defenders would have had nowhere to go." Good explanations make it "harder for you to fool yourself" and are "not yours to modify."
- **Reach test.** Ask what else it explains that it was not designed for. Axis tilt was invented for the sun's changing elevation and turned out to explain the seasons, why the hemispheres' seasons are out of phase, the tropics' lack of seasons and the midnight sun. Reach is not induction: "it is explanatory theories that tell us which (usually few) aspects of one situation can be 'extrapolated' to others." Gravity on Mars follows our theory of gravity; the map of Mars does not follow the map of Earth.
- **Testability comes second.** "It is only when a theory is a good explanation – hard to vary – that it even matters whether it is testable." Most false theories are rejected "without any experiment, simply for being bad explanations." The ancient Greeks rightly never tested whether yodelling staves off winter. A refuted good explanation is no longer good, because "the problem has expanded to include those observations."
- **Explanation vs prediction.** A rule that predicts what a conjuring trick will look like does not explain the trick. "There is no such thing as a purely predictive, explanationless theory." A *rule of thumb* (a purely predictive theory whose explanatory content is all background knowledge) breaks the moment some unfamiliar feature appears. "Denying that some regularity in nature has an explanation is effectively the same as believing in the supernatural."
- **Who created the knowledge?** Anything with the *appearance of design* (hard to vary while serving a purpose) embodies knowledge, so ask how that knowledge came to exist. "Already present at the outset" is creationism; "just happen" is spontaneous generation. The only known answers are variation with selection, and conjecture with criticism. This is also the test for whether a program is an AI.
- **Infinite-regress check.** An explanation is bad "not because it fails to explain *everything* (no theory does), but because what it leaves unexplained is effectively the same as what it purports to explain": a designer who needs a designer, a justification that needs a justification.
- **Occam's razor is not the test.** "Demeter did it" is simple and easy to vary. The best explanations are "the ones that are most constrained by existing knowledge."

## Named failure modes

Each of these is an idea Deutsch names, and each has the same root: it accepts or rejects ideas on some ground other than whether they are good explanations.

- **Empiricism** (knowledge derived from the senses), **inductivism** (the future will resemble the past), **justificationism** (knowledge is "justified, true belief", so "how do we *know*…?" becomes "by what authority do we claim…?") and **instrumentalism** (science only predicts). Applied to psychology instrumentalism becomes **behaviourism**, applied to mathematics **finitism**. Depth: [`explanation-and-science.md`](explanation-and-science.md).
- **Bad philosophy**: "philosophy that actively prevents the growth of knowledge." Its hallmark is claiming to stand "outside the jurisdiction of normal… criticism." **Explanationless science** follows every formal rule of method yet cannot make progress, because no one is seeking an explanation. Depth: `explanation-and-science.md`.
- **Reductionism** and **holism**: insisting explanations run only from parts to wholes, or only from wholes to parts. Explanations at any *level of emergence* can be fundamental. Depth: `explanation-and-science.md`.
- **Parochialism**, the **Principle of Mediocrity** ("there is nothing significant about humans") and **Spaceship Earth** ("the biosphere is a life-support system for humans"). Depth: [`people-and-universality.md`](people-and-universality.md).
- **Prophecy**, **blind optimism** (proceeding as if one knew bad outcomes will not happen), **blind pessimism**, also called the **precautionary principle** (avoiding everything not known to be safe), and the conceit that we are **"nearly there"**. Depth: [`optimism-and-the-future.md`](optimism-and-the-future.md).
- **"Who should rule?"**, **decision by weighing**, and **compromise policies**. Depth: [`politics-choice-persuasion.md`](politics-choice-persuasion.md).
- **Creationism**, **spontaneous generation** and **Lamarckism**. Lamarckism is "the counterpart of inductivism": both assume the environment can instruct a mind or genome. Also **argument by analogy**: "arguments by analogy are fallacies." Depth: [`evolution-and-memes.md`](evolution-and-memes.md).
- **Zeno's mistake**, confusing an abstract attribute with a physical one of the same name. Also the **argument from personal incredulity**, and **anthropic reasoning** offered as a whole explanation. Depth: [`infinity-and-anthropic.md`](infinity-and-anthropic.md).
- **"Because I say so"** packs five errors into four words:
  - It could "explain" anything.
  - It addresses who said something, not what was said.
  - It turns a request for explanation into a request for justification.
  - It confuses the nonexistent authority of ideas with human power.
  - It claims to stand outside criticism.

## Applying the book to a situation

1. **Find the explanation in play** and run the tests above on it. Usually it is a rule of thumb, a prophecy or an appeal to authority dressed as one. Done when you can say whether it is hard to vary and what its reach is.
2. **Separate prediction from prophecy.** Ask whether the outcome depends on knowledge not yet created. If it does, no probability exists. Turn the question into how well the people or institutions involved can detect and correct errors, and what knowledge they lack.
3. **Explain any failure by missing knowledge.** Following the principle of optimism, either the laws of physics forbid the goal, or some knowledge would achieve it. Name which, and what knowledge is missing.
4. **Check the means of error-correction.** Does the arrangement (a team, a policy, a theory held "immune from criticism", an education) let mistakes be detected and undone without violence? Deutsch asks whether the imperative "not to destroy the means of correcting mistakes" is the only moral imperative. An error that "prevents itself from being undone" is the deadliest kind.
5. **Create options rather than weigh them.** "To choose an option is to choose its explanation." Good explanations are discrete: the halfway point between two of them is usually worse than either.
6. **End with the better problem** the provisional answer opens. In Deutsch's terms, knowledge grows "from *problems* to *better problems*."
