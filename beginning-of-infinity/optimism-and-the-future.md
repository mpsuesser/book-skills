# Optimism and the future

Deutsch's rational philosophy of the unknowable: how to reason about risks, forecasts, doom, sustainability and the limits of knowledge. *Prediction vs prophecy*, the *principle of optimism*, and *problems are inevitable / soluble* are defined in `SKILL.md`. *Popper's criterion* and "who should rule?" are covered in `politics-choice-persuasion.md`.

## Random vs unknowable

Martin Rees (*Our Final Century*) gave civilization a 50 per cent chance of surviving the twenty-first century, and likened its position to Russian roulette, where you need lose only once. The crucial difference is this.

- **Random**: in Russian roulette the possible outcomes and their probabilities are known.
- **Unknowable**: the future of civilization is unknowable "because the knowledge that is going to affect it has yet to be created. Hence the possible outcomes are not yet known, let alone their probabilities."

"No explanation has enough reach to predict the content of its own successors." "People in 1900 did not consider the internet or nuclear power *unlikely*: they did not conceive of them at all." Given an experimental oddity, there is no way to predict whether it will be cleared up by a minor correction or will revolutionize whole sciences. Michelson and Morley did not realize what their 1887 experiment had observed.

"Trying to know the unknowable leads inexorably to error and self-deception. Among other things, it creates a bias towards pessimism." Malthus's 1798 *On Population* set population growing in a geometrical ratio against food growing in an arithmetical one. But food grew faster. The coming increase in population was more predictable than the even larger increase in food "not because it was in any sense more probable, but simply because it depended less on the creation of knowledge." "We do not yet know what we have not yet discovered."

**The diagnostic.** Whenever a forecast sets a *prediction* of one process (population, consumption, emissions, depletion) against a *prophecy* of another (what knowledge people will create), it is biased towards doom. Ask what in the forecast depends on knowledge yet to be created. Then ask Ehrlich's own question back: "what would we do *then*?" The honest answer is: solve the next problem.

## What optimism is and isn't

- It is not a mood or "glass half full". Churchill was depressed but optimistic, and Malthus was jolly.
- It is not Leibniz's "best of all possible worlds", and its opposite is not Schopenhauer's "worst". Both are spectacularly bad explanations, under which true explanations could never be imagined, and both are close to pure pessimism.
- ***Blind optimism*** (recklessness, overconfidence) is "proceeding as if one knew that bad outcomes will not happen." The *Titanic* was "practically unsinkable".
- ***Blind pessimism*** is the ***precautionary principle***: "avoiding everything not known to be safe." Its argument is an asymmetry: the downside of the unknown may be catastrophic. But "blind pessimism is a blindly optimistic doctrine". It assumes disaster cannot follow from existing knowledge, or rather from existing ignorance. Protecting against *any* disaster requires knowledge, and knowledge must be created. "The harm that can flow from any innovation that does not destroy the growth of knowledge is always finite; the good can be unlimited." Applied to itself, the precautionary principle rules itself out, since the transition to it was never known to be safe. Most past civilizations that were destroyed "had enthusiastically implemented the precautionary principle." Their caution was "as perverse as expecting a blindfold to be useful when navigating dangerous waters."
- Both blind optimism and blind pessimism "are prophetic": prophetic pessimism about one aspect of the future is always prophetic optimism about another.
- Pessimists keep announcing an exceptionally dangerous moment, which is always the present. Hawking urged hiding from extraterrestrials, on the analogy of Columbus. That overlooks the dangers of hiding, and repeats Spaceship Earth on a larger scale, as if they would want our resources. With transmutation they need not want our gold or water. "There can be only one type of person: universal explainers and constructors." So the only moral values that permit sustained progress are the objective ones the Enlightenment has begun to discover.

## The principle of optimism in use

"*All evils are caused by insufficient knowledge.*" It is first "a way of explaining failure, not prophesying success": there is no fundamental barrier, and failures happen "because we did not know enough, in time." It is also a stance towards the future, and it follows from the world being explicable. The prohibitions of physics are not themselves evils: being unable to prophesy, and the insoluble mathematical problems, are not.

- **The two-way test.** For any evil, "*either* the laws of physics forbid eliminating it in a given time with the available resources *or* there is a way of eliminating it in the time and with those resources."
- **Disease, ageing and death** are problems of the same type: finite complexity facing exponentially growing knowledge. The objection that this would cause overpopulation is the Malthusian fallacy. What each additional surviving person would need "is easily calculated"; what they would contribute "is unknowable". The worry that the old would entrench themselves in power is solved by traditions of criticism.
- **"Natural" disasters are disasters of ignorance.** People died of exposure standing on the raw materials for fire, and died of cholera within sight of hearths that could have boiled their water. "The distinction between a 'natural' disaster and one brought about by ignorance is parochial." The species nearly went extinct about 70,000 years ago. The twenty-first century is the first able to defend itself against a one-kilometre asteroid, which strikes about every 250,000 years. That is "unprecedented *safety*".
- **The talking horse.** A prisoner condemned to death promises the king to teach his horse to talk within a year: "A lot can happen in a year. The horse might die. The king might die. I might die. Or the horse might talk!" "Progress cannot take place at all unless someone is open to, and prepares for, those inconceivable possibilities". One cannot let the assumption that something will never exist condition one's planning.
- **Hard is not unlikely.** Kennedy in 1962: "We choose to go to the moon… not because they are easy, but because they are hard." The rocket would be built of "new metal alloys, some of which have not yet been invented." Hardness counts against a *means*, but when choosing the *objective* it can count in favour, "because we want to engage with projects that will involve creating new knowledge." "Probabilities and prophecies were not needed in that argument."
- **Popper's duty.** "It is our duty to remain optimists… not to prophesy evil but, rather, to fight for a better world."

## Sustainability

***Sustain*** has "two almost opposite, but often confused, meanings: to provide someone with what they need, and to prevent things from changing."

- **Unsustainable is good.** If your way of life merely makes you build a new giant statue, that is *sustainable*: you can go on exactly as before. Inventing better farming and curing a disease that kills children is *unsustainable*: the population grows, fewer people are needed in the fields, and "you have to live the solution, and to set about solving the new problems that this creates." Britain, with a worse climate than Easter Island, supports at least three times Easter Island's peak population density without its old forests.
- **Two conceptions of people.** In the pessimistic conception, people are wasters who convert precious resources into useless coloured pictures; that is true of static societies, whose statues really were waste. In the optimistic conception, vindicated by events, people are problem-solvers: "creators of the unsustainable solution and hence also of the next problem." "In the pessimistic conception, that distinctive ability of people is a disease for which sustainability is the cure. In the optimistic one, sustainability is the disease and people are the cure."
- **"All triumphs are temporary."** Antibiotic resistance is an indictment only "from the point of view of someone who expects a sustainable lifestyle. But in reality there is no such thing. Only progress is sustainable." Calling it "so-called progress" is bad philosophy. The prophetic remedy is to postpone disaster: shrink and disperse the population, restrict travel. That did not prevent the Black Death and will not cure cancer.
- **The aspiration is the danger.** To aim at a sustainable lifestyle, which in practice means only "the *semblance* of sustainability", is to force "the future world into our image, endlessly reproducing our lifestyle, our misconceptions and our mistakes". The alternative is "an open-ended journey of creation and exploration whose every step is unsustainable until it is redeemed by the next".

## Preparing for the unforeseen

"There is no resource-management strategy that can prevent disasters, just as there is no political system that provides only good leaders and good policies, nor a scientific method that provides only true theories. But there are ideas that reliably *cause* disasters, and one of them is, notoriously, the idea that the future can be scientifically planned. The only rational policy, in all three cases, is to judge institutions, plans and ways of life according to how good they are at correcting mistakes: removing bad policies and leaders, superseding bad explanations, and recovering from disasters."

- To expect problems *always* to be solved in time would be the same prophetic fallacy. The deeper Malthusian mistake is to claim a way of *averting* disaster through sustainability, which denies that problems are inevitable. No one can know in advance which solutions will stay problem-free, so short of stasis there is no avoiding unforeseen problems, and stasis is itself unsustainable.
- "Prevention and delaying tactics are useful, but they can be no more than a minor part of a viable strategy." Cures can be developed only for diseases already known, so we need "the capacity to deal with unforeseen, unforeseeable failures": "a large and vibrant research community, interested in explanation and problem-solving", "the wealth to fund it, and the technological capacity to implement what it discovers." ***Wealth*** is "the repertoire of physical transformations that one is capable of causing."
- "An ounce of prevention equals a pound of cure", but only when one knows what to prevent. "No precautions can avoid problems that we do not yet foresee." Relying on luck "would simply guarantee that we would eventually fail without the means of recovering."

**Climate change, worked.** Deutsch makes three observations, then a conclusion.

1. **We were lucky.** Emissions must eventually raise the temperature, and that must eventually do harm. Had the disaster struck in 1902, there would have been no computers, few physicists ("a luxury" then, as colour televisions were in the 1970s) and little wealth. Only places like the Netherlands could have built sea walls. Today almost any coastal nation can.
2. **Supercomputer climate simulations make conditional *predictions*; the economic forecasts make almost pure *prophecies*.** A forecaster in 1902 could not have foreseen nuclear power, nor the campaign against it, on environmental grounds, that stopped it reducing emissions.
3. **The debate fixates on how "anthropogenic" warming is.** That is like preparing only for human-induced hurricanes.

Conclusion: subordinating all policy to optimizing a century-long prediction cannot work. Delaying tactics "must be subordinate to, increasing our ability to intervene *after* events turn out as we did not foresee." Effort should go into cheap ways to reduce the temperature or thrive at a higher one: removing carbon dioxide, generating clouds over the oceans, encouraging aquatic organisms to absorb more. Deutsch's evidence that it was unforeseen: in 1971 Paul Ehrlich warned of global *cooling*.

## Case studies of failed prophecy

- **Paul Ehrlich, 1971.** He predicted billions starving within ten or twenty years, the Vietnam War as a fight over tin, rubber and petroleum, and a United States that must shrink to 50 million people. A schoolgirl asked what would happen if one of the problems were solved. His reply ("how could we possibly solve it? … And what would we do *then*?") showed that the prophecy amounted to "*If we stop solving problems, we are doomed*". It was not shocking, "for how could it be otherwise?" He was "prophesying the content of future knowledge", and missed the real determinant: do the people and institutions "have what it takes to solve problems?"
- **Colour television and europium.** A graduate student argued that colour TV was useless "conspicuous consumption" (Veblen's term). Its red phosphor needed europium, one of the rarest elements, so there would be permanent class division once it ran out. It was a "miracle" even one element worked. Deutsch noticed this was a fine-tuning coincidence violating the Principle of Mediocrity: why would the laws of physics *only just* allow a technology humans happen to want, and in many technologies at once? No alternative red phosphor has been found, yet liquid-crystal displays use no europium, and enough europium has since been mined for a dozen screens per person. Colour displays now serve medical imaging, publishing and nature documentaries, and are given away free with magazines.
- **Easter Island.** Bronowski asked: "They came here by accident: that is not in question. The question is, Why could they not get off?" The statues "were all made *alike*" because Easter Island was a static society. Statue-building surged as disaster loomed, on roads made of trees. Attenborough instead made the island a "miniature world", a Spaceship Earth warning. But "survival depends on good resource management" has almost no content, since any object is a resource. Julius Caesar's death was not "imprudent iron management": a Popperian analysis notes that he had made himself impossible to remove *without* violence. The explanation of any collapse is never why the people made mistakes ("problems are inevitable") but "why they failed to correct them." Their errors were too elementary to teach us anything, and they failed in practically every area. Study instead their small successes, such as "stone mulching", while remembering that "one cannot draw general conclusions from rules of thumb." "The fall of tyranny is never enough": recovery needs optimism, a tradition of criticism, tolerance of dissent and education that spares creativity. The survival for centuries of a culture that never improved was itself the tragedy.
- **Biogeography as "ultimate explanation"** (Jared Diamond; earlier Engels). Llamas stayed in the Andes, the argument goes, because a hot lowland region blocked them. But traders travelled between South and Central America, and Polynesians carried livestock across greater barriers. The spread of llamas "*can only* have been prevented by people's ideas and outlook." One hunter who decided to ride a mammoth before killing it could have changed history, and biogeographers would credit the distribution of megafauna. Resources offer opportunities and pose problems, but "it is knowledge alone that converts landscapes into resources." "Both the principal opportunities for progress and the principal obstacles to progress have consisted of ideas alone." The Soviet system lost the Cold War "because its ideology wasn't true". Mechanical reinterpretations of human affairs "not only lack explanatory power, they are morally wrong as well", because they cast people as side effects of the landscape.

## Mini-enlightenments

Optimism has broken out before and been snuffed out, every time "except this once."

- **Athens.** Pericles's Funeral Oration valued democracy not because "the people should rule" but because "instead of looking upon discussion as a stumbling-block in the way of action, we think it an indispensable preliminary to any wise action at all." What it aspired to be mattered more than what it had become. Sparta, pessimistic and static, defeated it in 404 BCE.
- **Florence.** It flourished under Lorenzo de' Medici until Savonarola's Bonfire of the Vanities, "the bonfire of optimism".
- **Others.** Roger Bacon had no tradition of criticism, so his optimism died with him. The Islamic Golden Age was another.

Deutsch: "if any of those earlier experiments in optimism had succeeded, our species would be exploring the stars by now, and you and I would be immortal."

## "Nearly there" and the end of science

Because of the bias of prophecy, people assume our theories are near the limit of the knowable. Michelson said in 1894 that undiscovered physics lay in "the sixth place of decimals"; Lagrange said "the system of the world can be discovered only once"; Feynman expected no novelty "for a thousand years". Their prophecies entail "*no matter what you do, you will understand no further*", and "almost no one is creative in fields in which they are pessimistic." The most fundamental discoveries "not only consist of new explanations, but use new modes of explanation."

- **The two versions.** The *dismal* version bounds knowledge by law or decree, and it is rank pessimism, often called "optimism". The *cheerful* version says remaining ignorance will soon be eliminated. It is optimistic in form and pessimistic in substance, and utopia from a finite list of known changes "is a well-known recipe for dogmatism and tyranny."
- **Name theories as misconceptions**: Einstein's Misconception of Gravity improving on Newton's, which improved on Kepler's. Frame all growth of knowledge as "a continual transition from *problems* to *better problems*." "Infinite ignorance is a necessary condition for there to be infinite potential for knowledge. Rejecting the idea that we are 'nearly there' is a necessary condition for the avoidance of dogmatism, stagnation and tyranny." Popper: "in our infinite ignorance we are all equal."
- **John Horgan's *The End of Science*.** It rests on empiricism (only science can "resolve questions") and on confusing two kinds of ambiguity: multiple true meanings, which mark deep ideas, and deliberate vagueness, which marks "deep silliness". Splitting any future discovery into an equation plus an "ironic" interpretation lets "any potential progress… be pre-emptively reinterpreted as non-progress." Today's knowledge itself refutes him: quantum theory and general relativity are radically inconsistent, dark energy is unexplained, and "never before… has it been so obvious that our knowledge is tiny and our ignorance vast."
- **The horizon of prediction.** For each phenomenon, prediction ends where new knowledge may begin to make a difference. So treat "*all* our predictions as implicitly including the proviso 'unless the creation of new knowledge intervenes'." Predicting beyond the horizon is prophecy, but *wondering* is not, and wondering that leads to conjecture is *speculation*, which "is vital". "Every speculation begins with a problem," and problems reach beyond the horizon too.
- **The Singularity.** It is no discontinuity: our capacity to cope grows with the rate of innovation. "If everyone were suddenly able to think a million times as fast, no one would feel hurried." (On superhuman AI, see `people-and-universality.md`.)

"What lies ahead of us is in any case infinity. All we can choose is whether it is an infinity of ignorance or of knowledge, wrong or right, death or life."
