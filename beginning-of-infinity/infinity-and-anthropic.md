# Infinity, proof and anthropic arguments

Deutsch's account of infinity and of what it does to probability, proof and "typicality", and how that settles fine-tuning, anthropic, simulation and doomsday arguments. The *infinite-regress check* is in `SKILL.md`.

## Why infinity is unavoidable

- **The universality of reason.** The beginning of infinity depends on infinities: finite local symbols for laws that apply to the whole of space-time, and people as universal explainers. "If you believe that there are bounds on the domain in which reason is the proper arbiter of ideas, then you believe in unreason or the supernatural… if you reject the infinite, you are stuck with the finite, and the finite is parochial." "The best explanation of *anything* eventually involves universality, and therefore infinity. The reach of explanations cannot be limited by fiat."
- ***Finitism*** holds that only finite abstract entities exist, and must therefore deny the law of the excluded middle. "Finitism is instrumentalism applied to mathematics". It "regards parochialism as a virtue of a theory rather than a vice", and claims a privileged access to the finite that does not exist: "All access to abstract entities, finite or infinite, is via theory", and "there are no entities *within* our 'direct experience'."
- **The argument from personal incredulity** (Dawkins's name for it) "is no argument: it represents nothing but a preference for parochial misconceptions over universal truths."
- Cantor's work was what Conway calls "the mathematicians' liberation movement". Religious objections to it resembled the Principle of Mediocrity.

## The logic of infinity

- **Mathematical infinity**: a set is infinite if it "can be placed in one-to-one correspondence with part of itself". *Countably infinite* sets correspond one-to-one with the natural numbers.
- **Hilbert's Infinity Hotel** has rooms 1, 2, 3… with no last room.
  - A new guest arrives at a full hotel: everyone moves from room *n* to room *n*+1. An infinite train arrives: everyone doubles their room number.
  - Every room is infinitely far from "infinity", and every guest is more privileged than almost all others. "Every room is at the beginning of infinity… we are only just scratching the surface, and shall never be doing anything else."
  - Trash passed from room to room in halving times is, after two minutes, *nowhere*: a ***singularity***. A puppy thrown in cannot be retrieved; reversing the process would leave "why a puppy?" unexplained.
  - The staff cannot delegate endlessly. "Infinity is not magic. It has logical rules".
- **Cantor's diagonal argument** shows the continuum is larger than the naturals: list every number between 0 and 1, then build one that differs from the *n*th in its *n*th digit. There are uncountably many orders of infinity. Announcements are finite, so the instructions that can be given are countable, and "*almost all* ways in which the guests could… be distributed among the rooms are unattainable."
- ***Infinite regress***: "a fallacy in which an argument or explanation depends on a sub-argument of the same form which purports to address essentially the same problem as the original argument". Turtles all the way down; the designer who needs a designer.

## Probability and typicality over infinite sets

- **No typical member.** There is no typical room number. "Rare", "common", "typical" and "proportion" do not apply to infinite sets: the odd numbers can be rearranged to look like a third of the naturals instead of half.
- **The book-gift thought experiment.** Every guest receives a book, and one in a million gets an old one. The guests are then re-sorted into odd and even rooms. "There is *no such thing* as the probability" that your book is old.
- **Labels cannot predict.** Relabel an infinite set of universes and the "proportion" in which some constant lies in a given range changes. "Scientific explanations cannot possibly depend on how we choose to label the entities referred to in the theory. So anthropic reasoning, by itself, cannot make predictions."
- ***Measure***: "a method by which a theory gives meaning to proportions and averages of infinite sets of things, such as universes". Only physics can supply one: in the Lyra thought experiment, a device that visits universes in a fixed order gives a running average, and so a meaningful "half". Quantum theory provides the measure for the multiverse (see `multiverse.md`).
- **Before using a probability, ask** where the measure comes from. If none is given by a physical theory, the attributes "probable or improbable, rare or common, typical or untypical have literally no meaning".

## Physics decides what is finite, and what is provable

- **Zeno's mistake**: "to confuse an abstract attribute with a physical one of the same name". It assumes a priori knowledge of physics. "Only the laws of physics determine what is finite in nature." Zeno presumed that mathematical infinity captured a physical distinction about Achilles and the tortoise. Kant held Euclidean geometry to be true of nature a priori. Gauss measured a triangle, and near Earth angles sum to as much as 180.0000002 degrees, which satellite navigation must allow for.
- **Almost everything is out of reach.** Turing showed almost all functions are non-computable, because functions are uncountable and programs countable. Gödel showed almost all mathematical truths have no proofs, and almost all statements are *undecidable*. "The laws of physics provide us with only a narrow window through which we can look out on the world of abstractions." That undecidable statements about infinite sets exist is itself an argument that abstractions exist objectively. In the Infinity Hotel the prime-pairs conjecture could be decided in five minutes: "there is nothing *mathematically* special about the undecidable questions… They are distinguished by physics only."
- **Proof is physical.**
  - ***Computation***: "a physical process that instantiates the properties of some abstract entity". ***Proof***: "a computation which, given a theory of how the computer on which it runs works, establishes the truth of some abstract proposition."
  - "Whether a mathematical proposition is true or not is indeed independent of physics. But the *proof* of such a proposition is a matter of physics only. There is no such thing as abstractly proving something, just as there is no such thing as abstractly knowing something."
  - "The reliability of our knowledge of mathematics remains for ever subsidiary to that of our knowledge of physical reality." "Proof theory is a science: specifically, it is computer science." What counts as *simple* or *complex* also depends on the laws of physics, and quantum computation changes it.
- **What mathematics is for.** Seeking secure foundations is justificationism. "The object of mathematics is to understand – to *explain* – abstract entities. Proof is primarily a means of ruling out false explanations"; "mathematics seeks not random truths but good explanations." The order is: understand, conjecture a proof, prove. P≠NP has been fruitful though unproved.
- **Undecidability does not contradict "problems are soluble".** "Problems are conflicts between ideas", and most mathematical questions are uninteresting. Proving a question undecidable solves the mathematician's problem, and failed attempts yield theorems. Deutsch's conjecture: "*if the question is interesting, then the problem is soluble.*" It has three corollaries:
  1. Insoluble problems are inherently uninteresting.
  2. Whether a problem is interesting is objective in the long run.
  3. Why interesting problems are soluble is itself a soluble problem, and "when we do" solve it, "there will be infinitely more left to explain."
- **The real limits.** One is the speed of light. Another is that knowledge comes only by conjecture and criticism, so errors are inevitable. And "the most important of all limitations on knowledge-creation is that we cannot prophesy: we cannot predict the content of ideas yet to be created, or their effects." That limit is entailed by unlimited growth. Soluble does not mean we already know the solutions; that would be creationism. "If *progress* ever depended on violating a law of physics, then 'problems are soluble' would be false."
- **The laws look fine-tuned for computation.** They have a finite set of elementary operations, a uniform distinction between finite and infinite, and are computable by a universal classical computer (Wigner's "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics"). Anthropic reasoning cannot explain this. Neither can a "Great Simulator" running our world, which is a chimera and an infinite regress, and gives up on explanation. "Computational universality is all about computers *inside* our physical world".

## Fine-tuning and anthropic explanations

- ***Fine-tuning***: the constants of physics seem tuned for life. Brandon Carter calculated in 1974 that if the interaction between charged particles were a few per cent smaller, there would be no planets. It is a legitimate problem. But "arguing for supernatural explanations on the grounds that a current scientific explanation is flawed or lacking is just a mistake… the existence of an unsolved problem in physics is no more evidence for a supernatural explanation than the existence of an unsolved crime is evidence that a ghost committed it."
- ***Anthropic explanation***: we observe these values because only they permit observers. The weak anthropic principle is just logic.
- **Sciama's argument.** Suppose a constant permits astrophysicists only between 137 and 138, with the optimum at 137.5. Anthropic reasoning predicts a value spread across that range like darts on a dartboard. Measuring a value near the optimum would statistically *refute* the anthropic explanation. With several constants the prediction is that the values lie near the edge. Counting "near the edge" as within 10 per cent of it, one constant gives a 20 per cent chance, two give 36 per cent, three nearly half, and a hundred over 99.9999999 per cent. "Whatever anthropic reasoning predicts about the values of multiple constants, it predicts will only just happen."
- **Feynman's argument.** If observers were the explanation, the cheapest world containing you would be a sphere of order around your brain with chaos outside, and you would die within a picosecond. Each further moment of order outside is "an extreme version of the gambler's hunches".
- **The verdict.** "Anthropic reasoning may well be part of the explanation… it can never be the whole explanation… Specific explanation, in terms of specific laws of nature, is needed." Anthropic selection is a single act of selection, unlike evolution. It belongs to the interconnected family of easy explanations, with spontaneous generation and creationism. The real difficulty is that the world is "so *simple* that we cannot yet understand it".
- **Black-hole cosmology** (Smolin), where universes evolve, has the measure problem and an infinite regress. If evolution reached equilibrium "an infinite time ago", it never happened; if it started at a finite time, fine-tuning returns.
- **A single infinite space** could stand in for anthropic infinities of universes. Physical laws are analytic functions, so "the probability that an astrophysicist exists" is non-zero almost everywhere, and there are infinitely many astrophysicists regardless. The anthropic explanation cancels itself out, and it cannot solve Fermi's problem, "Where are they?", without a measure.

## Arguments about our place in a population

Each of these treats us as a typical member of an infinite or open-ended set. Check the measure, and check for prophecy.

- **The simulation argument** (Bostrom) says most instances of us are simulated, so we probably are. Ask whether "most instances" means "near certainty". Deutsch's puff-pastry thought experiment imagines space layered so that London has a million layers and Oxford one; you should not bet on being in London. "We should be counting histories not instances."
- **The doomsday argument** says a typical human lives halfway through human history, so the total number of humans will be about twice the number so far. It is a prophecy. It also fails if the total is infinite, since then every human lives unusually early, which "suggests that we are at the beginning of infinity." Illness and old age may be cured, and brain backups may give effective immortality, so the total number of humans gives no bound on how long people live.
- **The quantum suicide argument** connects a lottery to a death machine to make a win what its proponents call a "subjective certainty". It requires an extra assumption: that histories without the decision-maker can be ignored. Deutsch guesses that assumption is false.
- **Cosmology and the long run.** Tipler's *omega-point* universes would perform infinite computation before a Big Crunch, but they are ruled out by observation: the expansion is accelerating, driven by *dark energy*. Dark energy might be harnessed for unbounded but ever-slower computation. In an infinite space whose visible portion grows, everything physically possible is eventually seen, and a colonizing civilization, a beginning of infinity, would reach us.
