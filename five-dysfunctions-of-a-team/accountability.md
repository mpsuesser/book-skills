# Dysfunction 4: Avoidance of accountability

*Accountability* has become a buzzword, as overused as *empowerment* and *quality*. In teamwork it means one specific thing: "the willingness of team members to call their peers on performance or behaviors that might hurt the team." Once a team has clarity and buy-in, its members hold each other accountable for what they signed up to do and "for high standards of performance and behavior." The dysfunction's mark is **low standards**.

**The essence** is members' unwillingness to tolerate the **interpersonal discomfort** of calling a peer on behavior, along with the general tendency to avoid difficult conversations. It is that moment when you know you have to call a peer on something that matters and decide to let it go. Members of great teams overcome the inclination and **enter the danger** with one another.

**Why peers are hardest.** Holding direct reports accountable, even on sticky issues, is hard; holding peers accountable is harder. "We're supposed to be equals," and correcting a peer feels like sticking your nose into their business. Close friendships make it harder still: members fear jeopardizing a valuable relationship. Ironically, the relationship then deteriorates as members resent one another for missing expectations and letting the group's standards erode. Holding one another accountable improves relationships, because it shows respect and high expectations.

**Who escapes accountability.** "Some people are hard to hold accountable because they are so helpful. Others because they get defensive. Others because they are intimidating." The helpful member who does everyone's unglamorous work is the easiest to let off the hook, and "it's hard to come down on someone who is always pitching in" is not a good excuse.

**Peer pressure.** "As politically incorrect as it sounds, the most effective and efficient means of maintaining high standards of performance on a team is peer pressure." It cuts the need for bureaucracy around performance management and corrective action. More than any policy or system, "the fear of letting down respected teammates" motivates people to improve.

## What a team does, avoiding accountability and holding to it

| A team that avoids accountability | A team whose members hold one another accountable |
|---|---|
| breeds resentment between members with different standards of performance | builds respect among members held to the same high standards |
| encourages mediocrity | makes poor performers feel pressure to improve |
| misses deadlines and key deliverables | spots potential problems quickly, because members question one another's approaches without hesitation |
| leaves the leader as the sole source of discipline, an undue burden | avoids excessive bureaucracy around performance management and corrective action |

## Tools for overcoming it

A few classic management tools, as effective as they are simple:

- **Publication of Goals and Standards.** Clarify publicly what the team must achieve, who must deliver what, and how everyone must behave to succeed. "The enemy of accountability is ambiguity." Even after a team commits to a plan or behavioral standards, keep the agreements in the open so no one can easily ignore them.
- **Simple and Regular Progress Reviews.** A little structure goes a long way, especially for feedback on behavior or performance. Members regularly tell one another, verbally or in writing, how they think their teammates are doing against stated objectives and standards. Leaving it to them to do so on their own, with no expectations or structure, invites avoidance.
- **Team Rewards.** Shift rewards from individual performance to team achievement. A team is unlikely to stand by quietly and fail because one peer isn't pulling their weight.

## The leader's role

The hard part is to encourage and allow the team to be "the first and primary accountability mechanism." Strong leaders often create an **accountability vacuum**, leaving themselves as the only source of discipline. Members then assume the leader is holding others accountable and stay quiet even when they see something wrong. Once a culture of accountability exists, the leader must still be willing to act as "the ultimate arbiter of discipline when the team itself fails." That should be rare. Everyone must understand that accountability has not been handed to a consensus process, only made a shared team responsibility, and that the leader will step in whenever necessary.

## How Kathryn worked it

- **Review real goals, then ask who challenged.** At the second off-site she skipped exercises and had the new sales head review progress on the four drivers behind the customer goal: product demonstrations, competitive analysis, sales training, product brochures. When the helpful member admitted competitive analysis hadn't started because other people weren't available, the sales head asked constructively who needed to be more responsive. Kathryn then asked the room, "Does anyone see a problem here?" The member should have raised the blockage earlier, and "Not one of you challenged him." A vice president must prioritize by what the team agreed and challenge people who don't respond to his requests. She told him she was using him as an example because he was easy to let off the hook.
- **What to challenge.** "I want all of you challenging each other about what you are doing, how you are spending your time, whether you are making enough progress."
- **How to push.** "Push with respect, and under the assumption that the other person is probably doing the right thing. But push anyway. And never hold back." When a member objected that pushing sounds like a lack of trust, Kathryn answered that trust "is not the same as assuming everyone is on the same page as you, and that they don't need to be pushed."
- **Slamming an absent teammate.** "Don't ever slam one of your teammates when that person isn't in the room... take your issues to her directly, or to me. You're going to have to make that right." When the team started running down a colleague who had just quit, the very peer she had corrected stopped them: "let's not do this. He's gone, and we need to figure out what we're going to do."
- **Announce the standard and invite the team to enforce it.** Closing the first off-site, Kathryn told the team that for the next two weeks she would be "pretty intolerant of behavior that demonstrates an absence of trust, or a focus on individual ego", would encourage conflict, drive for clear commitments, and expect them all to hold each other accountable: "I will be calling out bad behavior when I see it, and I'd like to see you doing the same."
- **Keep pressing after the off-sites.** In the following weeks she chided one member for eroding trust by appearing smug in meetings, forced another to confront the team about its slow response to customer issues, and worked late nights with two others through budget battles that had to be fought.
- **The team takes over.** By the last off-site, the members who once avoided discomfort were choosing it: "if it comes down to a little interpersonal discomfort versus politics, I'm opting for the discomfort." Even a meeting rule like no laptops passed from Kathryn enforcing it to the team watching for it.

## Connection to results

Teammates who aren't held accountable for their contributions turn their attention to their own needs and to advancing themselves or their departments. "An absence of accountability is an invitation to team members to shift their attention to areas other than collective results."
