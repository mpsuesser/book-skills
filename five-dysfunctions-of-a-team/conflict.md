# Dysfunction 2: Fear of conflict

All great relationships that last require productive conflict to grow, in marriage, parenthood, friendship and business. Yet conflict is taboo in many settings, especially at work, and the higher up the management chain, the more time and energy people spend avoiding the passionate debates every great team needs. Without trust, a team won't "engage in open, constructive, ideological conflict" and will "continue to preserve a sense of artificial harmony."

**Productive ideological conflict vs destructive fighting.** Ideological conflict is limited to concepts and ideas and avoids "personality-focused, mean-spirited attacks." It can share the external marks of interpersonal conflict (passion, emotion, frustration), enough that an outsider might mistake it for discord. Teams that do it know its only purpose is "to produce the best possible solution in the shortest period of time." They resolve issues faster and more completely and come out of heated debates with no residual feelings or collateral damage, ready for the next issue.

**Tension is not conflict.** A team can have plenty of friction and no constructive conflict at all: frustration surfaces as subtle, passive or sarcastic comments, or is bottled up and carried around. Harmony is good only "if it comes as a result of working through issues constantly and cycling through conflict." Harmony that comes from people holding back opinions and honest concerns is false. Kathryn would trade it any day "for a team's willingness to argue effectively about an issue and then walk away with no collateral damage."

**Two ironies.**
- Teams avoid ideological conflict to spare feelings and end up breeding dangerous tension. Disagreements that aren't debated openly turn into back-channel personal attacks, "far nastier and more harmful than any heated argument over issues."
- Teams avoid conflict in the name of efficiency, yet healthy conflict saves time. Avoiders revisit the same issues again and again without resolution. Taking an issue "off-line" is often a euphemism for not dealing with it, and it comes back at the next meeting. At DecisionTech, outsourcing IT came up at every meeting, half for and half against, and sat there because no one wanted to anger anyone. As one member put it, that is exactly what made everyone angry.

## What a team does, fearing conflict and engaging in it

| Teams that fear conflict | Teams that engage in conflict |
|---|---|
| hold boring meetings | hold lively, interesting meetings |
| let back-channel politics and personal attacks thrive | minimize politics |
| ignore the controversial topics that are critical to the team's success | put critical topics on the table |
| miss some of their members' opinions and perspectives | extract and exploit every member's ideas |
| spend time and energy on posturing and interpersonal risk management | solve real problems quickly |

## Tools for overcoming it

The first step is acknowledging that conflict is productive and that the team tends to avoid it. While some members believe conflict is unnecessary, it has little chance of happening.

- **Mining.** On conflict-avoiding teams, someone must occasionally act as a "miner of conflict": a person who extracts buried disagreements and sheds the light of day on them. It takes courage and confidence to call out sensitive issues and force the team to work through them, objectivity during the meeting, and a commitment to stay with the conflict until it is resolved. A team may assign a member to the role for a given meeting or discussion.
- **Real-Time Permission.** While mining, members coach one another not to retreat from healthy debate. When people in a conflict grow uncomfortable with the level of discord, interrupt to remind them that what they are doing is necessary. Simple and paternal as it sounds, it drains tension from a productive but difficult exchange and gives participants the confidence to continue. Afterwards, remind them that the conflict was good for the team and not something to avoid in future.
- **Other tools.** Personality and behavioral preference profiles describe how each type deals with conflict, so members can anticipate their own and others' approach or resistance. The Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument (TKI) maps natural inclinations around conflict so members can choose deliberately which approach fits which situation.

## The leader's role

A leader's hardest challenge here is the wish to protect members from harm. It leads to "premature interruption of disagreements" and keeps people from developing their own skills for handling conflict. Like parents who shield children from every quarrel with siblings, it strains the relationships and leaves the participants "hungry for resolution that never occurs." So the leader **demonstrates restraint**, letting resolution occur naturally, however messy, even though losing control of the team can feel like failing at the job. The leader also models appropriate conflict personally. A leader who avoids necessary conflict, as many executives do, lets this dysfunction thrive.

## How Kathryn worked it

- **Meetings are the arena.** The single most important setting for conflict is meetings: "If we cannot learn to engage in productive, ideological conflict during meetings, we are through." The team's capacity for "passionate, unfiltered debate" will shape its future as much as any product or partnership.
- **Meetings and movies.** A movie and a staff meeting run about the same length, ninety minutes to two hours. Meetings are interactive and relevant: we have our say, and the outcome changes our lives. Movies are neither, yet we would rather watch a bad movie than sit through a meeting. Every movie worth watching has conflict, and "without it, we just don't care what happens to the characters." Her promise: every staff meeting would be loaded with conflict and not boring, "And if there is nothing worth debating, then we won't have a meeting."
- **Mining the buried issue.** When a member hesitated ("I don't want to stir things up"), she answered "I want you to." When the buried issue surfaced (whether engineering held too much of the company's resources), she first defused it ("let's have this out. And let's not pretend we're doing anything wrong... This is not a religious battle. It's about strategy"), then stoked it by naming the defender's frustration aloud: "I'm guessing you're tired of people questioning our investment in engineering."
- **Peers press, the frame widens.** A peer asked the defender directly why he got so defensive. Another reframed the issue from his competence to a question for everyone: how good the products need to be to win. Kathryn added that no one is smart enough to know the answer "without hearing from everyone else." The defender then mapped his whole organization on the whiteboard. The team argued vehemently for two hours, changed their minds, retrenched, and found the answer less obvious than it had seemed. Every member, Kathryn included, went to the whiteboard at least once. "If anyone yawned, it was because they were exhausted, not bored." They agreed on a complex but workable solution with an aggressive timeline.
- **Hold back and let peers carry it.** She let a peer challenge a sarcastic colleague rather than stepping in to protect him. When an uncomfortable exchange broke out over dinner, she watched, "resisting the temptation to make artificial peace." She asked why the group didn't debate and let the uncomfortable silence sit until someone answered. In the heat of a real deal, though, she found that restraint "was not her best quality." The leader's own opinions can shut down a debate that should belong to the team.
- **Fighting about issues is the job.** When two executives ended up pounding the table over sales spending and one concluded that nothing had changed, Kathryn called it perfect: "You *are* fighting. But about issues. That's your job." If executives don't hash it out, they leave their people to solve problems they can't solve; employees want the debate settled so they get clear direction.
- **It never gets comfortable.** "I don't think anyone ever gets completely used to conflict. If it's not a little uncomfortable, then it's not real. The key is to keep doing it anyway."
- **Meeting ground rules.** Be present and participate: everyone fully engaged, no laptops ("It's more of a behavioral issue than a technological one"). When an issue truly concerns only some members, anyone may say it belongs outside the meeting.

## Connection to commitment

By engaging in productive conflict and drawing out every member's perspective, a team can commit confidently, knowing it has benefited from everyone's ideas.
