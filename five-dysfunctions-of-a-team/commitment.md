# Dysfunction 3: Lack of commitment

Lack of commitment is "the failure to buy in to decisions," and its evidence is **ambiguity**. Commitment is a function of **clarity** and **buy-in**. Great teams "make clear and timely decisions and move forward with complete buy-in from every member of the team, even those who voted against the decision," and leave meetings confident that no one is quietly harboring doubts about supporting what was agreed. "When people don't unload their opinions and feel like they've been listened to, they won't really get on board."

## The two causes

- **Consensus.** "Consensus is horrible." When real agreement arrives quickly and naturally, that's terrific. Usually it doesn't, and consensus becomes an attempt to please everyone, which, as a member put it, ends up displeasing everyone equally. Great teams know reasonable people don't need their way to support a decision; they need to know their opinions were heard and considered. Genuinely considering every idea creates a willingness to rally around whatever the group decides. At an impasse, the leader is allowed to make the call. Some teams are "paralyzed by their need for complete agreement." **Disagree and commit**: members can argue and disagree and still commit "as though everyone originally bought into the decision completely." Making people comply is not the same: a coach doesn't let players vote on wind sprints, but he lets them make their case, tells them why he disagrees, and then sends them running.
- **Certainty.** Great teams unite behind decisions even with little assurance the decision is correct, following the old military axiom that "*a* decision is better than *no* decision." It is better to decide boldly and be wrong, then change direction with equal boldness, than to waffle. Teams that hedge and wait for enough data to feel certain breed paralysis and a lack of confidence.

Conflict underlies the willingness to commit without perfect information. Often a team already has all the information it needs, inside "the hearts and minds of the team itself," and it must be extracted through unfiltered debate.

**The ripple effect.** For an executive team, more than any other dysfunction, failing to commit creates "unresolvable discord deeper in the organization." Employees clash when they try to interpret marching orders that don't line up with those of colleagues in other departments. "Like a vortex, small gaps between executives high up in an organization become major discrepancies by the time they reach employees below."

## What a team does, failing to commit and committing

| A team that fails to commit | A team that commits |
|---|---|
| leaves its direction and priorities ambiguous | is clear on direction and priorities, with everyone aligned around common objectives |
| lets windows of opportunity close through excessive analysis and needless delay | takes opportunities before competitors do |
| breeds lack of confidence and fear of failure | learns from its mistakes |
| revisits discussions and decisions again and again | moves forward without hesitation |
| encourages second-guessing among its members | changes direction without hesitation or guilt |

## Tools for overcoming it

Maximize clarity, achieve buy-in, and resist the lure of consensus and certainty.

- **Cascading Messaging** (minimum 10 minutes, and free). At the end of every staff meeting or off-site, explicitly review the key decisions and agree on what to communicate to employees and other constituencies. Members often find they are not on the same page and must clarify outcomes before acting. They also settle which decisions stay confidential and which go out quickly and comprehensively. Leaving aligned sends a welcome message to employees used to contradictory accounts from managers who attended the same meeting.
- **Deadlines.** Set clear deadlines for when decisions will be made, and honor them with discipline and rigidity. Ambiguity is this dysfunction's worst enemy, and timing is one of the most critical things to make clear. Deadlines for intermediate decisions and milestones matter as much as final ones, because they surface misalignment before its costs grow too great.
- **Contingency and Worst-Case Scenario Analysis.** Briefly discuss contingency plans up front or, better, clarify the worst case for a decision the team is struggling with. This usually shows that the cost of a wrong decision is survivable and far less damaging than feared.
- **Low-Risk Exposure Therapy.** Practice decisiveness in low-risk situations: decide after substantial discussion but little analysis or research. Teams usually find the decision was better than expected, and that lengthy study wouldn't have changed it much. Research and analysis matter; commitment-phobic teams overvalue them.

## The leader's role

More than anyone, the leader must be comfortable with the prospect of a decision that turns out wrong, and must keep pushing the group toward closure and toward keeping the schedules it has set. The leader cannot put too high a premium on certainty or consensus.

## How Kathryn worked it: deciding the overarching goal

At the end of the first off-site, she turned the remaining two hours into the team's first substantive decision-making meeting. The sequence:

1. **Pose one question.** "If we do anything between now and the end of the year, what should that be?" The answer is the team's **overarching goal**, a single theme for the rest of the year.
2. **Surface the dissenters.** Most said market share. Two looked unconvinced, and she called on them: product improvement, cost containment.
3. **Make peers argue it.** "Someone take them on." She held back from answering the alternatives herself.
4. **Refuse a list of goals.** Asked whether they could have more than one: "If everything is important, then nothing is." She left the group to work it out.
5. **Reward real debate.** "This is the most productive conversation I've heard since I've been here. Keep going."
6. **Insist on distinctions that matter.** When someone said market share or customers didn't matter, "we just need to sell," she said it did matter and asked another member to weigh in.
7. **Break the endless loop.** When a member said this was the same academic conversation they had every meeting, she asked how those conversations usually ended ("We run out of time") and timeboxed this one to five minutes. Anyone who thought the team was "in the wrong ballpark completely" should "speak up now, and loudly."
8. **Assign advocates.** She asked one member to make a passionate plea for revenue; he talked himself out of it. Revenue would still have a goal, just not be the overarching one. A member who shrugged got the question: "Are you shrugging because you don't agree and you're giving up, or because you feel like he made a more compelling point than you can counter?"
9. **Hear the case for the finalist.** One member gave the reasons for new customer acquisition: press coverage, employee confidence, product feedback, references for next year's customers. Another added follow-on sales. Then: "unless I hear something extremely compelling in the next five seconds that makes me think otherwise, I believe we have a primary goal."
10. **Make it specific.** "How many new customers do we need to get?" After thirty minutes of debate she closed it herself: "unless someone is holding something back, I think I've heard all the opinions in the room. And we are probably not going to agree completely, which is fine, because there is no science here. I'm going to set the number based on your input, and we are going to stick with that number." She told the high and low advocates why their numbers were wrong, then declared it: eighteen new customers by December 31, at least ten of them willing to be active references.
11. **Drill into each function's part.** For the next hour they worked out what each function, from marketing to finance to engineering, had to do to make the number. In twenty minutes they had made more progress than in a month of meetings.
12. **Ask for last concerns.** "Are there any comments, questions, or concerns people want to raise before we leave?"

Elsewhere the same pattern held. To break a tie on whether to hire from inside, she asked, "This has been a good discussion, and I think I've heard everyone. Does anyone have anything else to add?" Then she decided, and everyone nodded support, even those who had argued the other way. When a peer answered an objection with "No, no. Go ahead," the new sales head wouldn't accept it: "if you think there is a good reason... then I'm open to hearing it." A swallowed objection is unbought-in commitment.

## Connection to accountability

To call each other on behaviors and actions, teammates need a clear sense of what is expected. Even the most ardent believers in accountability balk at holding someone accountable for something that was never bought in to or made clear. People won't hold each other to a plan they haven't all bought into, because the answer will be "I never agreed to that anyway."
