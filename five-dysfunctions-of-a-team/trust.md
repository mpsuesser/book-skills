# Dysfunction 1: Absence of trust

Trust lies at the heart of a functioning, cohesive team. "Trust is the foundation of real teamwork," and there is "nothing soft about it": it is probably the most critical part of building a team. The word is used and misused until it sounds like motherhood and apple pie, so be specific about what it means here.

**Vulnerability-based trust** requires members to make themselves vulnerable to one another and be confident that their vulnerabilities will not be used against them. The vulnerabilities are "weaknesses, skill deficiencies, interpersonal shortcomings, mistakes, and requests for help." Only when members are comfortable being exposed do they stop protecting themselves. Then they can put their energy into the job rather than into being "strategically disingenuous or political" with one another. "Great teams are honest with one another... They admit their mistakes, their weaknesses, and their concerns without fear of reprisal."

It is hard because career advancement and education teach successful people to compete with their peers and protect their reputations. Turning those instincts off for the team is exactly what is required.

**Costs.** Distrusting teams waste inordinate time and energy managing their behaviors and interactions. They dread meetings and are reluctant to ask for or offer help. Morale is usually low and unwanted turnover high.

**The meeting sign.** The lack of debate is the clearest evidence of a trust problem. A lack of debate could be a good sign only if everyone were fully on the same page, in lockstep, with no confusion, and every effective team Kathryn observed had substantial debate, "even the most trusting teams." Her test of the DecisionTech staff: they agreed on almost nothing, yet no one would admit to having concerns.

## What members do, without trust and with it

| Without trust, members | On a trusting team, members |
|---|---|
| hide their weaknesses and mistakes from each other | admit weaknesses and mistakes |
| hold back from asking for help or giving constructive feedback | ask for help, and take risks in offering feedback and assistance |
| hold back from helping outside their own area of responsibility | accept questions and input about their own area |
| jump to conclusions about others' intentions and aptitudes without checking | give each other the benefit of the doubt before reaching a negative conclusion |
| leave one another's skills and experience untapped | appreciate and tap one another's skills and experience |
| spend time and energy managing their behavior for effect | spend time and energy on the important issues, not politics |
| hold grudges | offer and accept apologies without hesitation |
| dread meetings and find reasons to avoid time together | look forward to meetings and other chances to work as a group |

## Tools for overcoming it

Vulnerability-based trust can't be built overnight. It takes shared experiences over time, repeated follow-through and credibility, and in-depth understanding of each member's unique attributes. A focused approach can still accelerate it dramatically.

- **Personal Histories Exercise** (minimum 30 minutes). Go around the table and have each member answer a short list of low-risk questions: number of siblings, hometown, unique challenges of childhood, favorite hobbies, first job, worst job. Kathryn's five were: Hometown? Number of kids in the family? Interesting childhood hobbies? Biggest challenge growing up? First job? Her caveat: "I want to hear about your life as a child, but I'm not interested in your inner child." Members begin to see one another as human beings with life stories. This builds empathy and discourages "unfair and inaccurate behavioral attributions." Almost every answer turns up something the others didn't know. At DecisionTech, forty-five minutes of mild disclosure made the team tighter than it had been all year.
- **Team Effectiveness Exercise** (minimum 60 minutes). More rigorous and relevant, and riskier. Each member identifies, for every peer, the single most important contribution that peer makes to the team and the one area the peer must improve or eliminate for the team's good. Responses are reported one person at a time, usually starting with the leader. It looks intrusive but is remarkably manageable and yields a great deal of constructive and positive information in about an hour. Even a relatively dysfunctional team can often make it work.
- **Personality Profiles** (minimum 4 hours). Profiles of behavioral preferences and personality styles are among the most effective and lasting trust tools. Lencioni's firm, The Table Group, later built The Six Types of Working Genius to identify each member's natural gifts and frustrations. Other common profiles are the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) and Everything DiSC. Understanding one another's styles stops members from making unfair judgments about one another's motivation and lets them use their gifts for the team.
- **360-Degree Feedback**. Riskier still, because peers make specific judgments and give constructive criticism. The key is divorcing it entirely from compensation and formal performance evaluation and using it as a developmental tool. Even a slight link to evaluation or pay gives it "dangerous political undertones."
- **Experiential Team Exercises**. Ropes courses and similar activities have lost their luster, deservedly, because their benefits don't always carry into the working world. They add value only when "layered upon more fundamental and relevant activities." Kathryn's version: "We're not going to be catching each other falling out of trees."

Every tool needs regular follow-up in daily work. Revisit individual development areas so progress keeps its momentum. Even on a strong team, "perhaps especially so," atrophy erodes trust.

## The leader's role

The most important action is to **demonstrate vulnerability first**, risking loss of face so that others will take the same risk. Build an environment that doesn't punish vulnerability: even well-intentioned teams subtly discourage trust by chastising one another for admitting weakness or failure. The leader's displays must be genuine; "One of the best ways to lose the trust of a team is to feign vulnerability in order to manipulate the emotions of others."

## How Kathryn worked it

- **Strength and weakness exercise.** After introducing invulnerability ("the only way to do that is to overcome our need for invulnerability"), she gave everyone five minutes to name their single biggest strength and weakness in terms of their contribution to the company's success or failure. Her rules: no generic weakness, no glossing over strengths out of modesty, "be willing to put yourself out there." She went first, naming a real weakness and asking for help with it: she was a bad external spokesperson and would need the team's help.
- **Peers sharpen the disclosures.** When someone downplayed a strength, a peer wouldn't let them off the hook. When someone named a safe weakness, a peer named the real one, and the person just nodded and took a note. A pattern surfaced: a person's strength and weakness are usually rooted in the same things (fearless negotiator, comes across as arrogant).
- **A member who won't open up.** One member gave a shallow answer and the room went silent. Kathryn judged she couldn't force it: the member "had to do it herself." Walking back after a night when the group had been hard on that member, Kathryn checked in privately, without drama, and told her the group needed to hear what she thought. The same member was never teased in the good-natured but substantive ribbing the others traded. Her silence and her exclusion from the ribbing spoke volumes about her inability to trust her teammates.
- **Guard the disclosures.** When that member mocked a peer who had just exposed a weakness, Kathryn let it pass and later regretted it. A jab at someone's disclosure is the punishment of vulnerability that the leader must not let stand.
- **The leader takes correction.** When a member asked her to say "us" and "we" instead of "you," Kathryn agreed on the spot ("I'm not a consultant here. Thanks for calling me on it") and admitted she didn't feel part of the group yet. That opened another member to admit she felt isolated in her own function.
- **Disclosure of motive.** A peer who had pushed a deal publicly admitted it was really about having something to work on, apologized for slamming an absent colleague, and asked the team for help finding a way to contribute. Kathryn let the moment speak for itself rather than congratulating him.
- **Expect the glow to fade.** The warmth of personal disclosure diminishes as soon as talk turns back to work. Back at the office after the first off-site, the team acted embarrassed at having exposed themselves and pretended it hadn't happened. That is a typical first response; the cure is to dive right back in.

## Connection to conflict

Trust makes conflict possible: members engage in passionate, sometimes emotional debate because they know they won't be punished for saying something that might otherwise be read as destructive or critical.
