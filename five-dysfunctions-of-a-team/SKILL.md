# The Five Dysfunctions of a Team

"Not finance. Not strategy. Not technology. It is teamwork that remains the ultimate competitive advantage, both because it is so powerful and so rare." Teams are made of imperfect human beings, so they are inherently dysfunctional. Building a strong team is "both possible and remarkably simple. But it is painfully difficult": the behaviors are theoretically uncomplicated and extremely hard to practice day after day. Success is "common sense with uncommon levels of discipline and persistence." The principles hold for the executive staff of a multinational, a small department, a school, a church, a nonprofit, and for someone who is simply a member of a team that needs improvement.

At the foundation lies **humility**: it makes vulnerability palatable and makes trust possible among people "naturally inclined toward self-protection." Teamwork is unnatural; it asks people to enter "risky discomfort" willingly.

## The model

A triangle of five stacked sections, each dysfunction resting on the one below. Beside each, Kathryn writes the condition that marks it.

| Level | Dysfunction | Marked by | What it is | A cohesive team instead |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (base) | **Absence of trust** | Invulnerability | Members are unwilling to be vulnerable within the group and are not genuinely open about their mistakes and weaknesses | They trust one another |
| 2 | **Fear of conflict** | Artificial harmony | Without trust, the team cannot engage in "unfiltered and passionate debate of ideas" and resorts to "veiled discussions and guarded comments" | They engage in unfiltered conflict around ideas |
| 3 | **Lack of commitment** | Ambiguity | Having never aired their opinions, members rarely buy in to decisions, "though they may feign agreement during meetings" | They commit to decisions and plans of action |
| 4 | **Avoidance of accountability** | Low standards | With no clear plan committed to, even focused and driven people hesitate to call peers on actions and behaviors counterproductive to the team | They hold one another accountable for delivering against those plans |
| 5 (peak) | **Inattention to results** | Status and ego | Members put individual needs (ego, career development, recognition), or the needs of their departments, above the collective goals of the team | They focus on the achievement of collective results |

The five are not distinct issues to fix in isolation. They form an interrelated model: each dysfunction breeds the next, so susceptibility to even one is "potentially lethal," and teamwork deteriorates "like a chain with just one link broken." Every team needs constant work, because without it even the best ones deviate toward dysfunction.

## The distinctions that carry the model

- **Trust** here is **vulnerability-based trust**: "the confidence among team members that their peers' intentions are good, and that there is no reason to be protective or careful around the group." It is not the predictive trust of knowing a teammate will deliver because they always have. Trust is also not assuming everyone is on the same page and needs no pushing: "Trust is knowing that when a team member does push you, they're doing it because they care about the team." Depth: [trust.md](trust.md).
- **Artificial harmony** is the peace of people holding back opinions and concerns. Harmony is good only when it comes from working through issues and cycling through conflict. **Tension is not conflict**: "Passive, sarcastic comments are not the kind of conflict I'm talking about." **Productive ideological conflict** is limited to concepts and ideas; **destructive fighting and interpersonal politics** go after personalities. The two can look alike from outside, with the same passion, emotion and frustration. Depth: [conflict.md](conflict.md).
- **Politics**: "Politics is when people choose their words and actions based on how they want others to react rather than based on what they really think." It thrives when a team is too ambiguous about what it is trying to accomplish, which makes individual success easy to chase.
- **Commitment** is "a function of two things: clarity and buy-in." Its two great enemies are the desire for **consensus** and the need for **certainty**. Reasonable people don't need their way; they need to be heard and to know their input "was considered and responded to." People "need to weigh in before they can really buy in." **Disagree and commit**: argue, disagree, then commit "as though everyone originally bought into the decision completely." Depth: [commitment.md](commitment.md).
- **Accountability** is peer-to-peer: "the willingness of team members to call their peers on performance or behaviors that might hurt the team." What stops it is **interpersonal discomfort**; great teams **enter the danger** with one another. **Peer pressure** is the most effective and efficient means of keeping standards high. Depth: [accountability.md](accountability.md).
- **Results** are collective and outcome-based, wider than profit. The alternatives a team drifts toward are **team status** (being in the group is satisfaction enough) and **individual status** (one's own position and career). "The key is to make the collective ego greater than the individual ones." Members' **first team** must be the team of peers, ahead of the departments they lead. Depth: [results.md](results.md).

## The leader's role at each level

| Dysfunction | What the leader does |
|---|---|
| Trust | Demonstrates vulnerability first, genuinely, risking losing face; builds an environment that doesn't punish admissions of weakness or failure |
| Conflict | Shows restraint while people fight, letting resolution occur naturally, however messy; models healthy conflict personally |
| Commitment | Is comfortable making a decision that turns out wrong; pushes constantly for closure and adherence to schedules; breaks impasses by making the call |
| Accountability | Lets the team be the first and primary accountability mechanism; stays the ultimate arbiter when the team fails, and steps in without hesitation |
| Results | Sets the tone: values nothing above results, stays selfless and objective, and reserves rewards and recognition for real contributions to group goals |

## Applying the book

- **Diagnosing a team**: run the team assessment with every member and discuss where answers differ ([assessment.md](assessment.md)). Read meetings for the signs: no one argues yet tension hangs in the room, decisions never get made, issues come back every meeting, meetings start and end on time to a published agenda and still decide nothing, people wait for the meeting to end, a member agrees to everything and rarely follows through, members can't say what peers are working on, departments lobby for resources. Name each finding with the model's term, and trace it down the chain to its root.
- **Leading the rebuild** as the team's leader, whether planning off-sites, giving the opening speech, sequencing the work, recovering from setbacks, or setting meeting cadence: follow Kathryn's methods ([playbook.md](playbook.md)).
- **Working one dysfunction**: open that dysfunction's file. Each holds the behaviors that show it, the tools for overcoming it with their minimum times, the leader's role, and how Kathryn worked it in the fable.
- **One member is the problem** (a peer who disparages absent colleagues, a star who won't open up, someone who should leave): see the hard conversations in [playbook.md](playbook.md).
- **The user is a member, not the leader**: the model applies to them too. They can go first with vulnerability, mine buried disagreements and give real-time permission in meetings, call a peer on behavior directly rather than behind their back, apologize quickly, keep the peer team as their first team, and judge their own department by the team's results.
- Speak in the model's terms, write scripts in the user's situation on the pattern of Kathryn's lines, and keep the work tied to real business issues and the team's results.
