# Dysfunction 5: Inattention to results

The ultimate dysfunction is "the tendency of members to care about something other than the collective goals of the group": seeking "individual recognition and attention at the expense of results." Its mark is **status and ego**. Results are "the only true measure of a team" ("The ultimate test of a great team is results"), and any team that judges itself on performance needs "an unrelenting focus on specific objectives and clearly defined outcomes."

**Results are broader than money.** They are not limited to profit, revenue or shareholder returns but mean outcome-based performance. Every good organization specifies what it plans to achieve in a given period. Those goals, more than the financial metrics they drive, make up most of the near-term, controllable results. Profit may be a corporation's ultimate measure, but the goals executives set along the way are the results they strive for as a team, and those goals drive profit.

**What a team focuses on instead.**
- **Team status.** For some members, belonging to the group is enough. Results are desirable but not worth great sacrifice or inconvenience. Altruistic nonprofits that believe the nobility of their mission justifies their satisfaction are prone to it, and so are political groups, academic departments and prestigious companies, which see success in mere association with their *special* organizations.
- **Individual status.** The familiar tendency to enhance one's own position or career prospects at the team's expense. Everyone has an innate tendency toward self-preservation, but a functional team makes the group's collective results matter more to each member than their individual goals.

Many teams are simply not results-focused; they exist to survive, not to achieve meaningful objectives. "No amount of trust, conflict, commitment, or accountability can compensate for a lack of desire to win."

**Collective ego.** There is a place for ego on a team: "The key is to make the collective ego greater than the individual ones." When everyone uses results to define success, ego has trouble getting out of hand, because "if the team loses, everyone loses." A function cannot be doing well while the company is failing.

**The score.** Sports dominate talk of teamwork because a game ends with a clear score, which leaves little room for "subjective, interpretive, ego-driven success." Great athletes have huge egos, but those egos are tied to winning. Few teams work this way, so the ones that do have a bigger advantage than ever: their competitors are mostly individuals looking out for themselves. A team's job is to make its collective results so clear that no one would even consider acting purely to enhance their own status or ego. Kathryn's husband, a high school basketball coach, won with less talented players because they played team basketball. He benched his most talented player, who cared only about his own statistics; the team played better without him. His job, as he saw it, is "to create the best team possible, not to shepherd the careers of individual athletes."

**First team.** A company of good managers who don't act as a team leaves them confused about who their **first team** is. An executive's first team must be the team of peers, not the department they lead. Loyalty to one's own staff is good, but "it simply cannot come at the expense of the loyalty and commitment we have to the group of people sitting here today." Members don't have to destroy what they've built in their departments, but they must be willing to make it secondary, and "for many of you, that might very well feel like abandonment." Test it by asking what members told their people about the team's private discussions, and who they confide in.

## What a team does, inattentive to results and focused on them

| A team not focused on results | A team focused on collective results |
|---|---|
| stagnates, fails to grow, and rarely defeats its competitors | feels its successes and its failures acutely |
| loses achievement-oriented employees | retains them |
| pushes members toward their own careers and individual goals | minimizes individualistic behavior, and gains from members who subjugate their own goals and interests to the good of the team |
| is easily distracted | avoids distractions |

## Tools for overcoming it

Make results clear, and reward only the behaviors and actions that contribute to them.

- **Public Declaration of Results.** Coaches hate a player publicly guaranteeing a win, because it can provoke the opponent. For most other teams, public proclamations of intended success help. Teams that commit publicly to specific results work with a passionate, even desperate desire to achieve them. "Teams that say, 'We'll do our best,' are subtly, if not purposefully, preparing themselves for failure."
- **Results-Based Rewards.** Tie rewards, especially compensation, to specific outcomes. Relying on this alone assumes money is the only driver of behavior. Still, paying a bonus for "trying hard" with no results signals that the outcome may not matter much.

## The leader's role

More than with any other dysfunction, the leader sets the tone. If members sense the leader values anything other than results, they take it as permission to do the same. Leaders must be selfless and objective, and reserve rewards and recognition for those who make real contributions to group goals.

## How Kathryn worked it

- **Build a scoreboard.** She split the team into groups of two or three to propose results categories, with no numbers yet. More than fifteen categories were merged and cut to seven: revenue, expenses, new customer acquisition, current customer satisfaction, employee retention, market awareness, product quality. They would be measured monthly, since a quarter gave too few chances to spot problems and change course. Goals must be "simple enough to grasp easily, and specific enough to be actionable." Profit alone is not actionable enough: with only profit as a guide, the team doesn't know how it is doing "until the season is almost over."
- **Metrics on paper are not a focus on results.** The team objected that they already tracked these metrics. Kathryn asked one member what last quarter's PR goal had been; he didn't know. The member who owned it had brought the numbers every week and no one had asked about them. A focus on results means "everyone adopting a set of common goals and measurements, and then actually using them to make collective decisions on a daily basis." Her two diagnostic questions:
  - How often did you talk about moving resources from one department to another in the middle of the quarter to rescue a goal in jeopardy?
  - How disciplined were you in meetings about reviewing the goals in detail and drilling down on why they were or weren't being met?
- **The half-time locker room.** Picture a coach at half-time calling in the center, then the point guard, then each other player, one-on-one, none of them knowing what the others heard. "That's not a team. It's a collection of individuals." That is how the former CEO had run the staff, leaving each executive accountable for their own area. Kathryn's answer: "All of you, every one of you, are responsible for sales. Not just JR. All of you are responsible for marketing." The same went for product development, customer service and finance. When the top priority was closing deals, every executive attended sales training, because "we should all consider ourselves to be salespeople."
- **Lifeboats on the Titanic.** One member, defending his budget, caught himself: he didn't want the company's epitaph to blame bad technology. He named it as the fifth dysfunction, avoiding individual blame instead of helping the company win. A peer generalized it: each executive was guarding their own legacy (finance, customer support, brand), all scrambling for lifeboats on the *Titanic*, or at least standing as close to them as possible just in case.
- **Career or team.** To a peer pushing an acquisition mainly to give himself something to do: "You have to decide what is more important: helping the team win or advancing your career." They aren't mutually exclusive; "It's just that one has to be more important than the other."
- **The test of the collective ego.** A year later the former CEO, a founder and board member, asked to report to the COO instead of the CEO because that made more sense for the company and the team. The team challenged Kathryn on whether she had considered the hit to his status, and it turned out to be his own idea. When a rival offered to buy the company for more than it was worth, the board left the decision to the team as a test of whether they had "the fire in our bellies." They voted it down.
