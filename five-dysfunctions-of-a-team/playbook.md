# Kathryn's methods: leading the rebuild

How Kathryn Petersen, a new CEO hired into a gifted, well-funded and badly dysfunctional executive staff, turned it into a team. Its symptoms when she arrived were deadlines slipping, key employees leaving, morale sinking, backstabbing raised to an art, and a reputation as a political place to work. It had more talent, cash, technology and board connections than its competitors and was still behind two of them. Employees called the executives "The Staff"; no one called them a team.

## Before the first session

- **Observe first.** For her first two weeks she did "almost nothing": she walked the halls, talked with staff, interviewed each direct report, and silently watched as many meetings as she could. She asked her predecessor to keep running the weekly staff meetings while she listened and took notes. The only action she took was announcing a series of two-day off-sites over the coming months, and she refused suggested topics because she had set her own agenda.
- **Get the boss's backing for the pain.** When the Chairman worried she was setting fires before building bridges, she told him everything she was doing was "purposeful and intentional." She added, "if you knew how to do what I am trying to do, you wouldn't need me," and asked him, "Are you prepared for the consequences of letting me do this right?" It would not be easy or pretty for anyone, him included. **The rebreak**: a fractured team is like a broken arm or leg. Fixing it always hurts, and sometimes it has to be rebroken to heal correctly, "And the rebreak hurts a lot more than the initial break, because you have to do it on purpose."
- **Handle moments of truth face-to-face.** When a member announced by e-mail to the whole staff that he would skip the first day and a half of the off-site for a customer meeting, she skipped the e-mail reply and went to his office. The customer meeting was good news and would be rescheduled: "I only have one priority at this point: we need to get our act together as a team, or we're not going to be selling anything." She offered to help rearrange it and left. When a colleague then lobbied on his behalf, she welcomed the disagreement ("I'm fine with your disagreeing with me, especially when you tell me face-to-face"), credited his care for the company, and held the line: "from a team standpoint, we are completely broken."
- **Pick the setting.** Close enough to avoid costly travel, far enough to feel out of town, one comfortable room, and a series of two-day sessions over the coming months.

## The speech

Open every session with the same short speech, and say so: "this won't be the last time I say them." Its content: we have more talent, more cash, better technology and a stronger board than our competitors, yet we are behind two of them, because "We are not functioning as a team." The only reason we are here is to achieve results (revenue growth, profitability, customer retention and satisfaction), and none of that happens unless we fix what stops us acting as a team. Point at no one. Warn honestly that some may find the new company isn't where they want to be. It "isn't a threat or a dramatic device," just a realistic probability, and everyone is "eminently employable." Promise it is all about making the company succeed: no trust falls, and no "holding hands, singing songs, or getting naked." When a member later asked her to stop giving the speech: "I'll stop making this speech when it's no longer true."

## The first off-site, in order

1. The speech, then the triangle drawn on the whiteboard with its five empty sections, filled in one dysfunction at a time. "None of this is rocket science... The trick is putting it into practice."
2. Absence of trust, written at the base. Kathryn answered challenges with her evidence (the lack of debate) and let the silence sit until members named the problem themselves.
3. Ground rules: "be present and participate." She raised them the moment one member started typing, with a sincere "Are you working on something?" Then she set the rules for the whole group rather than reprimanding him.
4. Personal histories, then personality profiles completed in advance and worked through for hours, mixing in operational issues as they came up ("It gives us a chance to see how we put this into action"). Repeat the previous day's material the next morning despite the groans, because "this stuff won't stick unless we understand how to apply it completely."
5. The strength and weakness exercise, with the leader going first ([trust.md](trust.md)).
6. Jump to the peak, inattention to results, and build the scoreboard ([results.md](results.md)).
7. When a member demanded the rest of the model at once, she took it as "honest resistance," exactly what provokes real change, and revealed the other three right away ([conflict.md](conflict.md), [commitment.md](commitment.md), [accountability.md](accountability.md)).
8. Meetings and movies, then the team's first real decision meeting: the overarching goal and its number ([commitment.md](commitment.md)).
9. Close by asking for remaining concerns. When members suggested dropping the remaining off-sites, she waited for a peer to argue for them, then restated the speech and announced her intolerance of dysfunctional behavior for the next two weeks ([accountability.md](accountability.md)).

**Responding to a challenge to her diagnosis.** When she called the group one of the most political she'd seen, the team united against her. She let them "empty their chambers first." Then she gave a partial apology, taking care not to follow it with "but," and conceded her outsider's reference point might be off. She didn't back down: "I would rather overstate the problem than understate it. But only for the good of the team." Asked what she meant by politics, she gave the definition, and the loudest skeptic conceded the point. When a member proposed that the fix was more "heavy hitters," she asked whether they knew the track records of the people running their competitors: those teams had no heavy hitters and were ahead anyway.

## Later sessions

- **Second off-site.** Open with the speech. Ask, without interrogating, what members told their people about the first session; a staff member had leaked the details. Talking to their teams wasn't the problem, and she owned that she should have been explicit about the need to do so. Use the answers to raise the first team ([results.md](results.md)). Then ask "How are we doing?" and refocus on "But how are we working as a team?" Mine the biggest buried issue and work it to a decision. Spend the afternoon reviewing real goals for accountability rather than running exercises.
- **Third off-site.** Redraw the triangle and have the team assess itself level by level. When they agree too easily, let some air out of the balloon: "there will be many days during the next few months when you will wonder if you've made any progress at all." Real impact on the bottom line takes more than a few weeks of behavioral change, and "I've seen plenty of groups slide backward that were a lot further along than we are." What it takes is discipline and persistence.
- **Signs the team is getting there.** Members rarely discuss teamwork directly and just work the business. They stay together during breaks instead of scattering, they are noisier, and they laugh more. They leave eager to schedule follow-up meetings with one another. No one questions whether they should be doing what the leader asks, and there is a shared sense of purpose. Later still, a new member sees passionate debates end "with crystal-clear agreements and no sense of lingering bitterness," and peers call each other on the carpet and steer the discussion back to results.

## Setbacks are the process

- Progress comes in ebbs and flows. "Two years of behavioral reinforcement around politics is a tough thing to break, and one lecture, no matter how compelling, is not going to do it." Things will probably get worse, maybe much worse, before they get better.
- After an off-site, expect the team to retreat into guardedness and act as if it never happened. Dive right back in.
- Use every available hour rather than ending early on a high; the leader needs progress fast enough that the boss or board won't cut the effort short.
- When a business problem comes back into the room, people revert to the behaviors that caused it. Expect it and be ready.
- "Building a team is hard." Kathryn preferred doubt about whether the team *could* do it to doubt about whether it was important.

## Hard conversations with one member

**Public or private.** When a member attacked her in front of others, she first asked, "would you rather that we have this conversation right here, or one-on-one?"

**The member acting from a personal agenda** (pushing an acquisition, disparaging an absent colleague, telling the CEO she didn't "know squat about our business"). One-on-one, calm and relaxed:
- Name the rule and require repair: never slam an absent teammate; take issues to them or to the leader; "You're going to have to make that right."
- Find the motive: "So this is about you?" Then sit and listen until the real story comes out, in his case feeling underused after moving his family across the country.
- Make it about the team: "Are you making this team better, or are you contributing to the dysfunction?" Affirm the person while doing it ("You clearly have a lot to offer"). Venting is allowed from time to time.
- Point him toward the team: tell the group what you just told me.
- When he threatened to quit: "It's not about what I want. It's about you. You have to decide what is more important: helping the team win or advancing your career."

He came to the next staff meeting, apologized publicly, admitted his motive, asked the team for help, and stayed. When the head of sales turned out to have quit that same day, the team weighed who should take over (a volunteer with less time to spare, the member with the most sales experience who had wanted to escape being labeled a field guy) and gave it to him.

**The member who has to go.** The marketing head was talented and produced good work. She also rolled her eyes, disparaged peers, gave only shallow disclosure, defended her department while the company failed, produced work without the peers it affected, and dismissed a request that everyone attend sales training. Kathryn became certain when the member answered a peer's sincere offer to hear her objection with sarcasm, then brushed off his offer to attend her own meeting in return.
- Open plainly: "This is going to be a tough conversation." Go "right to the bone": "I don't think you are a fit for this team. And I don't think you really want to be here."
- Give the basis: she doesn't seem to respect her colleagues, won't open up to them, and has a distracting and demotivating effect in meetings. When asked for specifics, give concrete behaviors: the eye-rolling, the insult to a peer, the indifference to the company's top priority.
- When threatened with the board's reaction: "my job is not to avoid confrontation with the board. My job is to build an executive team that can make this company work."
- Be truthful and kind about her future: she might find a company that values her style, but not easily "if you don't take a look at yourself."
- Offer the real choice: "I'm not firing you. And you don't have to leave... But your behavior would have to change completely. And it would have to change fast."
- Hold the silence, nearly a full minute, so she hears the emptiness of her own arguments. Grant reasonable exit terms without showing relief.

**Can people like that change?** Kathryn's answer: "No. For every kid like that one, there are ten who never made it." The leader's job is the best team possible.

**After a departure.**
- Tell the team directly and confidentially: she wasn't willing to change her behavior, it was hurting the team, so she was asked to leave. Name the interim plan (strong people step up, start a search). Tell employees and the press simply that she is moving on, and don't be intimidated by initial reactions.
- Expect mourning. Even the most difficult person's departure provokes "some degree of mourning and self-doubt among their peers." Enter the danger and address "the elephant" as a team before explaining it to the company: ask how everyone feels. Expect worry about losing another member, second thoughts that the person's work was good enough to tolerate them, and fear of being next.
- **Tell the Fred story** (or one like it from your own career). In her first management job, Kathryn's best analyst, Fred, outproduced everyone, helped no one, and flaunted it. She ignored the complaints because she assumed people resented his skill and wouldn't come down on her top performer. As output slid she gave him more work, then promoted him. Within two weeks three of her seven analysts quit, and her own manager let her go. After Fred left, performance improved dramatically within a month, with three fewer analysts. The lesson: the damage came not from Fred's behavior but from "My tolerance of his behavior. Listen, they fired the right person." Then: "I don't plan on losing any of you. And that's why I did what I did."
- After the all-hands, the news hit employees harder than expected, more for its symbolism than for the person. Let the team debate the replacement, decide it, and have everyone interview candidates. The hiring bar is the model itself: someone who can "demonstrate trust, engage in conflict, commit to group decisions, hold their peers accountable, and focus on the results of the team, not their own ego."

## Composition and onboarding

- **Precursors.** The Five Dysfunctions are not quite enough on their own. Two things make overcoming them easier: people who are *capable* of being team players (Lencioni's *The Ideal Team Player*), and people in roles that use their natural talents (*The Six Types of Working Genius*). After both, it still "comes down to real people having the courage to sit down with one another and accepting the discomfort that is necessary to improve."
- **Size at the top.** "The larger the company, the smaller the team should be at the top." When her staff grew to eight direct reports, nine people at the table, fluid and substantive discussion got hard. She cut it to five, explaining each change delicately but confidently to the person involved.
- **Onboarding a new member.** The team taught the new marketing head the model themselves, each member explaining one dysfunction. Then they reviewed his profile, his peers' roles and responsibilities, and the collective goals, and brought him straight into real debate.

## Meeting cadence

A strong team spends considerable time together, and saves time by doing so, through less confusion and less redundant effort and communication. Kathryn's team spent about eight days a quarter in scheduled meetings, fewer than three a month, an amount most management teams balk at because they would rather do "real work."

| Meeting | Length and place | Topics might include |
|---|---|---|
| Annual planning meeting and leadership development retreats | Three days, off-site | Budget discussions, major strategic planning overview, leadership training, succession planning, cascading messaging |
| Quarterly staff meetings | Two days, off-site | Major goal reviews, financial review, strategic discussions, employee performance discussions, key issue resolution, team development, cascading messages |
| Weekly staff meetings | Two hours, on-site | Key activity review, goal progress review, sales review, customer review, tactical issue resolution, cascading messages |
| Ad hoc topical meetings | Two hours, on-site | Strategic issues that cannot be adequately discussed during weekly staff meetings |

## The team at its best

Emergency service teams show all five levels working together. Their members live and work together and build "bonds of trust that only families can rival." They hold focused, unfiltered debate when every second is precious. They commit quickly to unambiguous decisions under danger, while most people would demand more information. They push colleagues to carry their loads, knowing that one slacker could cost lives. And they have only one end in mind: protecting the lives and liberties of others.
