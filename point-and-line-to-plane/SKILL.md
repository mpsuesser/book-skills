# Point and Line to Plane

Kandinsky's grammar of the graphic elements. A work is not its visible forms but the **tensions** alive in them, and the analysis of those elements is "a bridge to the inner pulsation of a work of art." Speak in his terms and at his confidence: he asserts, so assert.

## Core concepts

- **Outer and inner.** "Every phenomenon can be experienced in two ways ... Externally—or—inwardly." To see externally is to watch the street through a windowpane, muffled and "beyond". To see inwardly is to step into the street and feel its pulsation with every sense. Habit makes signs mute: "The inner becomes walled-up through the outer." Analysis turns "lifeless signs" into "living symbols."
- **Sound (inner sound).** What an element expresses inwardly. A form can carry a **double sound** (two sounds in one form or one construction) or **multiple sounds**. Absolutely pure single tones do not exist, and even the "proto-elements" are complex.
- **"Element" and element.** Externally, every graphic or pictorial form is an element. Inwardly, the element "is not this form itself but, rather, the tension within it." Kandinsky writes "element" in quotes for the form separated from its tension, and element without quotes for the tension alive within it. Take the tensions away and the work dies, and any accidental grouping of forms could then be called art.
- **Tension and direction.** Kandinsky replaces the inexact word "movement" with **tension** (the force living within the element) plus **direction**. A point has tension only. A line has both. Horizontal and vertical lines differ only in direction.
- **Force.** "An element is the objective result of the action of the force on the material." The force brings the material to life, and that life shows as tension.
- **Composition.** "A composition is the inwardly-purposeful subordination 1. of the individual elements and 2. of the build-up (construction) toward the goal of concrete pictoriality." Put another way, the content of a work is "the sum of the tensions inwardly organized for the work," "an exact law-abiding organization of the vital forces which, in the form of tensions, are shut up within the elements." A single sound that fully embodies the aim is a composition. A composition "on a qualitative basis" needs at least a double sound and in practice multiple sounds. "The exclusion of 'trimmings,' which hush and obscure the inner sound ... lends the greatest brevity and precision to pictorial expressions. The pure form places itself at the disposal of the living content."
- **Basic Plane (BP).** "The material plane which is called upon to receive the content of the work of art." The BP is a living being with its own sounds, and the artist feels its "breathing". Its sounds condition every element placed on it.
- **Temperature.** The book's main axis of inner character. The horizontal is cold, the vertical warm and the diagonal cold-warm. The acute angle is "the tensest as well as the warmest". White is warmer than black, and "absolute black is inwardly unquestionably cold."
- **Lyric and dramatic.** Cold, single-force and central placements are **lyric**. Collision of forces, eccentric placement and placement near the border are **dramatic**. Straight lines are entirely lyric, because a single force makes them. The dramatic needs at least two forces.
- **Parallel and contrast.** The two principles of combination are "setting side-by-side or setting opposite." Order means "not only the mathematical 'harmonious structure' ... but also structure in accordance with the principle of contrast." Contrasts, even "of an inharmonious character," properly used raise the total harmony.
- **Approaching-of-the-boundary.** The zone where point turns into plane, or line into plane, has no fixed edge ("Where does the river stop and the sea begin?"). Working at that boundary veils the absolute sound of a form and gives "a dual note in a single form." It is a means, and in the end an element, of composition. It loosens a composition whose main elements are acutely dry. Pushed too far it becomes "repulsive over-niceties". Here feeling alone decides.
- **Intuition and calculation.** The two heads of creative power. "In the long run neither the one nor the other alone suffices." Measurement and **numerical expression** belong to the future. A formula that lags behind perception is "glue," "flypaper," "an overstuffed chair," and even formulae become obsolete.

## The three elements at a glance

| Element | Origin | Inner nature | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Point** | "the initial collision of the tool with the material plane" | "Point—rest." Concentric tension, no direction. "The innermost concise form," "the briefest, constant, innermost assertion." Silence. | "temporally the briefest form" |
| **Line** | "the track made by the moving point," created by a force from without that destroys the point's repose | "Line—inwardly animated tension created by movement." The leap "out of the static into the dynamic," the greatest antithesis to the point. | "length is a concept of time." Curves take longer to follow than straight lines of equal length. |
| **Basic plane** | bounded by 2 horizontals (cold rest) and 2 verticals (warm rest) | tranquil-objective sound. Each side and each quarter has its own voice. | — |

Line types by the forces that make them: one force gives the **straight line**. Two forces acting alternately give the **angular line**. Two forces acting simultaneously, with one constantly exceeding the other, give the **curved line**. Combining these gives the **combined line**. The **primary contrasting pairs**: straight line / curved line, triangle / circle, yellow / blue.

## Method: analyse, then build

Kandinsky's research ideal has three parts: (1) each phenomenon **in isolation**, (2) the reciprocal effect of phenomena **in combinations**, (3) general conclusions. Work "meticulously exact and pedantically precise": "not the smallest alteration in the nature, in the characteristics, in the effects of the individual elements should escape the watchful eye." Apply this to any composition, whether you are reading one or making one.

1. **Read the BP first.** Find its format. A square is the most objective format, cold and warm balanced. A horizontal format is colder, a vertical one warmer. Every element placed on it is brought "into a colder or warmer atmosphere" that cannot later be fully removed. Mark the center, the four sides and the four quarters.
2. **Isolate each element and name its sound.**
   - *Point*: size relative to the plane and to the other forms (a dot beside a very thin line reads as a plane), shape (round, angular, jagged, free), texture.
   - *Line*: type (straight, angular, curved, combined), direction and temperature, angle size, emphasis (thickening or thinning along its length), outer edges (smooth, jagged, torn, rounded), and whether it lies free or touches the border.
   - *Plane*: triangle, square or circle family.
3. **Place it on the BP.** Is it centric or eccentric? Which side and quarter does its tension head toward? How close is it to the border? Does it run with the inner tensions of the BP (**inner parallel**), merely parallel to the edges (**external parallel**), or against them (**contrast**)? Use the BP rules below.
4. **Inventory the combination.** Count sounds the way Kandinsky does with two points on a plane: (1) the inner sound of a point, (2) the repetition of that sound, (3) the double sound of the first point (the point plus its place), (4) the double sound of the second, (5) the sound of the sum. The schematic horizontal-vertical cross in a square already gives 12 sounds: 6 elements of cold rest and 6 of warm rest. Check what repetition adds, what parallel and contrast add, and how the complexes subordinate to larger complexes and finally to the whole.
5. **Judge against the definition of composition.** Is every element, technique and texture inwardly-purposefully subordinated to the aim? Is the result lyric or dramatic, balanced or dramatized, and is that what the work wants?
6. **To build**, run the same levers forward. Choose the format for the temperature and restraint you want. Then choose centric placement (lyric, rest) or eccentric placement (relative, double sound). Then choose parallel or contrast with the BP's tensions, distance from the border, repetition and its intervals, and emphasis along each line.

Two checks for any placement:

- **Mirror and upside-down.** View the work in a mirror to test right and left, and turn it upside-down to test above and below. "The 'reflection' and the 'upside-down' are still rather mysterious facts which are of great importance to the theory of composition."
- **First impression.** "Rely upon the first impression because the power of perception tires rapidly and lets the imagination run rife."

## Basic-plane rules every placement needs

Left and right are the viewer's left and right, because the BP mirrors the artist facing it.

| Side | Character | Tension / "literary" |
|---|---|---|
| **Above** | looseness, lightness, emancipation, freedom. Small areas disintegrate. "Every weightier form thereby grows heavier." Climbing and falling intensify. | toward heaven |
| **Left** | the same as above but less so: loose, light, free. Increases upward from the center. | "into the distance": going out, adventure. Forms tensed leftward gain intensity and speed. |
| **Right** | continuation of below, weakened. Resistance "greater, compacter and harder" than at the left. | "toward home": fatigue, rest. Forms tensed rightward grow languid and weak. |
| **Below** | condensation, heaviness, constraint. Forms lose weight. Climbing grates with friction. Restraint is at its maximum. | toward the earth |

- **Quarters** (a upper-left, b upper-right, c lower-left, d lower-right). Quarter a, tensed toward top and left, is the loosest. Quarter d, toward right and bottom, has the greatest resistance, so a and d are in greatest contrast. Quarters b and c are in moderate contrast. Weight runs from a (lightest) through b and c to d (heaviest).
- **Diagonals.** The **cb** diagonal (lower-left to upper-right) is "harmonious", a "lyric" tension. The **ad** diagonal (upper-left to lower-right) is "disharmonious", a "dramatic" tension: weight bears down at d and the line is deflected upward at a.
- **Dramatization or balance.** Heavier forms below and lighter above dramatize, raising tension both ways (schema 4:8). Heavier forms above and lighter below balance (6:6). Here "weight" means inner tension, not material weight.
- **Border.** "On approaching the boundary of the BP, a form increases in tension until, at the moment of contact with the boundary, the tension suddenly ceases." Forms near the border make a construction more **dramatic**. Forms gathered about the center make it more **lyric**. A line that touches the upper edge loses its upward tension and becomes "sickly, almost despairing."
- **Format.** "The slightest deviation of the diagonal ... from the vertical or from the horizontal is decisive." "Through an unskillful choice of the plane format, a well-conceived order can result in repulsive disorder."

## Failure modes Kandinsky names

- **Mannerism.** Texture or technique becomes an end in itself: "the means drown out the end. The external has taken over the inner." Texture "must serve the idea residing in the composition."
- **Imitating one medium in another.** Making a woodcut look like a pen drawing or a lithograph like an etching is one of the "testimonials of spiritual poverty". So is the cock's crow imitated on a violin.
- **Stifling the element with representation.** A print built of points meant to read as lines (a famous "Head of Christ") condemns the point to "a poverty-stricken half-life." In abstract art the element "attains its full, unveiled sound."
- **Fear of "dissection".** This fear comes from ignorance. Analysis does not kill art.
- **Emotion alone, or formula alone.** "The emotional alone ... could easily lead off the track; this can only be avoided with the help of exact analytic work." A formula can in turn inhibit perception.
- **Literal reading of the BP's associations.** Heaven, distance, home and earth "serve the purpose of representing the inner tensions of the BP analytically" and cannot determine the compositional idea.
- **Separating what belongs together.** Painting and graphics differ in no elementary way, so line belongs in painting as fully as in graphics. Art and nature share basic laws but differ in material, so treat them separately.
