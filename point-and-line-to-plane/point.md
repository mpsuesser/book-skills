# Point

The proto-element of painting, and especially of the "graphic."

## From sign to element

- **The geometric point** is invisible and incorporeal: "Considered in terms of substance, it equals zero." Yet it is "the ultimate and most singular union of silence and speech". It is the greatest brevity, a restraint that still speaks.
- **In writing** the point is practical-useful. It is interruption, non-existence (the negative element), and a bridge to the next existence (the positive element). Habit makes its sound mute.
- **Release.** To hear its inner sound, tear the point out of its practical-useful setting, step by step:
  1. Put it in an illogical position. "Today I am going. To the movies" can still pass as emphasis or fanfare. "Today I. Am going to the movies" is the illogical in pure form: it reads as a typo, and the inner value flashes and dies.
  2. Move it so far that it loses connection with the sentence. It now needs "considerable open space around it, in order that its sound may have resonance," and is still overpowered by the print.
  3. Enlarge the point and the space around it. The print's sound fades and the point's grows, giving a "double sound—print-point" of two worlds that "can never meet." Then the point leaps into the world of painting, and its subordination becomes inner-purposeful.
- **In painting** the point is "the result of the initial collision of the tool with the material plane." Planes include paper, wood, canvas, stucco and metal. Tools include pencil, burin, brush, pen and etching-point. "The basic plane is impregnated by this first collision."

## Size, shape, boundary

- Abstractly the point is "ideally small, ideally round". In actuality it is "an ideally small circle." Materially it can take unlimited shapes. It turns pointed and tends toward the triangle, or, "prompted by an urge for relative immobility," the square. It can become jagged with projections of varied size, or entirely free. "The realm of points is unlimited." Shape and size give only "a relative innermost colouration" of a basic nature that "yet rings its pure tone."
- Whether a form is still a point depends on two relations: (1) its size relative to the plane, and (2) its size relative to the other forms on the plane. A blot that reads as a point on an empty plane "must be termed a plane" beside a very thin line. For now this is tested "on the basis of feeling only."
- **At the boundary.** Push the point toward its limit, and slightly over it, until the point begins to disappear and the plane begins "its embryonic existence". This is a deliberate means. It veils the absolute sound and brings dissolution, imprecision, instability, flickering and tension. It risks inner overlapping, where the sounds of point and plane "collide with each other, fuse, and then recoil". The result is "a double sound by a single form."

## Inner nature

- "The point is the innermost concise form. It is turned inwards," even when its outer shape is angular.
- Its tension is **concentric**. Where it shows eccentric tendencies, a double resonance of concentric and eccentric arises.
- It is "a small world cut off more or less equally from all sides." It barely fuses with its surroundings, and not at all when perfectly round. It does not move horizontally or vertically, and it neither advances nor recedes. Its concentric tension relates it to the circle, and its other traits point to the square.
- "The point digs itself into the plane and asserts itself for all time": "short, fixed and quickly created."
- **Time.** Perception time is at its minimum and the time element is almost eliminated, which in some compositions makes the point inevitable. It is the kettle-drum blow, the triangle strike, "the short taps of the woodpecker."
- **Force from within** (a point growing from its own center) only relatively lowers its concentric tension. **Force from without** tears the point loose and pushes it across the surface. The concentric tension is destroyed and the line is born.

## Point on the basic plane

- **Prototype of pictorial expression.** A single point at the center of a square BP. The double sound of point and plane becomes nearly a single sound, because the plane's voice is too slight to notice. It is "the last stage in the progressive dissolution of multiple and double sounds," and theoretically a whole work. Judged externally, compositions differ by number. Judged qualitatively, at least a double sound is needed.
- **Eccentric structure.** Move the point off center and a double sound appears: (1) the absolute sound of the point, and (2) the sound of its location on the BP. The point's sound changes "from the absolute to the relative."
- **Quantitative increase.** "Repetition is a potent means of heightening the inner vibration and is, at the same time, a source of elementary rhythm," which in turn leads to elementary harmony in every art. Two points on a plane give the five-part inventory: inner sound, repetition, the double sound of each point, and the sum. Points that keep accumulating, and that grow ever more unequal in size and form, raise "a storm of sounds."
- In a circle, "the point centered in the circle represents the most complete form of rest of the no longer isolated point." Only the isolated point offers complete rest.

## Abstract and "objective" use

In "objective" art the element's own sound is "veiled, thrust back". In abstract art it is unveiled. Prints made wholly of points meant to read as lines (such as a famous "Head of Christ") are an unjustifiable use: the point is "stifled by the representation." The screen dots of zincography are a separate case. That division of the surface is a technical necessity, and the point is deliberately suppressed there. In abstract work a technique may be chosen because the composition needs it.

## The graphic techniques

The three typical techniques differ most clearly in how they make the point, and the same differences carry to the line.

| | Etching (esp. dry-point) | Woodcut | Lithography |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easy | the smallest black point | the smallest white point ("one stab") | everything, equally |
| Hard | a large white point | a large black point | — |
| Correction | impossible | restricted | unlimited |
| How the point is made | first "in the negative through a short, precise prick". The needle is cold metal, the plate warm copper. Ink is wiped so the point lies "in the lap of brightness." Heavy pressure: "the plate eats its way into the paper," complete fusion. | the point is encircled "like a fortress—with a ditch". Violence is done to its surroundings. Light pressure. | stone of "an indefinable yellow—warm". Pen, crayon, brush or atomizer. The point is there "with the speed of lightning". The colour rests loosely and grinds off. Fleeting pressure. |
| Where the point sits | **in** the paper | **in and on** the paper | **on** the paper ("it would not be surprising if it were to fly off") |
| Line | most easily made, deeply embedded (esp. with acid). The very thin line is "an 'absolute' sound among lines." Specifically black and white. | careful, difficult work | lies lightly on the surface |
| Element it favours | point and line | plane, as the easiest result | point, line and plane, in black-and-white or colour |
| Social character | aristocratic: few good prints, each unique | more abundant and uniform, hard to use for colour | democratic: almost limitless prints, colour, "a certain substitute for the picture" |

Dry-point suits the present "atmosphere of haste" and has incisive precision. Lithography came last and suits the "spirit of our time". It is speed with an almost indestructible block, the greatest economy, and limitless correction, so it serves work done "without an exact previously formed plan (e.g., in experiments)." Interest revived in the order woodcut (during the "art of the plane"), then etching (the search for the thinnest line), then lithography. Every technical difference "must point to the profound depth, that is, to the inner life of things."

## Texture

"'Texture' signifies the manner in which the elements are externally combined with each other and with the basic plane." It depends on three factors:

1. the character of the given space: smooth, rough, flat, plastic;
2. the type of tool: the brush or whatever replaces it;
3. the manner of application: loose, compact, stippled or sprayed, which depends on consistency, binding media and pigments.

For a point, consider:

1. its character from the tool together with the receiving surface (the plate);
2. its union with the surface that finally receives it (the paper);
3. the qualities of that surface: smooth, granular, striated, rough.

A pile of points adds one more question: was it made by hand or mechanically (spray)? Kandinsky's examples are a concentric complex of free-form points, and a large point built of small points by spraying. "Texture is a means to an end ... it must serve the idea residing in the composition." When it becomes an end in itself, the result is mannerism.

## Point beyond painting

- **Nature.** Accumulations of points in nature are "invariably purposeful and organically necessary". Examples are seeds, the poppy head (itself "a somewhat large spherical point") full of blue-grey seed points ordered by physical law, a star cluster, and a nitrate-forming nodule. The desert is a sea of "dead" points whose violent shifting terrifies. "In nature, the point is a self-contained thing, full of possibilities."
