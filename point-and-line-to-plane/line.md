# Line

"The track made by the moving point." It is born when a force from without destroys the point's repose: "the leap out of the static into the dynamic." Strictly speaking it is a secondary element. Its variety depends on the number of forces acting and on how they combine.

| Forces on the point | Product |
|---|---|
| I A. one force, direction unchanged | straight line |
| I B. two forces, alternately (one thrust, or repeated) | angular line (simple, then many-angled) |
| II. two forces at once, one constantly exceeding the other by the same degree | curved line (simple, then wave-like) |
| III. combination of the above | combined line |

## Straight lines

The straight line's tension is "the most concise form of the potentiality for endless movement." There are three typical kinds, and every other straight line is a variation.

1. **Horizontal**: the ground a human stands and moves on, "a cold supporting base". "Coldness and flatness are the basic sounds." It is endless **cold** movement.
2. **Vertical**: height replaces flatness and warmth replaces coldness. It is endless **warm** movement.
3. **Diagonal**: equally inclined to both, an "equal union of coldness and warmth." It is endless **cold-warm** movement.
4. **Free straight lines** (unbalanced lines) deviate from the diagonal and "can never attain a balance between warmth and coldness". Their greater or lesser lean to cold or warm sets their sound. They are either **centric** (sharing a center) or **acentric**.

- A dense **star** of straight lines through one meeting-point grows a point at its center. The lines flow into one another and a plane is born, the circle. This is one way the line creates a plane, as a shovel's cuts create one in earth.
- **Acentric free lines** relate loosely to the plane. They seem to pierce it occasionally, sharing the advancing and retreating tensions of yellow and blue. They are farthest from the point, which "claws itself into the plane." On a bounded plane this works only when the line lies free and does not touch the borders.
- **Horizontals and verticals are silent lines**, as black and white are silent colours: "scarcely audible whispering and stillness." Placed centrally they cannot be repeated, so they are solitary. Their **proto-sound** "can never be completely drowned out."
- **Prototype of linear expression.** A horizontal and a vertical crossing at the center of a square divide it into four squares, "the most primitive form of the division of a schematic plane." The sum of its tensions is 6 elements of cold rest + 6 of warm rest = 12. That is, 4 plane sounds + 2 line sounds = 6, doubled by the combination.
- **Lyric to dramatic.** From horizontal to free acentric lines, the cold lyric warms into a dramatic flavor, but "the entire field of straight lines is lyric" because one force makes them. The dramatic carries "the sound of collision," which needs at least two forces. Simultaneous forces are "hotter" than alternating ones. "The realm of lines embraces all the expressive sounds from the cold lyric in the beginning, to the hot dramatic at the end."

## Angular lines (I B)

- **Simple angular lines** have two parts. They are "in much closer touch with the plane" and carry something plane-like, the plane in creation, so the angular line is a bridge. There are three typical angles: **acute 45°, right 90°, obtuse 135°**, plus the **free angle** (free angular line). The right angle cannot change size, only direction. Four right angles touching at their points make a cross. Touching along their sides they make right-angled planes, usually the square. The right angle is cold-warm or warm-cold depending on its direction.
- The **lengths** of the segments greatly modify the sound.
- **Absolute sound** of an angular line comes from three components. They can sound as a **triple sound**, singly, or in pairs, and none is ever wholly silent:
  1. the sound of its straight lines;
  2. its inclination to a more or less acute tension;
  3. its inclination to a smaller or greater "conquest of the plane."
- **Right**: "the most objective," the coldest. It divides the square into exactly 4 parts. **Acute**: "the tensest as well as the warmest." It divides the plane into exactly 8. **Obtuse**: forward tension weakens and the greed for the plane grows, but it cannot divide the plane exactly. It goes in twice and leaves 90° unconquered.
- **Three sounds = the artistic process.**
  - Acute is "sharp and highly active": the inner thought (vision).
  - Right is "cold and controlled": masterly execution (realization).
  - Obtuse is "clumsy, weak and passive": "the unsatisfied feeling and the sensation of one's own weakness following the completed work" (the artist's "hangover").
- **Many-angled lines (I B 2)** come from several pushes of two alternating forces. Their schematic type has equal segments at right angles. They vary by mixing acute, right, obtuse and free angles, and by segment lengths. Zig-zags with equal segments are "an animated straight line". Acute zig-zags suggest height and the vertical, obtuse zig-zags the horizontal. An obtuse angle opened by a regularly growing force caves in toward "the profoundest self-absorption of the circle." More alternating forces, more varied directions and lengths give ever more complex planes.

### Angles, forms, colours

The acuter the angle, the sharper its warmth. Warmth falls toward the red right angle and turns cold toward the obtuse. Kandinsky's scale, in 30° steps:

| Angle | Colour |
|---|---|
| 30° (acute) | yellow |
| 60° | orange |
| 90° (right) | red |
| 120° (obtuse) | violet |
| 150° | blue: "a typical blue angle," a presentiment of the curve and circle |
| 180° (the horizontal) | black |

Kandinsky also singles out the **60° angle**. Two of them, opening to each other, make an equilateral triangle, "three sharp, active angles", which is the sign-post to yellow. "The acute angle has a yellow colour within."

| Angular line | Primary form | Primary colour |
|---|---|---|
| acute | triangle | yellow |
| right | square | red ("Not without reason has the red square appeared so often") |
| obtuse | circle | blue |

| Straight line | Colour (inner parallel, not identity) | By temperature and light |
|---|---|---|
| horizontal | black | = blue |
| vertical | white | = yellow |
| diagonal | red (or grey, or green) | = red |
| free straight line | yellow and blue | |

Red lies firmly on the plane like the diagonal, unlike yellow and blue, and seethes inwardly unlike black and white. White is warmer than black. The horizontal colour scale runs white, yellow, red, blue, black, and the same order stacked vertically reads as "a gradual, natural sliding-downward from above to below." Black is the symbol of death and white of birth, as the horizontal (lying, supporting, passive) relates to the vertical (standing, climbing, growing, active).

**A plane's components sum to the missing primary.**

| Plane | Components | Sum |
|---|---|---|
| triangle | horizontal (black = blue) + diagonal (red) | yellow |
| square | horizontal (black = blue) + vertical (white = yellow) | red |
| circle | active tension (yellow) + passive tension (red) | blue |

"The sum would supply the missing factor necessary to balance": lines can be derived from the plane, and the plane from lines. Practice confirms it. A black-and-white work of lines and points gains balance by adding a plane, because "lighter weights require the heavier", and colour painting needs this even more. Kandinsky writes these links with ⇄ (as chemistry does for relations short of identity), and "the truth is not infrequently reached by way of error."

## Curved lines (II)

- A curve is "a straight line which has been brought out of its course by constant sideward pressure". The more pressure, the more outward tension, and finally "the tendency to close itself."
- A straight line has two primitive tensions, outward at its two ends. A curve's chief tension sits in the arc, a **third tension** that opposes and out-sounds the other two. The angle's piercing quality is gone, but greater force is confined here, with greater endurance. "Something thoughtlessly youthful exists in the angle while in the arc is a mature energy."
- **Straight and curved are the primary contrasting pair of lines.** The angular line is the intermediate: birth, youth, maturity.
- The straight line "is a complete negation of the plane". The curve "carries within it a seed of the plane." Two forces rolling the point back to its start create the circle, "the most unstable and, at the same time, the most stable of planes." Straight lines need three impulses to close a plane, the triangle, whose beginning and end stay visible at three points. Hence **triangle / circle, the primary contrasting pair of planes.** The spiral is "a circle going off its track in a uniform manner". The spiral is a line, the circle a plane, a distinction geometry does not make but painting must.
- Planes made by curves always keep "circle tensions within them."

### Wave-like lines (II 2)

These are made of geometric parts of a circle, free parts, or combinations. Kandinsky's examples show how pressure, direction and accent produce sound:

- **Geometric**: equal radius, a uniform alternation of positive and negative pressure, a horizontal course of tension and release.
- **Free**: displacement destroys the geometric character. Positive pressure gains the upper hand irregularly.
- **Free, increased**: an "especially temperamental struggle between the two forces". Positive pressure drives very high.
- **Variation**: the high point turns left, giving way to the attack of negative pressure. The height is stressed by broadening the line (accentuation).
- **Free**: four waves subordinated to one direction, from lower left to upper right.
- **Geometric ascent**: radii from bottom to top of 4, 4, 4, 2, 1. The sudden weakening of the wave increases the vertical tension.

The results come from (1) the combination of active and passive pressures, (2) the sound of direction, and (3) **emphasis**, a gradual or sudden increase or decrease in strength. A geometric curve rising with uniformly decreasing thickness gains "heightened tension of ascent." A fourth factor is (4) the **outer edges**: smooth, jagged, torn or rounded, which call up touch sensations. They combine freely (smooth edges on a jagged line, torn edges on a rounded line), apply to straight, angular and curved lines, and each side can be treated differently.

## Combined line (III)

- **Geometric combined line**: all parts geometric.
- **Mixed combined line**: free and geometric parts together.
- **Free combined line**: all parts free.

Whatever the type, "the original source of every line remains the same—the force."

## Line and plane

A short line that spreads out behaves like a growing point: "When does the line as such die out, and at what moment is a plane born?" The answer depends on proportions. This **approaching-of-the-boundary** is a potent compositional means. It loosens acute dryness and becomes over-nice when exaggerated (see SKILL.md). No accepted distinction between line and plane exists yet.

## Complexes, repetition, rhythm

Kandinsky's elementary rhythm figures:

- repetition of a straight line with alternating weights;
- repetition of an angular line;
- opposed repetition of an angular line, forming a plane;
- repetition of a curved line;
- opposed repetition of a curved line, repeatedly forming planes;
- central-rhythmic repetition of a straight line, and of a curved line;
- repetition of an accented curve by an accompanying line;
- contrasting repetition of a curve.

Repetition of a straight line comes in three kinds:

1. **Equal intervals**, "the primitive rhythm". This is quantitative reinforcement, like many violins reinforcing one.
2. **Uniformly increasing intervals**. A qualitative element joins the quantitative, like repeating measures after a pause or repeating in "piano".
3. **Unequal intervals**. A more complex rhythm, the most intricate of the three.

Angular and curved lines allow far more complex combinations:

- **Curve against angular line**: "The characteristics of both acquire a strengthened sound."
- **Curves running along together, or running apart**: quantitative and qualitative intensification, but "something soft and velvet-like ... the lyric oversounds the dramatic."

Independent complexes subordinate to greater ones, as the solar system belongs to the cosmos. "The universal harmony of a composition can, therefore, consist of a number of complexes rising to the highest point of contrast."

## Time and number

- Line carries far more time than point: "length is a concept of time." A curve takes longer to follow than a straight line of equal length, and "the more animated the curved line becomes, the longer is the span of time it represents." Horizontals and verticals of equal length differ in the inner colouration of their time. Account for the time element in any linear composition.
- "Every force finds expression in number." Exact theory of composition awaits numerical expression. It will arrive first for a work's "basic plan" and larger complexes, and patience will break those into ever smaller groups. Simple proportions tempt now and complex ones will tempt later. In practice "law is subordinated here to purpose whereby the work of art attains the highest quality—genuineness."
- Each kind of line seeks its external means "on the general basis of economy: the minimum effort for the maximum result."
