# Translation: point and line beyond painting

"Every phenomenon of the external and of the inner world can be given linear expression—a kind of translation." This applies to Raphael, J. S. Bach, a storm, fear, a cosmic process or a toothache. "The only danger would be to remain bound to the external form and to neglect the content."

## Procedure for a linear translation

1. Determine the **lyric or dramatic content** of the phenomenon.
2. In the linear realm, find the form that fits it. Lyric content points to straight lines, one force, centric placement and cold or horizontal lines. Dramatic content points to colliding forces (angular, then curved and wave-like lines), eccentric placement and placement near the border.
3. Check against existing translations, such as musical "pictures" of nature or music written on paintings. Kandinsky cites Liszt's "Années de Pèlerinage" on Michelangelo's "Pensieroso" and Raphael's "Sposalizio". Prefer systematic, laboratory-style experiments to intuition alone.

The elements of the different arts are the same. "The differences first reveal themselves in the structure." The other arts' elements (spatial elements in sculpture and architecture, tones in music, movements in dance, words in poetry) need the same uncovering of their external and inner characteristics, their "sounds." The resulting tables may finally merge into one synthetic table.

## Music

- **Points**: the kettle-drum and triangle strike, all percussion, and above all the piano, which can build whole compositions from "tonal points." Kandinsky translated the opening of Beethoven's Fifth into points. Three equal dots are followed by one large dot trailing a dotted wedge for the held note, and the figure repeats lower. He also translated its second theme.
- **Lines**: most instruments are linear, and the organ is as typically "linear" as the piano is a "point" instrument. Pitch is the width of the line. Violin, flute and piccolo are very fine. Viola and clarinet are thicker. The deep instruments broaden down to the bass-viol and tuba, the broadest. Instrumental timbre gives the line its colour. Dynamics from pianissimo to fortissimo are the line's sharpness, its brilliance. "The pressure of the hand on the bow corresponds exactly to the pressure of the hand on the pencil." In music the line supplies the greatest means of expression.
- **Notation** is nothing but point and line. Time is shown by the colour of the point (white or black) and the number of pennant strokes, and pitch by five horizontals. Its brevity tempts painting and dance to seek their own "notes," but "there is ... only one way ... analytic separation into fundamental elements."
- **Repetition**: one violin reinforced by many is quantitative. Repeated measures after a pause, or a repeat in "piano", add the qualitative. The same pitch on other instruments is coloured-qualitative.

## Dance

Classical ballet has "points". Toe-running leaves a trace of points. The leap aims the head at a point above and lands on a point. The modern leap can form "a five-pointed plane" from head, two hands and two feet, with the ten fingers as ten smaller points (the dancer Palucca). Moments of rigid stillness are points, so there are active and passive point formations. In line, the modern dancer moves on exact lines (Sacharoff), and the whole body, "right down to his finger tips, is at every moment an uninterrupted composition of lines" (Palucca).

## Sculpture and architecture

- **Point**: the cross-section of several planes, "the termination of an angle in space" and the nucleus the planes grow from. Gothic building stresses points by plastic means. Chinese building leads curves to the point, so the form fades into the surrounding air. The masses divide into points leading to the highest peak: "Peak—point."
- **Line**: a structure in space is a linear construction. A research task is to trace "the fate of lines in architecture" across peoples and epochs, translate the works graphically, and relate the graphic formulae to the spiritual atmosphere of each time. Present building restricts itself to horizontal and vertical, with projecting upper parts conquering the air. It is "cold-warm or warm-cold" depending on which is emphasized.

## Poetry

Meter is the straight and curved line, recurring regularly. Recital adds a melodic line of rise and fall, tension and release, bound to the content. The reciter's freedom with that line matters little in "literary" verse but is "fatal in an abstract poem," where pitch is essential and needs notation as exact as music's. "Abstract art must reckon with a more precise form than representative art." The point in language is silence.

## Engineering and technics

Line grows ever more important in engineering:
- **Structures of line**: the Eiffel Tower ("the line has here replaced the plane"), a sailing ship's hull and rigging ("linear construction for the purpose of movement"), a freighter's framework, a radio tower, a "forest of masts" (a "technical forest" of line and point only).
- **Joints**: connections and rivets are the points of these "line-point constructions, not upon the plane, but in space."
- **Constructivism**: constructivist works are the same constructions made purely, without practical use, so they belong to absolute art.
- **Charts**: lines also express number in statistical charts, meteorological traces, current curves and the astronomer's "lightcurve". There "the line partially replaces the number" and makes the chart clear to the layman.

## Nature

- Nature uses the basic elements in her own realm. The artist should study which elements, with which characteristics, and how they combine. The aim is to contrast nature's laws with art's, not to imitate superficially. Both realms show the principle of the parallel and the principle of contrast, and kept apart they lead toward "the whole body of the laws of world composition."
- **Point to line in nature**:
  - The crystal is a purely linear formation, and the ice crystal its plane form.
  - A plant passes from point to line, from seed to root and up to the bud, and then into complexes of line such as the leaf network and the evergreen's eccentric construction. Leaf positions follow mathematical spirals.
  - Branching patterns range from geometric to loose: fir, fig, date palm, liana, the spider's web, a rat's loose ligament tissue, a clematis blossom, a lightning stroke. Abstract painting uses both geometric and loose structure.
- **Same laws, different material.** "The difference between art and nature lies not in the basic laws but, rather, in the material." Nature's proto-element, the cell, is in constant movement. Painting's, the point, "knows no movement and is rest."
- **Concentric and eccentric.** Skeletons from toad to giraffe to man are variations on one theme, drawn from "the one principle of concentric structure". Nature's laws exclude the eccentric, but for art "the road of the eccentric remains completely free and open." A finger grows from the hand as a twig from a branch, out of the center. In painting a line may stand with no outer relation to the center, because its subordination is inner. Do not confuse "the shell with the nut."

## Art, time and culture

Art relates to its time in three ways:
1. **Art submits to its time.**
   - (a) If the time is strong and concentrated, an equally strong art follows it effortlessly.
   - (b) If the time is strong but its content has decayed, a weak art succumbs. Kandinsky's examples are the exclusive horizontal-vertical, which "condemns 'pure' art to death" and leaves only the practical-useful, and Dadaism, which mirrors the disintegration and loses its foundations.
2. **Art stands in opposition**, voicing possibilities that contradict its time. His example is the formalized "eccentricity" of American theatre art.
3. **Art transgresses the time's boundaries** and forecasts the future. Abstract art does this.

- **Explaining art.** Geographic, economic, political and other purely "positive" explanations "can never be conclusive". Only form questions read through their spiritual content show the direction. "The goal of a creative power is an inner one—this inner cannot be shelled out of the external alone." "Under the visible and comprehensible lies the invisible and incomprehensible."
- **Today.** Kandinsky's present is "blind alley and threshold", with the blind alley preponderant. Absorption in the external drives the exclusive preference for horizontal-vertical and for black and white, an inner silence amid outer noise. Retreats into the past are no answer.
- **Nations.** Inner differences between "nations" decide, not geography. Europe mourns in black and China in white, and both colours are silence. For the European, silence is a "bottomless pit". For the Chinese it is "birth", the first step to a new language. The science of art must be international.

## The science of art

- It has two goals: **"pure" science**, the non-utilitarian urge to know, and **"practical" science**, the balance of intuition and calculation in the creative powers.
- It starts by distinguishing **basic elements** (without which a work in a given art cannot exist) from **secondary elements**, each in organic gradation.
- Art history should analyse each period's elements, construction and composition. It should also study their growth: its direction, its speed, and the necessity behind it, which perhaps follows a wave line.
- Systematic work builds an **elementary dictionary**, then a **"grammar"**, then a **theory of composition** for "Art" as a whole. Like a living language's dictionary, it changes perpetually. Painting first needs an exact terminology.
- It should be collective: "a work program accepted by many" and systematic international art institutes, replacing methods that were "too haphazard and, frequently, too personal."
- **Goal of theory**: "1. to find the living, 2. to make its pulsation perceptible, and 3. to determine wherein the living conforms to law." Philosophy then draws the synthesis.
- Painting and graphics differ only externally. Kandinsky proposes "hand-painting" and "print-painting", subdivided into black-and-white and colour.
