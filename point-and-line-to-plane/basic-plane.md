# Basic Plane (BP)

"The material plane which is called upon to receive the content of the work of art." The schematic BP is bounded by 2 horizontals and 2 verticals, which set it off from its surroundings as an individual thing. Its sound depends on the BP itself and not only on the elements, a fact independent of the artist's powers and "a source of great possibilities in composition."

## The BP is a living being

Every living thing stands in a fixed relation to above and below, and so does the BP. Every artist feels "the 'breathing' of the still untouched BP." Its frivolous abuse "is akin to murder". The artist "fertilizes" it, and it receives "the right elements in the right order" and becomes a new, fully developed organism. Treat the BP "solely as the base upon which the work is to be built." Left and right are read as the viewer sees them, because the BP mirrors the artist facing it.

## Format and temperature

- Two elements of cold rest (the horizontals) and two of warm rest (the verticals) give the BP its "tranquil—objective sound."
- **Square**: both pairs sound equally and cold and warm are relatively balanced, so it is the most objective BP. Put a single most-objective element on it and the result is "a coldness similar to death", which can serve as the symbol of death. Absolute objectivity is unattainable: the square is not colourless (square ⇄ red). Only an isolated point offers complete rest.
- **Horizontal format**: cold rest predominates. **Vertical format**: warm rest. "Striving upward and stretching outward are antipodes." The square's objectivity gives way to a one-sided tension of the whole BP that acts on every element.
  - The predominance is set from the start and "cannot be completely eliminated."
  - "A concentration of active tensions tending upwards upon a colder BP (horizontal format) will always more or less 'dramatize' these tensions," because the restraint is strong. Driven to the extreme, the restraint becomes painful.
  - Rectangles are more complex than the square because their borders depend on each other more. A horizontal format's long upper edge offers freedom that its short sides soon suppress. In a vertical format "full play upward is facilitated," because the pressure of the surroundings comes mostly from the sides.
  - Kandinsky also says that elements tending upward "can be made 'dramatic' in the vertical format by bringing them into a milieu of restraint."
- **The diagonal as measure.** A square's diagonal stands at 45°. In other rectangles it inclines toward the vertical or the horizontal, so it is "a kind of measure of tension." "The slightest deviation ... is decisive in compositional and, more especially, in abstract art": every form's tension changes direction and colour, and complexes are compressed or stretched. "Through an unskillful choice of the plane format, a well-conceived order can result in repulsive disorder."
- In "objective" painting, vertical and horizontal formats are naturalistic conventions (portrait and landscape) untouched by inner tension.

## The four sides

Each side has a second sound, beyond warm or cold rest, bound to its position.

**Above**: "great looseness, a feeling of lightness, of emancipation and, finally, of freedom."
- Looseness negates density: the smallest areas near the top seem disintegrated.
- Lightness: these areas lose weight and the capacity to support, so "every weightier form thereby grows heavier in this upper position."
- Freedom: tension plays out easily, "climbing" or "falling" gains intensity, and restraint is minimal.

**Below**: "condensation, heaviness, constraint."
- The atmosphere thickens toward the bottom and the small areas easily sustain large heavy forms, which lose weight.
- Climbing grows hard. Forms tear loose "by main force", with "the grating noise of friction."
- Restraint is at its maximum.

Above and below form a double sound of the greatest possible contrast.

**Left**: repeats "above" (looseness, lightness, emancipation, freedom) in lesser degree. It is denser and less free than above, yet far lighter than below. These qualities increase upward from the center and diminish downward.

**Right**: "the continuation of 'below'" with the same weakening. Heaviness and constraint decrease, but resistance stays "greater, compacter and harder than the resistance of 'left'". It increases from the center downward. Left and right each colour the corners they share with above and below.

**"Literary" correspondences.** These follow the only two movements a human has.

| Side | Tension | "Literary" |
|---|---|---|
| above | toward | heaven |
| left | into | the distance: going out, leaving convention, adventure. Forms tensed leftward gain intensity and speed. |
| right | toward | home: fatigue, rest. Forms tensed rightward grow languid, slow and weak. |
| below | toward | the earth |

These relations "serve the purpose of representing the inner tensions of the BP analytically, and to bring these tensions into consciousness". Do not take them literally, and do not let them dictate the compositional idea. Words like "movement," "climbing" and "falling" come from the material world. On the BP they mean the tensions within elements, modified by the tensions of the BP.

## Weight: dramatization and balance

Kandinsky's schematic weights (BP weight + form weight):

| Case | Above | Below | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. "dramatization": heavier forms below, lighter above | 2 + 2 = 4 | 4 + 4 = 8 | 4:8 |
| 2. "balance": heavier forms above, lighter below | 2 + 4 = 6 | 4 + 2 = 6 | 6:6 |

Tensions directed from above to below, or from below to above, also equalize. "Weight" is "the expression of an inner force or, in our case, an inner tension". It is not material weight, and present measurement is too primitive to give even a scarcely visible point an exact number.

**Resisting forces of the borders.** Every border resists an approaching form, and this separates the BP from the world. Kandinsky's diagram draws the top border thinnest, then the left, then the right, with the bottom thickest. "The approach of a form to the border is, therefore, subject to a special influence, which is of critical importance in the composition." The square's outer expression has four 90° angles. Its inner expression is a distorted quadrilateral, for example with angles of 60°, 80°, 90° and 130°, whose axis is deflected toward the angle at the upper right.

## Quarters and diagonals

The diagonals cross at the center. A horizontal and a vertical through that center cut four primary parts, all touching at the "indifferent" center, from which tensions flow diagonally. Label the corners **a** upper-left, **b** upper-right, **c** lower-left, **d** lower-right. Label the borders **1** top, **2** left, **3** right, **4** bottom.

| Part | Tension toward | Character |
|---|---|---|
| a | 1-2 | loosest combination |
| b | 1-3 | moderate resistance upward |
| c | 2-4 | moderate resistance downward |
| d | 3-4 | greatest resistance |

a and d are in greatest contrast. b and c are in moderate contrast. Weight rises from a (lightest) through b and c to d (heaviest).

- **cb: the "harmonious" diagonal**, lower-left to upper-right. The triangle above it rests lightly on the one beneath. **"Lyric" tension.**
- **ad: the "disharmonious" diagonal**, upper-left to lower-right. The triangle above it bears down, with pressure concentrated at d, so the diagonal is deflected upward at a and out of the center. **"Dramatic" tension.**

These labels are "arrows pointing the way to the inner content ... bridges from the external to the inner." Kandinsky reads his own "Composition 1" (1910) as "structure cb and da with forcible emphasis on cb—this is the backbone of the picture." Each part of the BP "has its own particular voice and inner colouration."

## Eight basic positions (the wedge experiment)

Put a simple pointed form, a wedge halfway between line and plane, on the square in each direction.

**A. External parallels** follow the BP's edges, not its inner tension.
- **I. Vertical position, "warm rest."** One wedge points up and one points down. This is the **greatest contrast**: one is directed toward the loosest resistance, the other toward the stiffest.
- **II. Horizontal position, "cold rest."** One wedge points left and one points right. This is a **mild contrast**: both point toward the milder resistances.

**B. Inner parallels** follow the BP's inner tensions, which "requires the diagonal direction."
- **I. Diagonal position, "disharmonious" (ad).** One wedge points to the loosest angle (a) and one to the firmest (d). This is the **greatest contrast**.
- **II. Diagonal position, "harmonious" (cb).** This is a **mild contrast**.

These four pairs give **eight basic positions**, lying on the surface or hidden beneath it. Further main directions can be piled on them, kept central or moved off center, and the foundation itself may avoid the center. "The number of possibilities of construction is unlimited." The basic sound of compositional "tendencies" comes from the inner mood of the time, of the nation, and of the personality. Kandinsky notes that recent decades swung between waves of the concentric and the eccentric, and between the wish to abandon the BP and the wish to keep it.

## Testing a form's direction

Kandinsky's example is a free curve with two bends to one side and three to the other. Its broad upper part gives it an "obstinate" look. Set it in a tall rectangle:

- Facing one way: "Obstinacy with forbearance. The bends are loose. Resistance from the left, weak. At the right, compressed layer."
- Facing the other: "Obstinacy in stiff tension. The bends harder. Resistance from the right strongly restraining. At the left, loose 'air.'"
- Upside down, the obstinacy vanishes and "a laborious tension" replaces it. Everything is "in the process of growing". Turned left, the growing is stronger. Turned right, the laboriousness is.

Reverse right and left with a mirror and above and below by turning the work over. Trust the first impression.

A distorted square laid on the BP can be an inner parallel or a contrast. As Kandinsky labels them, one placement is "Inner parallel of lyrical sound. Concurrence with the inner 'disharmonious' tension," and the other "Inner parallel of dramatic sound. Contrast to the inner 'harmonious' tension."

## Distance from the border

Take a straight line of fixed length:

- **Lying free** near the upper right, it gains pronounced tension toward the upper right, and its lower end weakens.
- **Touching the upper edge**, it "immediately loses its tension upwards." Downward tension rises and it turns "sickly, almost despairing". It also looks longer.

The rules:
- "On approaching the boundary of the BP, a form increases in tension until, at the moment of contact with the boundary, the tension suddenly ceases."
- "The farther a form lies from the edge of the BP, the weaker becomes the attraction of the form to the edge."
- Forms near the border augment the **dramatic** sound. Forms gathered about the center lend a **lyrical** sound.

These rules are schematic. Other means can bring them to full force or reduce them to a whisper, but they always act.

Kandinsky's examples:
- Four elementary lines (horizontal, vertical, two diagonals) centered and equal: "Silent lyric ... expression of rigidity."
- The same lines unequal and displaced: "Dramatization of the same elements—complex pulsating expression."
- "Diagonals centered. Horizontal-vertical acentric. Diagonals in the greatest tension. Balanced tensions of the horizontal and vertical."
- "Everything acentric. Diagonals strengthened through their repetition. Restraint of the dramatic sound at the point of contact above. The acentric structure here serves the purpose of increasing the dramatic sound."

Replacing the straight lines with simple curves triples the sum of sounds, since each curve has two tensions that produce a third. Wave lines multiply it again, with every bend relating separately to the edges.

## Other BP shapes

- **Angled BPs**: obtuse and acute angles in any combination. Shape the BP so its upper-right angle confronts the elements "in either a reinforcing or restraining manner." Many-angled BPs reduce to one basic form.
- **Circle**: angles multiply and open until they vanish. It is simple because the pressure of its boundaries is equalized, and complex because the top flows into left and right and they flow down. Only four points keep the decided sound of the four sides, contrasting 1-4 and 2-3. The quadrant from top toward left restricts the maximum freedom step by step, and from left toward the bottom freedom turns to hardness. "The circle has the same inner tension concealed within it as was discovered in the square." It tends most toward colourless rest. Two diagonals through a circle, their ends joined by horizontals and verticals, yield the roots of Arabic and Roman numerals (after A. S. Puschkin), which suggests number and art forms share one root.
- **Oval and free BPs**: the compressed circle leads to free angleless planes. The same principles hold behind them.

## Carrying elements: material or floating

The BP carries elements in two typical ways:

1. They lie so materially on it that they stress its sound.
2. They are so loosely knit to it that it nearly disappears, and they "hover" in space without precise limits, especially in depth. This is the "destruction" of the BP. Advancing and receding forms pull it forward and back "like an accordion", and colour does this most strongly.

**BP texture**: smooth, rough, grainy, prickly, glossy, dull or plastic, depending on the material (canvas, stucco, paper, stone, glass) and the tool. Texture works either way:

1. **parallel** to the elements, supporting them externally;
2. by **contrast**, opposing them externally and supporting them inwardly. Variations lie in between.

The same means that build the material plane can destroy it optically. Knowledge of material and handicraft serves both its making and its **dematerialization**, "the road from the external to the inner."

**The observer.** Floating also depends on the eye. An undeveloped eye "will not be able to emancipate itself from the material plane." "The properly trained eye must have the ability partly to see the plane, as such, necessary to the work of art and partly to disregard it when it takes on spatial form." The BP's inner tensions carry over into indefinable space, and "the law does not lose its effect." Dematerialization also changes time: illusory depth takes real, if unmeasurable, time to follow, so it "offers the opportunity of increasing the span of time."

## Kandinsky's compositional diagrams (captions)

Patterns he demonstrated:
- **Points**:
  - "Cool tension toward the center" (a cluster thick at the center, sparse at the edges).
  - "Dissolution in progress (suggested diagonal d-a)".
  - "9 points in ascent (emphasis upon the diagonal d-a through weight)".
  - "Horizontal-vertical-diagonal point pattern for a free line construction".
  - "The black and white points as elementary colour values".
- **Weight and the plane's edge**:
  - "With a point on the edge of the plane".
  - "Emphasized weights in black and white".
  - "The thin lines hold their own in the presence of the heavy point".
  - "Eccentric structure in which the eccentricity is emphasized by the developing plane".
- **Format**: "The horizontal format favors the total tension of individual forms in slight tension".
- **Curves with accompaniment**:
  - "Free curve to the point—accompanying sound of geometric curves".
  - "Free wave-like line with accent—horizontal position", then "The same wave-like line accompanied by geometric lines".
  - "Simple and unified complex of several free lines", then "The same complex complicated by free spirals".
- **Tension and pulsation**:
  - "Diagonal tensions and counter-tensions with a point which brings an external construction to inner pulsation".
  - "Double sound—cold tension of the straight lines, warm tension of the curved lines, the rigid to the loose, the yielding to the compact".
  - "Colour vibration attained in essence through a minimum of colour (black)".
- **His own pictures**:
  - "Composition 4" (1911): vertical-diagonal ascent.
  - "Black Triangle" (1925): inner relation of a straight-line complex to a curve, left-right.
  - "Intimate Communication" (1925): horizontal-vertical structure with contrasting diagonal and point tensions.
