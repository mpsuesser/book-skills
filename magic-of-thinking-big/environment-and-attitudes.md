# Environment and attitudes

Two chapters: manage your environment and go first class; make your attitudes your allies (I'm activated, you are important, service first).

## Manage your environment: go first class

"The body is what the body is fed... the mind is what the mind is fed." **Mind food** is your environment: everything that influences your conscious and subconscious thought, and so your habits, attitudes and personality. Inherited capacity sets the start; how far it develops depends on the mind food. Picture being raised in another country and ask how different your tastes, beliefs and ambitions would be. "You are a product of your environment." Around negative people you think negatively, around petty people you pick up petty habits, around people with big ideas your level of thinking rises. Who you will be one to twenty years from now "depends almost entirely on your future environment."

### Recondition yourself for success

"The number one obstacle on the road to high-level success is the feeling that major accomplishment is beyond reach." Children set high goals; then **suppressive forces** go to work: "It's foolish to be a dreamer"; your ideas are "impractical, stupid, naive, or foolish"; you've "got to have money to go places"; success takes luck or important friends; you're "too old or too young." This you-can't-get-ahead-so-don't-bother-to-try propaganda sorts people into three groups:

1. **Those who surrendered completely**, the majority. They rationalize their status and explain how "happy" they are. A thirty-two-year-old in a safe, mediocre job argues himself out of a real opportunity. A rut is "a grave with both ends open." The other extreme is the aimless job switcher with no plan.
2. **Those who surrendered partially**, a smaller group. They prepared, worked and planned, then after a decade decided the top wasn't worth the effort ("We're earning more than the average... Why should we knock ourselves out?"). Their fears: of failure, social disapproval, insecurity, losing what they have. They "crawl through life because they are afraid to stand up and run."
3. **Those who never surrender**, maybe 2 or 3 percent. They don't let pessimism dominate, don't believe in surrender, and live and breathe success. They are the happiest, accomplish the most, and are the top salesmen, executives and leaders; each day is an adventure.

**The vice-president test.** Tell your "average" friends "Someday I'm going to be vice president of this company" and they laugh or pity you. Tell the president and he won't laugh; he'll wonder, "Does this fellow really mean this?" "Big men do not laugh at big ideas." Likewise the people who already live in an expensive home aren't surprised that you plan one. "People who tell you it cannot be done almost always are unsuccessful people." Their opinions "can be poison." Accept negative advice only as a challenge to prove you can.

### Negators

**Negators** are negative-thinking people who delight in sabotaging others' progress. Schwartz's friend W. W. was loyal and 100 percent sour: he mocked a hopeful columnist as "sweet sauce for the weak" and held that there were three ways to make money: marry rich, steal "in a nice clean legal way," or know people with pull, citing rare cases as proof. Schwartz was nearly converted until he realized he was "listening to the voice of failure," and that W. W. was talking mostly to convince himself. He turned W. W. into "an object lesson," a "personal experiment," studying him to learn what not to do. Eleven years later W. W. was a low-paid draftsman, more negative than ever, with the brains to earn five times as much. Some negators mean well; others are jealous, feel inadequate, and want to make a mediocre person of you. Study negators; don't absorb them.

- **The car pool.** Schwartz switched from a pool with a chronic fault-finder (forty-five minutes each way) to a group "who can see two sides to a question." "You are judged by the company you keep." At work some belittle the boss; others realize "they must be good followers before they can be good leaders." "Be sure you're in the flock that thinks right."
- **Jealous blockers.** People secretly aware of their own inadequacies try to block you: factory workers resenting the one who wants to speed up production, a clique mocking the soldier who wants to go to officers' school, high-school "lunkheads" jeering a good student until "it isn't smart to be intelligent." Their remarks are "merely a projection of the speaker's own feeling of failure and discouragement." Let them slide off like water off a duck's back and cling to progressive thinkers.
- **Freelance advisors** who "know the ropes" ("stay out of everybody's way... stay away from Mr. Z") have usually been there thirty years and are still bottom man on the totem pole.

### Seek advice from people who know

Successful people are accessible, humble and ready to help; they want their work to live on and to have a capable successor. The "would-be-big" are the most abrupt. One executive keeps no Do Not Disturb sign, gives "tutoring" for the asking, and likes curiosity and a desire to understand the job and how it relates to others; he won't advise the insincere. "Go first class when you have questions. Seeking advice from a failure is like consulting a quack on how to cure cancer."

### Off-the-job environment: psychological sunshine

Some executives interview a candidate's wife before hiring, because "what happens on weekends and between 6 P.M. and 9 A.M. directly affects a person's performance from 9 A.M. to 6 P.M."

- **John** plans his weekends: an evening with interesting friends, an outing or civic activity, Boy Scouts, chores, a patio project, a special Sunday family trip (a mountain, a museum, the countryside to look at future property), and Sunday evening reading. He gets **psychological sunshine**, like an athlete fed steak.
- **Milton** doesn't plan: he's "tired," watches TV or a movie ("What else is there to do?"), sleeps late, sees the same couple, Bill and Mary, and by Sunday has "cabin fever" and a little family "psychological warfare." He is psychologically starved, an athlete fed candy and beer.

Over months and years the gap widens until observers say "John has more on the ball." A department-store executive regularly entertains a writer, a doctor, an engineer, an accountant, a teacher, to avoid a rut: "people are my business."

Three "do's" for a first-class social environment:

1. **Do circulate in new groups.** Studying one small group to understand people is like mastering mathematics from one short book. Make new friends, join organizations, enlarge your social orbit.
2. **Do select friends who have views different from your own.** A Republican and a Democrat, people of different faiths: associate with opposites who have real potential, and learn to see both sides.
3. **Do select friends who stand above petty, unimportant things.** Choose friends who care more about your ideas than your square footage or appliances, who want you to succeed and breathe encouragement. Petty friends make a petty thinker.

### Throw thought poison out of your environment

**Thought poison**, commonly called gossip, is subtle: the victim doesn't know he is being poisoned. It reduces the size of your thinking by concentrating on petty things, warps your thinking about people with distorted facts, and creates guilt that shows when you meet the person discussed. It is "0 percent right thinking... 100 percent wrong thinking." Men gossip as much as women: the boss's marital or money troubles, Bill's politicking, John's transfer, Tom's favors, "why they brought in that new man." The pattern opens "Say, I just heard... of course, this is confidential." Conversation can be a walk in sunshine or a "poisonous, radioactive cloud." "Gossip is just negative conversation about people," and to successful people the gossiper becomes unlikable and unreliable. "Talk about people? Yes, but stay on the positive side." Bull sessions and shop talk are fine when constructive.

The gossip self-test (the right answers are obvious). Ask whether you:

- spread rumors about other people, or encourage others to bring rumors to you;
- like hearing reports of scandal;
- open conversations with "Don't tell anybody";
- always have good things to say about others, and judge them only on facts;
- keep confidential information confidential;
- feel guilty about what you say about other people.

Taking an ax to your neighbor's furniture doesn't make yours look better; verbal axes and grenades don't make you better either.

### Go first class in goods and services

The penny-wise, pound-foolish answers: a cheap suit; an alley garage that did a transmission job $25 cheaper and lasted 1,800 miles; a greasy spoon when the best restaurant's businessman's lunch costs only slightly more; a bargain accountant who brings IRS trouble; a cut-rate doctor's wrong diagnosis; cheap home repairs and hotels. "But I can't afford to go first class": "you cannot afford to go any other way." In the long run it costs less, and a few quality things beat many junk things (one good pair of shoes over three second-class pairs). "People rate you for quality." Develop an instinct for quality.

Chapter summary: (1) be environment-conscious; your mind diet makes your mind; (2) make your environment work for you, not against you; don't let suppressive forces make you think small; (3) don't let small-thinking people hold you back; (4) get your advice from successful people, not freelance advisors who are living failures; (5) get plenty of psychological sunshine: new groups, stimulating activities; (6) throw thought poison out of your environment; (7) go first class in everything you do.

## Make your attitudes your allies

"You Don't Need to Know the Language to Say You're in Love." "People speak without a sound": **attitudes are mirrors of the mind**, read through face, voice and bearing. A secretary's "Good morning, Mr. Shoemaker's office" says either "I like you... I think you are important. I like my job" or "You bothered me... I'm bored." Language is recent; people communicated by face, body and sound for ages before it, and even infants spot the phony. Actors convince only when they think and feel the character. Schell: attitude is the "linkage factor"; "When our attitude is right, our abilities reach a maximum of effectiveness."

Three attitudes to develop: **I'm activated**, **you are important**, **service first**.

### I'm activated

"To activate others, you must first activate yourself." A dull history professor had assistants patrol the aisles and warned the class, "You've got to pay attention"; he was bored, so they were bored. "To activate others, to get them to be enthusiastic, you must first be enthusiastic yourself." The enthusiastic salesman needn't worry about unenthusiastic buyers, nor the teacher about students, nor the minister about the congregation.

**Enthusiasm can make things 1,100 percent better.** One Red Cross captain told his group "I suppose it's a worthwhile organization... If you can make a donation, see me," and collected $94.35. A new captain told case histories, explained that the work depends on everyone, asked people to give by how much they'd give their neighbor if disaster struck, and showed "Look what the Red Cross has done!" He didn't beg or set quotas, and collected almost $1,100. "Results come in proportion to enthusiasm applied." Enthusiasm is simply "This is great!"

The three-step procedure to develop the power of enthusiasm:

1. **Dig into it deeper.** Name two things you have no interest in and ask, "How much do I really know about these things?" The odds are 100 to 1 the answer is "Not much." Schwartz had no use for modern art until he learned about it. "To get enthusiastic, learn more about the thing you are not enthusiastic about." The greenhouse exercise: suppose you learned how a $600 greenhouse could be sold to one family in fifty, a $600 million business, and perhaps a $250 million industry in plants and seeds; you'd get interested fast. Apply it to people (learn what a person does, his family, background, ideas and ambitions, and you find common interests and a fascinating person) and to places (a Detroit couple came back from a small Florida town within six weeks because they "moved their bodies to Florida but left their minds in Detroit"; a transferred executive should dig into the new community, mix, and feel like a citizen from the first day). Use this whenever you must do something you don't want to do or are bored: "dig in deeper and you dig up interest."
2. **In everything you do, life it up.** When you shake hands, shake: a firm clasp that says "I'm glad to know you"; a mouselike handshake that feels "more dead than alive" is worse than none. Smile with your eyes and show a few teeth; skip the pasted-on smile. Make "thank you" a real "thank you very much," not a routine "gleep, gleep." Put life into talk: as James F. Bender asks in *How to Talk Well*, is your "Good morning!" really good? A sermon without enthusiasm is forgotten "167 hours before next Sunday." Say aloud "I feel great today!" Let everything about you say "That fellow is alive," "He means it," "He's going places."
3. **Broadcast good news.** "I've got good news" gets 100 percent attention. "No one ever won a friend, no one ever made money, no one ever accomplished anything by broadcasting bad news." Tell your family the good things of the day and bring home sunlight; speak favorably about the weather, always; be an "I-feel-great" person ("How we feel is, in large part, determined by how we think we feel"). To coworkers: encourage them, compliment them, pass on the positive company news, listen to their problems, pat them on the back, give them hope, show faith in them, relieve the worriers. One salesman always opens with good news ("350,000 new babies"). Mills Lane, Jr., of Citizens and Southern Bank greets customers with "Good morning, it's a wonderful world. May I sell you some money?" A brush-company president keeps a frame whose visitor side says "Give me a Good Word or none at all" and whose own side says "Give them a Good Word or none at all."

The test after leaving anyone (employees, associates, family, customers, acquaintances): "Does that person honestly feel better because he has talked with me?"

### You are important

Everyone wants to feel important: "The desire to be important is man's strongest, most compelling nonbiological hunger." Ad headlines play to it ("For Smart Young Homemakers," "Be the Envy of Everyone"). It costs nothing, yet few use it. Philosophically, society spends whatever it takes to find a downed pilot or a child in a well, because every person matters. Practically, the blunder is "He can't do anything for me. Therefore, he's not important."

Two dollars-and-cents reasons:

1. **People do more for you when you make them feel important.** A grumpy Detroit bus driver who waited for no one waited for the passenger who said "Good morning, sir" and admired the "nerves of steel" it took to keep the bus on schedule. "It pays to make 'little' people feel like big people": secretaries can win or lose a salesman a sale, and customers, employees, associates and the boss all do more for you. "It pays to make 'big' people feel even bigger"; the big thinker visualizes people at their best.
2. **When you help others feel important, you help yourself feel important too.** Schwartz complimented the elevator operator Miss S. on her hair and used her name; the next morning she said "Good morning, Dr. Schwartz," the only name she ever used. "You must feel important to succeed."

How to show it:

1. **Practice appreciation.** Never let anyone feel taken for granted. Give a warm, sincere smile. Let people know how you depend on them ("Jim, I don't know what we'd do without you"); people who feel necessary do better work. Give honest, personalized compliments, including on little things (appearance, routine work, ideas, loyal effort), and send personal notes or make a special call or trip. Don't classify people as "very important," "important" and "unimportant"; the garbage collector matters as much as the vice president. "Treating someone as second-class never gets you first-class results."
2. **Practice calling people by their names.** People put their names on briefcases, pencils and Bibles. Pronounce and spell names correctly, and add Miss, Mister or Mrs. for people you don't know well (the office boy would rather be Mr. Jones).
3. **Don't hog glory; invest it instead.** At an awards dinner, a district manager three months in the job said "When I took over, I did..." and left his salesmen resentful and demoralized. Another manager credited her sales force, had each person stand, and paid each a personal compliment. "Praise, like money, can be invested to pay dividends." "Praise is power." Pass praise down.

**The family question.** Ask daily, "What can I do today to make my wife and family happy?" Of thirty-five married salesmen, only one had surprised his wife in the past month, many hadn't in three to six months, and over a third "can't remember." Schwartz had a florist deliver a 50-cent long-stemmed red rose to each wife; every one was happy. Thoughtfulness, not money, counts. **Give them planned attention:** one vice president spends 7:30 to 8:30 each morning with his two children, works at home 8:30 to 10:30, then gives his wife an undisturbed hour at 10:30, and keeps Sundays for family. "Gives me new energy."

### Put service first

Wanting money is natural and desirable. The minister Conwell, author of *Acres of Diamonds*: "Money printed your Bible, money builds your churches." A person who says he wants to be poor has a guilt complex or a feeling of inadequacy, like the student who pretends not to want A's. But the **money-first** attitude brings little money, because money can't be harvested without planting seeds. "The seed of money is service." "Put service first, and money takes care of itself."

- A Cincinnati station attendant cleaned the inside of Schwartz's windshield, which perhaps one in a hundred do. He could have figured the odds were twenty to one this out-of-town car would never be back; instead Schwartz returned eight times in three months, some 100 gallons. He "planted a money seed."
- F. H. called himself "the most underpaid man here" and demanded a raise because others had been promoted, his bills were up and he did "everything around here they ask me to do." The boss: "Why do you believe you are justified...?" and "When your record shows that you deserve more money, you'll get more money." F. H. held that "only a fool does something he isn't paid for." The rule: "You don't get a raise on the promise of better performance; you get a raise only by demonstrating better performance."
- The get-rich-quick movie producer cuts corners and treats the moviegoer as a sucker: "There never is a bandwagon movement to buy anything second-class, especially when it is given a first-class price." The big-profit producer gives more and better entertainment than expected.
- A waitress left coffee cups empty because "They don't look like tippers." A secretary sent out smudged letters ("What do they expect for $65 a week?") and stayed at $65.

The rule: "Always give people more than they expect to get." Each little extra (volunteering to work late, extra service to a customer, an efficiency idea) is a **money seed**. The daily question: "How can I give more than is expected of me?"

Recap: (1) **I'm activated**: results come in proportion to the enthusiasm invested; dig into it deeper, life up everything (smile, handshake, talk, walk), broadcast good news. (2) **You are important**: show appreciation, make people feel important, call people by name. (3) **Service first**: give people more than they expect.
