# Belief and excusitis

Schwartz's first two steps: believe you can succeed, then vaccinate yourself against excuses.

## Belief

**Belief is not wishful thinking.** You cannot *wish* away a mountain, wish yourself into an executive suite or a five-bedroom house. But "you *can* move a mountain with belief." Belief, "the 'I'm-positive-I-can' attitude, generates the power, skill, and energy needed to do." Strong belief "triggers the mind to figure ways and means and how-to," and it makes others place confidence in you.

- Young people starting jobs who *believe* they will reach the top study and observe senior executives: how they approach problems, make decisions and carry themselves. Those who only wish never discover the steps.
- A woman with under $3,000 set up a mobile-home sales agency against all advice. She admitted she lacked capital, experience and a soft market, but her "absolutely unquestioned belief" won two investors and a manufacturer who advanced inventory with no money down. She sold over $1,000,000 of trailers the next year.
- A state highway department offered a $5,000,000 job to design eight bridges, worth a $200,000 fee. Of seventeen small firms, sixteen said "It's too big for us." One firm of three engineers said "We can do it," bid, and won.
- Failures, in casual talk, drop remarks like "To tell the truth, I didn't think it would work." The "Okay-I'll-give-it-a-try-but-I-don't-think-it-will-work" attitude produces failures. Doubt is the **subconscious will to fail**, the *not really wanting* to succeed. "Think doubt and fail. Think victory and succeed."
- Respect, study and learn from the leader, but don't worship him: "Believe you can surpass." A young writer who said she could never be as successful as Mr. X held the "second-best attitude," and "those who harbor the second-best attitude are invariably second-best doers." Mr. X was "superconfident," nothing more.

**The thermostat.** The man shuffling in mediocrity believes he is worth little, so he receives little; his lack of belief shows in how he talks, walks and acts, and since others see in us what we see in ourselves, he shrinks in their estimation too. The man advancing believes he is worth much and can handle big assignments, and everything he does says "Here is a professional." "A person is a product of his own thoughts. Believe Big. Adjust your thermostat forward."

**The self-comparison exercise.** A tool-and-die worker, alone in a hotel the night before a job interview, wrote down five people he had known well who had far surpassed him, then compared himself with each on intelligence, education, integrity and personal habits. He matched them on all but initiative, and found he lacked initiative because he "didn't believe inside that I was worth very much"; "no one else was going to believe in me until I believed in myself." Next day, instead of the $750 to $1,000 raise he had hoped to ask, he asked $3,500 more and got it. Use this exercise when someone assumes others got ahead on superior gifts.

**The thought factory.** Mr. Triumph and Mr. Defeat (see `SKILL.md`) answer any signal. Tell yourself "Today is a lousy day" and Mr. Defeat manufactures the proof (weather, bad business, people on edge); say "Today is a fine day" and Mr. Triumph does the same for the good. Mr. Defeat can show why you can't sell Mr. Smith, why you'll fail, a brilliant case against Tom; Mr. Triumph will show the opposite. When any thought enters the mind, put Mr. Triumph to work on it.

**Step One: believe in yourself, believe you can succeed.** The three guides to acquiring and strengthening belief:

1. **Think success, don't think failure.** In a difficult situation think "I'll win," not "I'll probably lose"; competing, "I'm equal to the best," not "I'm outclassed"; facing opportunity, "I can do it," never "I can't." Let the master thought "I will succeed" dominate. Success thinking conditions the mind to create plans that produce success; failure thinking conditions it to produce failure.
2. **Remind yourself regularly that you are better than you think you are.** Successful people are "just ordinary folks who have developed belief in themselves and what they do." Success needs no superintellect, nothing mystical, no luck. "Never—yes, *never*—sell yourself short."
3. **Believe Big.** "The size of your success is determined by the size of your belief." Little goals, little achievements. "Big ideas and big plans are often easier—certainly no more difficult—than small ideas and small plans."

Self-development has to be self-driven (Ralph Cordiner of General Electric: "Nobody is going to *order* a man to develop"). People who reach the top in every pursuit follow "a *plan for self-development and growth.*" Any training program needs content (what to do), method (how), and the acid test (results).

## Excusitis, the failure disease

Every failure has excusitis in its advanced form; most "average" persons have a mild case. "The more successful the individual, the less inclined he is to make excuses." The mediocre are "quick to explain why they haven't, why they don't, why they can't, and why they aren't," while every excuse they make could be, but isn't, made by successful people (Roosevelt's legs, Truman's lack of college, Kennedy's youth, Johnson's and Eisenhower's heart attacks).

**How it progresses.** The victim thinks: "I'm not doing as well as I should. What can I use as an alibi that will help me save face?" He tries health, education, age, luck, misfortune, spouse, upbringing, picks a "good" one and sticks with it. At first he knows the alibi is more or less a lie; each repetition imbeds it deeper until he is convinced it is the real reason. **Procedure One: vaccinate yourself against excusitis.**

### 1. Health excusitis: "But my health isn't good."

From the chronic "I don't feel good" to "I've got such-and-such wrong with me." Physicians say the perfect adult specimen doesn't exist; everyone has something wrong, and success-thinking people don't surrender to it.

- A man of thirty with a "bad heart" four doctors couldn't find wanted support for holding back. Schwartz's three suggestions: see the finest specialist and accept his diagnosis as final ("Looking and looking and looking for an illness often actually produces illness"); read Dr. Schindler's *How to Live 365 Days a Year*, which holds that three of four hospital beds are filled by EII, Emotionally Induced Illness, and build a program of "emotions management"; and **resolve to live until I die**, like a lawyer with arrested tuberculosis who at seventy-eight still refused to "get life and death confused."
- On the same day Schwartz sat beside a man whose new plastic heart valve ticked audibly, twenty-one days after surgery, full of plans to study law. "The difference lay in how they thought toward health!"
- Schwartz, a diabetic, contrasts a mild case who "pitied himself into being an invalid" with a severe case taking about thirty times the insulin who said it's an inconvenience, "but so is shaving." A one-armed golfer: "the right attitude and one arm will beat the wrong attitude and two arms every time."

The four doses of vaccine:

1. **Refuse to talk about your health.** Talking about an ailment is "like putting fertilizer on weeds"; it bores people and makes one look self-centered. A chronic complainer may get a little sympathy but never respect and loyalty.
2. **Refuse to worry about your health.** Dr. Walter Alvarez of the Mayo Clinic begged a man whose gallbladder was normal on eight X-rays to stop getting it X-rayed, and hundreds of heart-conscious men to stop getting electrocardiograms.
3. **Be genuinely grateful that your health is as good as it is.** "I felt sorry for myself because I had ragged shoes until I met a man who had no feet."
4. **Remind yourself often, "It's better to wear out than rust out."**

### 2. Intelligence excusitis: "But you've got to have brains to succeed."

Perhaps 95 percent have it in some degree, and they suffer in silence. Two basic errors: **we underestimate our own brainpower, and we overestimate the other fellow's.**

The principle: "*the thinking that guides your intelligence is much more important than how much intelligence you may have.*"

- Edward Teller: a child needs no lightning-fast mind, miraculous memory or high grades to be a scientist, only "a high degree of interest in science." "Interest, enthusiasm, is the critical factor."
- A positive, optimistic, cooperative person with an IQ of 100 will out-earn and out-achieve a negative, pessimistic, uncooperative one with an IQ of 120. "*Stickability* is 95 percent of *ability*": enough sense to stick with a task until done beats idle intelligence, even of genius caliber.
- Chuck, an honors graduate, became "an expert in why a business idea won't work out" and audits freight shipments while less-bright classmates own businesses. A Phi Beta Kappa genius has a mediocre job (afraid of responsibility), never married (divorce), few friends (bored), no property (might lose it): he uses his brain "to prove why things won't work." A Ph.D. spent three Army years driving a truck because of his attitudes toward everyone.
- Phil F., a research director with no statistics and no degree, earned $30,000 when none of his graduate subordinates earned $10,000, because he was a "human" engineer: 100 percent positive, enthusiastic, inspiring, liked people.
- Fewer than 50 of 100 college entrants graduate, and an admissions director blamed attitudes (disliking professors, subjects, classmates), not ability or money. An executive: "Nearly always it's attitude." In an insurance company where the top 25 percent of agents sold over 75 percent and the bottom 25 percent sold 5 percent, personnel files showed no difference in intelligence or education; the top group "worried less, was more enthusiastic, had a sincere liking for people."

**Knowledge is power, when you use it constructively.** Knowledge is "only potential power." Einstein wouldn't store facts he could look up in two minutes; Henry Ford, asked trivia in his libel suit, said he could find a man in five minutes who knew. A company president would pay a quiz-show "fact man" "not a cent over $300... for life": "an idea man can make money with me; a fact man can't."

The three cures:

1. **Never underestimate your own intelligence, and never overestimate the intelligence of others.** Concentrate on your assets, discover your superior talents, manage your brains instead of worrying about your IQ.
2. **Remind yourself several times daily, "My attitudes are more important than my intelligence."** Develop an "I'm winning" attitude; use intelligence to find ways to win, not to prove you will lose.
3. **Remember that the ability to *think* is of much greater value than the ability to memorize facts.** Ask, "Am I using my mental ability to make history, or am I using it merely to record history made by others?"

### 3. Age excusitis: "It's no use. I'm too old (or too young)."

"The failure disease of never being the right age." It is spread by stories of the executive "washed up at 40," popular because they supply worried minds with an excuse. "It's one's attitude toward age that makes it a blessing or a barricade."

- **The productive-years computation** (the serum that cured Cecil, forty): productive life runs from about twenty to about seventy, fifty years. At forty Cecil had used twenty and had thirty left, only 40 percent used. He switched from "I'm already old" to "I'm still young." "Old age is a failure disease"; refuse to let it hold you back.
- A relative decided at forty-five, with three young children and little money, to become a minister; five years of training later he had a congregation and twenty productive years ahead. Bill, a stockbroker for twenty-four years, entered graduate school at fifty-one, finished at fifty-five and chaired an economics department: "I've got almost a third of my good years left."
- **Too young:** Jerry, twenty-three, was offered sales manager over eight men seven to twenty-one years older. Three points: *don't be age conscious* (on the farm a boy became a man when he proved he could do a man's work; prove you can handle the job and you are old enough); *don't take advantage of your new "gold bars"* (respect the men, ask their suggestions, be a team captain, not a dictator); *get used to having older persons working for you*, as every leader must. "Your age won't be a handicap unless you make it one." "Youth is a liability only when the youth thinks it is." What matters is knowing your job and understanding people. An insecure, job-scared person may use your age against you; the people who really count will not.

The cure:

1. **Look at your present age positively.** Think "I'm still young," not "I'm already old."
2. **Compute how much productive time you have left.** At thirty, 80 percent of productive life is ahead; at fifty, "a big 40 percent—the best 40 percent."
3. **Invest future time in doing what you really want to do.** "It's too late only when you let your mind go negative." Replace "I should have started years ago" with "I'm going to start now, my best years are ahead of me."

### 4. Luck excusitis: "But my case is different; I attract bad luck."

A traffic engineer: of 40,000-odd yearly traffic deaths, none is a true accident; each is human or mechanical failure. "*There is a cause for everything.*" Human affairs are no exception.

- Three junior executives explained a colleague's promotion by luck, pull, bootlicking and his wife's flattery. In fact George was better qualified, worked harder and had a more effective personality; top executives "don't select major executives by drawing names from a hat."
- A weak salesman congratulated John on a $112,000 order with "you're lucky again!" John had worked the account for months, seen a half-dozen people, stayed up nights on their needs, and had engineers draft preliminary designs: "carefully planned work and patiently executed plans."
- Imagine reorganizing General Motors by drawing names from a barrel for president, vice president and down the line. People rise "because they have superior attitudes and use their good sense in applied hard work."

Two cures:

1. **Accept the law of cause and effect.** Take a second look at someone's "good luck" and find preparation, planning and success-producing thinking. Look at "bad luck" and find specific reasons. Mr. Success learns from a setback; Mr. Mediocre fails to learn.
2. **Don't be a wishful thinker.** There is no effortless way. Success comes from mastering the principles that produce it; concentrate on developing the qualities that make you a winner.
