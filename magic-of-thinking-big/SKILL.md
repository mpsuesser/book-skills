# The Magic of Thinking Big

Schwartz's thesis: success "is determined not so much by the size of one's brain as it is *by the size of one's thinking.*" Harry, the top representative who earned five times the average, was no smarter, harder-working, better educated or better placed than the others: "Harry thought five times bigger." Size of income, of happiness, of satisfaction all depend on the size of one's thinking. Most people think small because, as Schwartz puts it, all of us "are products of the thinking around us," and most of that thinking is little. So the competition is thin: an employer gets 50 to 250 times as many applicants for $10,000 jobs as for $50,000 jobs, and "First Class Avenue... is a short, uncrowded street." Disraeli's line opens the book: "Life is too short to be little."

The mechanism, which every chapter applies:

- **Thinking regulates action; action regulates how others treat you.** "How you think determines how you act. How you act in turn determines how others react to you." "Others see in us what we see in ourselves." "We receive the kind of treatment we *think* we deserve."
- **Belief is the thermostat** "that regulates what we accomplish in life." "When you believe I-can-do-it, the *how*-to-do-it develops." Disbelief "is negative power": the doubting mind "attracts 'reasons' to support the disbelief." The fix is to **adjust your thermostat forward**.
- **Thoughts grow with repetition and breed like thoughts.** An excuse, a fear, a grudge or a negative memory gets stronger each time it is rehearsed ("fertilized with constant repetition"); one negative thought sets off a chain reaction of similar ones. The same is true of positive thoughts, which is why the cures are practices repeated daily.
- **Motions are the precursors of emotions** (Crane). Feelings are changed through actions: "To think confidently, act confidently." "Act the way you want to feel."
- **The mind is what the mind is fed.** Environment (people, conversation, reading, how weekends are spent) is "mind food" and shapes the size of one's thinking.

The program is **self-administered**: "Only *you* can command yourself to apply this training... evaluate your progress... bring about corrective action." The person's **laboratory** is the people around them. Schwartz's standing assignment: pick the most successful and the most unsuccessful person you know and watch how closely each follows these principles.

## Core vocabulary

Use these terms as the leading words of any diagnosis or advice; each reference file defines its own in full.

- **Thought factory** with two foremen: **Mr. Triumph** manufactures reasons why you can; **Mr. Defeat** specializes in the "why-you-will-fail" chain of thoughts. Both obey the slightest signal, and the one given more work grows until he runs the whole plant. Fire Mr. Defeat; use Mr. Triumph 100 percent of the time.
- **Excusitis**, "the failure disease": the habit of picking a face-saving alibi and repeating it until one believes it. The four common forms are health, intelligence, age and luck excusitis.
- **Self-deprecation**, "selling oneself short": "probably the greatest human weakness."
- **Big thinker / petty (small) thinker**: the book's master distinction, tested item by item in the size-of-thinking test.
- **Activationist / passivationist**: the doer versus the "don'ter" who "is going to do but doesn't."
- **Go first class**: in appearance, advice, friends, goods and services, and in how you treat people.

## How Schwartz coaches

- **Name the small thought, then prescribe a concrete practice.** Every chapter turns a diagnosis into a procedure with numbered steps, a question to ask oneself, a daily exercise or a checklist. Give the person the procedure, not encouragement alone.
- **Reframe by Socratic arithmetic or experiment.** When Cecil, forty, said he was too old, "You're only as old as you feel" got nowhere. What worked was asking when a productive life begins (about twenty) and ends (about seventy), then letting him compute that he had used only 40 percent of it. When trainees insisted jails were necessary, he asked them to assume jails could be eliminated and list how; they produced seventy-eight ideas. Lead people to compute or generate the big answer themselves.
- **Ask the question that forces specificity.** "What kind of action can I take to conquer my fear?" "Will you describe for me your image of yourself ten years from now?" "Is it really important?" "Will this help take me where I want to go?"
- **Assign a deadline and a report-back.** The clerk who called finishing college "impossible" was told to decide he would go back, think how, and return in two weeks with ideas; he came back with a plan and later earned the degree.
- **Point to evidence in the person's own world.** Compare oneself with five people who surpassed you; list three successful people who have less of each of your assets; watch who in a meeting talks most and who is most successful.
- Speak as the book does: plainly, confidently, in the second person, with "Try it and see."

## Life's most crucial situations

Schwartz closes with guides for the moments when thinking shrinks under pressure, meant to be carried on small cards. Use them as the first response; each links to depth above.

- **When little people try to drive you down:** you win when you refuse to fight petty people, since fighting them reduces you to their size; expect to be sniped at, because "it's proof you're growing"; remind yourself snipers are psychologically sick and feel sorry for them. "Think Big Enough to be immune to the attacks of petty people."
- **When the "I-haven't-got-what-it-takes" feeling creeps up:** "if you think you are weak, you are." Look important; concentrate on your assets and use your sell-yourself-to-yourself commercial; put the other person in proper perspective as "just another human being."
- **When an argument or quarrel seems inevitable:** ask, "Honestly now, is this thing really important enough to argue about?" and remember "you never gain anything from an argument but you always lose something."
- **When you feel defeated:** treat the setback as a lesson, research it, salvage something from it; blend persistence with experimentation; back off and start afresh. It is not possible to succeed without setbacks, "but it *is* possible to live the rest of your life without defeat."
- **When romance starts to slip:** "She's-(He's)-unfair-to-me-so-I'll-get-even" thinking slaughters romance. Concentrate on the biggest qualities in the other person and put little things in second place; do something special for your mate, often.
- **When progress on the job slows:** higher status and pay come only from increasing the quality and quantity of your output. Think "I can do better," which switches on creative power, and put service first.

The book's last word, from Publilius Syrus: "A wise man will be master of his mind, A fool will be its slave."
