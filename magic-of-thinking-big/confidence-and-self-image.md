# Confidence and self-image

Two chapters: building confidence and destroying fear, and "You are what you think you are" (self-respect, appearance, job thinking, self-talk).

## Fear and confidence

"It's only your imagination. Don't worry" never cures fear; that kind of fear medicine presumes fear isn't real. **Fear is real**, it is "success enemy number one," and it must be recognized before it is conquered. Most fear is psychological: worry, tension, embarrassment and panic "stem from mismanaged, negative imagination." Fear is a psychological infection, cured like a body infection, by specific proved treatments. Pretreatment fact: **"all confidence is acquired, developed. No one is born with confidence."**

### Action cures fear

Navy recruits terrified of a six-foot jump into deep water lost the fear the moment they were "accidentally" pushed off the board. "*Action cures fear.*" Indecision and postponement "fertilize fear."

A buyer whose department sales were down 7 percent while the store was up 6 percent said there wasn't much he could do "but hope for the best." Schwartz: "Honestly, now, is hope enough?... Why not take action to support your hope?" Two kinds of action: start that afternoon to move sales up (find the reason; clear slow stock with a sale, rearrange displays, enthuse the salespeople; talk privately with the merchandise manager and ask his advice, which buys time; "Quit acting like a drowning man"), and, as insurance, let two or three trade friends know he'd consider a substantially better offer ("it's ten times easier for a man with a job to get another job"). He began daily sales meetings; within weeks his sales beat the store's average and he turned down two offers. "Hope is a start. But hope needs action to win victories."

**The two-step procedure to cure fear and win confidence:**

1. **Isolate your fear.** Pin it down. Determine exactly what you are afraid of.
2. **Then take action.** "There is some kind of action for any kind of fear." Ask: "What kind of action can I take to conquer my fear?" Hesitation only enlarges the fear; act promptly and decisively.

Schwartz's examples of fears and their action cures:

| Type of fear | Action |
|---|---|
| Embarrassment because of personal appearance | Improve it: barber or beauty salon, shine shoes, clothes cleaned and pressed, better grooming. It doesn't always take new clothes. |
| Losing an important customer | Work doubly hard to give better service; correct anything that may have cost his confidence in you. |
| Failing an examination | Convert worry time into study time. |
| Things totally beyond your control | Turn your attention to helping relieve the fear of others. Pray. |
| Being physically hurt by something you can't control (a tornado, an airplane out of control) | Switch your attention to something totally different: pull weeds, play with your children, go to a movie. |
| What other people may think and say | Make sure what you plan to do is right, then do it. No one ever does anything worthwhile without being criticized. |
| Making an investment or buying a home | Analyze all factors, then be decisive: decide, stick with it, trust your own judgment. |
| Fear of people | Put them in proper perspective: the other person is just another human being much like yourself. |

### The memory bank

"Much lack of self-confidence can be traced directly to a mismanaged memory." The mind is a bank: every day you make thought deposits, and when you face a problem you ask it, "What do I already know about this?" Your memory is the raw material of new thought. **Mr. Teller** is perfectly obliging: ask him for proof you're inferior and he hands over every past failure and every overheard criticism; ask for reassurance and he hands over your past successes and the confidence others placed in you. "After all, it is your bank."

1. **Deposit only positive thoughts in your memory bank.** Unsuccessful people dwell on unpleasant situations and make them the last thing they think about at night; successful people "don't give it another thought." Negative thoughts are dirt in the crankcase of the mental motor. When alone (driving, eating alone), recall pleasant, positive experiences. Just before sleep, count your blessings: your spouse, children, friends, health, the good things you saw people do today, your little victories, the reasons you are glad to be alive.
2. **Withdraw only positive thoughts from your memory bank.** A psychological consultant said his services would be unnecessary if people would "destroy their negative thoughts before those thoughts become mental monsters." Clients run "their own private museum of mental horror": the "honeymoon monster," recalled hundreds of times for five or ten years; a job failure, a jilted romance, a bad investment, a disappointing teenager. "Any negative thought, if fertilized with repeated recall, can develop into a real mind monster." A depressed woman who remembered her whole life as unhappy, and saw a thunderstorm in an ambiguous sunrise-or-sunset painting, recovered when he helped her see her past in a different light and assigned her, each day, to write down **three specific reasons she has to be happy**. "What one remembers from the past colors what one sees in the present."

When you remember any situation, concentrate on its good part and bury the bad; if the mind turns to the negative side, turn it off. "Your mind wants you to forget the unpleasant": refuse to recall it and the teller cancels it out.

### Put people in proper perspective

A shy man lost his fear of people as an Army medic examining hundreds of naked recruits: whatever they had been days before, they looked alike, all confused and lonesome. "People are alike in many, many more ways than they are different."

1. **Get a balanced view of the other fellow.** He is important; so are you. Meeting anyone, think: "We're just two important people sitting down to discuss something of mutual interest and benefit." An executive hired an applicant because, unlike the half-scared applicants who act "a little like beggars," he respected the interviewer and himself and asked as many questions as he answered.
2. **Develop an understanding attitude.** It is the defense against the "adult bully." A hotel clerk took a public tongue-lashing from a guest who demanded a double room he hadn't ordered, then greeted the next guest warmly: "he really isn't mad at me. I was just the scapegoat... Underneath he's probably a very nice guy. Most folks are." When someone declares war on you, hold your fire; let him blow his stack and forget it. "Many people will bark, but it's a rare one who bites."

### Do what your conscience says is right

Paul W., a top student, cheated with a "pony," became convinced the professor had seen him (he hadn't), and wrote the worst exam in the class. The argument is practical, not moral: acting against conscience creates guilt that "jams your thought processes," because the mind keeps asking "Will I get caught?" A salesman who misleads a customer is self-conscious on the next call and loses the repeat sales; a man hiding an affair loses his grip at work and home; criminals are caught because they act guilty. Going against our desire to be right puts "a cancer in our conscience." Avoid anything that makes you ask "Will I get caught? Will they find out? Will I get away with it?" Doing wrong costs twice: the guilt eats confidence, and sooner or later others find out and lose confidence in you. "Do what's right and keep your confidence."

### To think confidently, act confidently

Read it "until it absolutely saturates you." George W. Crane: "motions are the precursors of emotions"; you control emotions only through your choice of actions ("You can't act devoted for very long without feeling devoted"). Make yourself smile and you feel like smiling; stand tall and you feel superior. **The shy-introduction cure:** do three things at once: clasp the other person's hand warmly, look directly at them, and say "I'm very glad to know you."

The five confidence-building exercises:

1. **Be a front seater.** Back seats fill first because people fear being conspicuous. Sit as close to the front as you can: "there is nothing inconspicuous about success."
2. **Practice making eye contact.** Avoiding eyes says "I feel inferior to you. I'm afraid of you" or "I feel guilty." Looking someone in the eye says "I'm honest and aboveboard... I'm confident." It gives you confidence and wins you confidence.
3. **Walk 25 percent faster.** Body action is the result of mind action. Down-and-outers shuffle; the average walk says "I really don't have very much pride in myself"; the superconfident walk faster, saying "I've got someplace important to go." Throw your shoulders back, lift your head, move a little faster.
4. **Practice speaking up.** The **conference clam** thinks his opinion worthless, says nothing, and promises himself "next time"; each silence is "one more dose of confidence poison." Speaking up is "a confidence-building vitamin." Rule: speak up voluntarily at *every* open meeting, committee and forum, no exception: comment, suggest or ask, and try to be the icebreaker, first in. You won't look foolish; for each person who disagrees another will agree. Concentrate on getting the leader's attention instead of asking "I wonder if I dare speak?" Consider a toastmaster's club.
5. **Smile big.** Test: try to feel defeated and smile big at the same time; you can't. A real smile also melts opposition (a driver who bumped Schwartz's car disarmed him with a smile and "Friend, I really didn't mean to do that"). A half-developed smile isn't guaranteed; smile until your teeth show. When you don't feel like it, tell yourself forcefully "I'm going to smile," then smile.

Chapter summary, the five procedures: action cures fear; put only positive thoughts in your memory bank; put people in proper perspective; practice doing what your conscience tells you is right; make everything about you say "I'm confident, really confident" (front seater, eye contact, walk 25 percent faster, speak up, smile big).

## You are what you think you are

Some people get the "Hey, Mac" or "Hey, buddy" treatment, others the sincere "Yes, sir." The explanation is thinking: "The fellow who thinks he is inferior, regardless of what his real qualifications may be, is inferior," and no bluff hides it long. "The person who feels he isn't important, *isn't*"; the one who thinks he is equal to the task, is. "To gain the respect of others, you must first think you deserve respect."

### Look important; it helps you think important

Rule: "your appearance 'talks.' Be sure it says positive things about you. Never leave home without feeling certain you look like the kind of person you want to be." Look at yourself through your superior's eyes and your associates' eyes.

- Appearance talks to you: "Your physical exterior affects your mental interior." A professor told students to dress up for finals: "Look sharp because it will help you think sharp." Schwartz's son couldn't "*think* like the Lone Ranger without a Lone Ranger hat." A salesman: "I can't feel prosperous... unless I know I look that way."
- Appearance talks to others: it is the *first* basis others have for evaluating you, and first impressions last. Identical grapes sold twice as fast in polyethylene bags at a higher price: "the better you are packaged, the more public acceptance you will receive." The well-dressed person's appearance says "Here is an important person: intelligent, prosperous, and dependable"; the shabby one's says careless, unimportant, "used to being pushed around."
- How can I afford clothes that make me feel right? **Pay twice as much and buy half as many.** Quality beats quantity, and you come out ahead: the garments last more than twice as long and show quality while they last, stay in style longer, and come with better advice from merchants who sell better goods.

### Think your work is important

The three bricklayers: "Laying brick," "Making $9.30 an hour," and "I'm building the world's greatest cathedral." Only the third moved up. Personnel selectors look for an applicant who thinks his present job is important even if he dislikes parts of it, having found "an amazingly close correlation between a person's job respect and his job performance."

- An appliance maker's personnel director interviewed some eight hundred nonproduction staff every six months. **Group B**, close to 80 percent, talked about security, retirement, sick leave, time off, overtime and disliked coworkers, and saw the job as "a sort of necessary evil." **Group A** wanted concrete suggestions for faster progress, expected nothing but a chance, and made suggestions for the business. Nearly every promotion went to group A; nearly every problem came from group B. A person can't be helped from B to A until he thinks his job is important.
- Self-talk to replace thinking you're weak or second-class: "I am important. I do have what it takes. I am a first-class performer. My work is important."
- Put yourself in a supervisor's shoes: the secretary who reads magazines while the boss is out, or the one who does the little things that help him on return; the employee who says "I can always get another job" or the one who takes criticism constructively; the salesman who says "I just do what they tell me" or "Mr. Brown, I'm here to help you"; the foreman who runs down the front office or the one who says "they'll do right by us." "Their thinking alone keeps them there."
- An agency starts college graduates in the mail room for exposure; one who finds it belittling is the wrong man. Executives answer "What would he do on that specific level?" by first answering "What kind of job is he doing where he is now?"
- The logic to read five times: a person who *thinks* his job is important receives mental signals on how to do it better, and a better job means more promotions, money, prestige and happiness.
- **Subordinates imitate the chief.** Children copy parents; adults copy leaders, down to slang, mannerisms, clothes and cars, and a nervous chief makes nervous staff. "*The way we think toward our jobs determines how our subordinates think toward their jobs.*" If you cheat the company on expenses, supplies or time, or arrive late, so will they; and superiors judge you by the output of those under you. To get others to do more: always show positive attitudes toward your job so subordinates pick up right thinking, and ask daily, "Am I worthy in every respect of being imitated? Are all my habits such that I would be glad to see them in my subordinates?"
- Enthusiasm is developed the same way: think enthusiastically, "this is great and I'm 100 percent for it," and others catch it.

### Give yourself a pep talk several times daily

A shy car salesman found Monday's sales-meeting inspiration gone by Tuesday, so he sat in a vacant car on the lot and told himself he was a good salesman who sold good cars at good deals to people who needed them. His calls changed at once; later he simply reminded himself silently before each dial. A trainee who froze in a talk on leadership had spent the five minutes beforehand telling himself he'd fail ("Who am I to be talking about being a leader?"). Sent to an empty room to repeat forcefully "I'm going to give a great talk. I've got something those people need to hear and I want to say," he gave a splendid speech. "Practice uplifting self-praise. Don't practice belittling self-punishment." "Think more of yourself and there is more of you."

**The "sell-yourself-to-yourself" commercial.** Coca-Cola resells you constantly because otherwise you'd grow cold to it; "half-alive people" are no longer sold on their most important product, themselves. Tom Staley carries a 60-second commercial in his billfold and reads it three times a day: addressed to himself by name, it tells him he's important, a big thinker who should think big about everything, able to do first-class work, a believer in happiness, progress and prosperity who should talk only those, full of drive and enthusiasm, great yesterday and greater today. To build yours:

1. Select your assets, your points of superiority: "What are my best qualities?" Don't be shy.
2. Put them on paper in your own words, written *to* you. Talk to yourself directly.
3. Practice it *out loud* in private at least once a day, before a mirror if possible, forcefully, with your body in it.
4. Read it silently several times every day: before anything that demands courage and whenever you feel let down. Keep it handy.

Many will "ho ho" at this because they don't believe success comes from managed thinking; ask the most successful person you know what he thinks of it.

### "How Am I Thinking?" checklist: think like important people think

| Situation | Ask yourself |
|---|---|
| When I worry | Would an important person worry about this? Would the most successful person I know be disturbed about this? |
| An idea | What would an important person do if he had this idea? |
| My appearance | Do I look like someone who has maximum self-respect? |
| My language | Am I using the language of successful people? |
| What I read | Would an important person read this? |
| Conversation | Is this something successful people would discuss? |
| When I lose my temper | Would an important person get mad at what I'm mad at? |
| My jokes | Is this the kind of joke an important person would tell? |
| My job | How does an important person describe his job to others? |

Cement the master question in mind: "Is this the way an important person does it?" Then obey the answer.
