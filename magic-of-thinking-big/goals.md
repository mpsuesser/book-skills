# Use goals to help you grow

All progress is first visualized. "A goal is an objective, a purpose. A goal is more than a dream; it's a dream being acted upon... a clear 'This is what I'm working toward.'" Nothing happens until a goal is set. "Goals are as essential to success as air is to life." Dave Mahoney, who went from the mail room to agency vice president at twenty-seven and Good Humor president at thirty-three: "The important thing is not where you were or where you are but where you want to get."

## Plan ten years ahead

A progressive corporation plans ten to fifteen years ahead ("Where do we want our company to be ten years from now?") and builds plant capacity for five or ten years out. Do the same: "form an image now of the person you want to be ten years from now." Think of yourself as a business unit whose talents, skills and abilities are its "products."

F. B., four years out of college, said "I don't know what I want to do." Trial and error, Schwartz told him, carries odds of several dozen to one against him. He asked: "Will you describe for me your image of yourself ten years from now?" You can't walk up to an airline counter and say "Give me a ticket"; you need a destination. "Before you start out, know where you want to go."

Two steps:

1. **Visualize your future in three departments: work, home and social.** Planning all three prevents confusion and conflict and shows the whole picture.
2. **Demand clear, precise answers** to: What do I want to accomplish with my life? What do I want to be? What does it take to satisfy me?

**Ten-Year Planning Guide: "An Image of Me, 10 Years from Now"**

Picture yourself ten years from now and write a specific answer under each heading:

- **A. Work department:** the income level you want to reach, the level of responsibility you seek, the authority you want to command, and the prestige you expect your work to bring.
- **B. Home department:** the standard of living you want for your family and yourself, the kind of house, the kind of vacations, and the financial support you want to give your children in their early adult years.
- **C. Social department:** the kinds of friends you want, the social groups you want to join, the community leadership positions you would like to hold, and the worthwhile causes you want to champion.

Schwartz and his young son built a doghouse for their pup Peanut with no plan; a friend looked at the result: "Why didn't you get a plan? Nobody these days builds a doghouse without a blueprint." "Don't be afraid to be blue sky. People these days are measured by the size of their dreams. No one accomplishes more than he sets out to accomplish." One trainee wrote a life plan in vivid detail: a Southern-manor country estate with fishponds, Doberman kennels and a tree-lined drive; active church membership; a family cruise around the world, or four or five separate vacations, which he knew depended on his work department. Five years later his two dimestores had become five and he had bought seventeen acres.

## Surrender to desire

The departments are interrelated, but work has the most influence, as it did for the caveman hunter. A McKinsey Foundation study found the most important qualification for an executive is "sheer desire to get ahead." Wanamaker: "A man is not doing much until the cause he works for possesses all there is of him." "Desire, when harnessed, is power."

Dan wrote for his college paper but avoided journalism ("dime a dozen... don't want to starve") and ended up a dissatisfied assistant personnel director in New Orleans: "my heart isn't in it." Success takes heart-and-soul effort, and you give that only to something you really desire. Switching to what you like is like putting a five-hundred-horsepower motor in a ten-year-old car.

Instead of surrendering to desire, people murder it with the **five weapons of success suicide**:

1. **Self-depreciation:** "I lack brains," "I'd fail," "I lack the education and/or experience."
2. **Security-itis:** "I've got security where I am."
3. **Competition:** "The field is already overcrowded."
4. **Parental dictation:** a parent steering a career. Patiently explain your reasons, and as a parent listen, since both want the same thing: success.
5. **Family responsibility:** "Now I've got a family and I can't change."

"Surrender to desire and gain energy, enthusiasm, mental zip, and even better health." It is never too late.

**Goals supply energy.** Successful people work well over forty hours without complaint. "Energy increases, multiplies, when you set a desired goal and resolve to work toward that goal." "Goals cure boredom. Goals even cure many chronic ailments."

**Automatic instrumentation.** A goal you surrender to works into your subconscious, which is always in balance; without one the conscious mind is hesitant and confused, and with one you react right automatically. Tom pictures himself a corporation vice president in ten years; the goal tells him "do this" and "don't do that," guides his suit purchases, his next steps, what to say in a conference or a conflict, what to read and what stands to take, and alerts him when he drifts off course. Jack, with no goal and no personal policy, wavers, guesses and flounders. The most successful people's lives are "integrated around a purpose." Let your goal obsess you.

**Deadlines.** A Saturday with no plan accomplishes nothing: "to accomplish something, we must plan to accomplish something." The atomic bomb came from a crash program with a target date. Set goals as deadlines, target dates and self-imposed quotas. "You will accomplish only what you plan to accomplish."

**Goals keep you alive.** Dr. George E. Burch of Tulane, a longevity researcher: "The quickest way to the end is to retire and do nothing. Every human being must keep an interest in life just to keep living." Retiring to "eat, sleep, and rock" is a way to poison yourself fast; Lew Gordon, a retired Atlanta bank vice president, said "I'm going to pitch right in and start fast," became a business consultant and speaker, helped build Pi Sigma Epsilon, and stayed "young thirty in spirit." Mrs. D. developed cancer when her son was two, three months after being widowed; her goal was to see him through college with a small store. Given "a few more months," she lived twenty years and died six weeks after his graduation. No medicine is as powerful as "the desire to do something."

## One step at a time

Progress is made one brick, one play, one customer at a time. Eric Sevareid, after parachuting onto the Burma-India border, made a 140-mile trek with a nail through his boot and blistered feet by aiming only at the next ridge, the next friendly village: the principle of the **"next mile."** He wrote a quarter-million-word book by thinking only of the next paragraph, and over two thousand broadcast scripts by thinking only of the next one.

The hour-by-hour method to quit smoking: resolve not to smoke for another hour; renew it; extend to two hours, then a day. "An hour is easy; forever is difficult." A junior executive treats each assignment as a step; a salesman qualifies for management one sale at a time; each sermon, lecture, experiment or conference is a step. "Sudden" successes had groundwork; fast-fading ones were phonies without a foundation. Make each next task a step in the right direction, and evaluate everything with one question: "Will this help take me where I want to go?" If no, back off; if yes, press ahead. Set monthly quotas.

**Thirty-Day Improvement Guide.** "Between now and ___ I will":

- **A. Break these habits** (suggestions): putting off things; negative language; watching TV more than sixty minutes a day; gossip.
- **B. Acquire these habits:** a rigid morning examination of my appearance; plan each day's work the night before; compliment people at every possible opportunity.
- **C. Increase my value to my employer:** do a better job of developing subordinates; learn more about my company, what it does and its customers; make three specific suggestions for improving efficiency.
- **D. Increase my value to my home:** show more appreciation for the little things my wife does that I've taken for granted; once each week do something special with the whole family; give one hour each day of undivided attention to my family.
- **E. Sharpen my mind:** spend two hours weekly reading professional magazines; read one self-help book; make four new friends; spend thirty minutes daily in quiet, undisturbed thinking.

At the end of thirty days, check your progress and build a new guide. The poised, effective people you admire weren't born that way.

**Take detours in stride.** When the sign says "road closed," find another road. Military planners have plan A and plan B; pilots carry alternate fields and reserve fuel. "When we detour, we don't have to change our goals. We just travel a different route."

## Invest in yourself

**Self-investment** is "the biggest and most rewarding kind of investment": buying what builds mental power and proficiency. "Profit comes from only one source: investment."

1. **Invest in education.** Education isn't years or diplomas: Ralph Cordiner noted that GE presidents Wilson and Coffin never went to college and that 12 of 41 officers had no degree; "interested in competency, not diplomas." Nor is it a quantity of stored facts, which books, files and machines warehouse better. "How well educated a person is, is measured by how well his mind is developed—in brief, by how well he thinks." Use nearby colleges; one evening class of twenty-five held the owner of a twelve-store chain, two national food-chain buyers, four engineers and an Air Force colonel. "School: One Night a Week for Life."
2. **Invest in idea starters.** Buy at least one stimulating book each month and subscribe to two magazines or journals that stress ideas. A reader of *The Wall Street Journal*: "I've found that I can't afford not to take it."

Recap: (1) get a clear fix on where you want to go, an image of yourself ten years from now; (2) write out your ten-year plan in its work, home and social departments; (3) surrender yourself to your desires, and set goals to get energy and to get things done; (4) let your major goal be your automatic pilot; (5) achieve your goal one step at a time; (6) build thirty-day goals; (7) take detours in stride; a detour should never mean surrendering the goal; (8) invest in yourself: education and idea starters.
