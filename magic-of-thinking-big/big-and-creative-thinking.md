# Big thinking and creative thinking

People "are not measured in inches or pounds or college degrees, or family background; they are measured by the size of their thinking." Recruiters find many top graduates more interested in retirement plans than opportunity, defining success as security; so many think small that there is "much less competition than you think."

## Don't sell yourself short

**Self-deprecation** is probably the greatest human weakness. John skips the job he wants ("I'm not good enough"), Jim doesn't call Joan, Tom won't call a prospect he thinks too big to see him, Pete writes a modest salary on the application. People read "Know thyself" as "*Know only thy negative self*" and make long lists of their faults. Knowing your inabilities shows where to improve, but if that is all you know, "our value is small."

**Exercise to measure your true size:**

1. Determine your five chief assets, with help from an objective, intelligent friend (spouse, superior, professor). Typical assets: education, experience, technical skills, appearance, well-adjusted home life, attitudes, personality, initiative.
2. Under each asset, write the names of three persons you know who have achieved large success but do *not* have this asset to as great a degree as you.

You will find you outrank many successful people on at least one asset. "You're bigger than you think." Fit your thinking to your true size.

## The big thinker's vocabulary

Big words are not the point: the man who says "adamantine" for "immovable" is usually a stuffed shirt and a small thinker. What counts is "the effect his words and phrases have on his own and others' thinking." "*We do not think in words and phrases. We think only in pictures and/or images.*" Speaking or writing, you are "a projector showing movies in the minds of others." "We've failed" shows defeat; "Here's a new approach that I think will work" encourages. "Problem" pictures something unpleasant, "challenge" something like sport; "expense" is money gone, "investment" returns profits. "*To think big, we must use words and phrases that produce big, positive mental images.*"

Small, negative phrasing and its big, positive version:

| Small, negative | Big, positive |
|---|---|
| It's no use, we're whipped. | We're not whipped yet. Let's keep trying. Here's a new angle. |
| I was in that business once and failed. Never again. | I went broke but it was my own fault. I'm going to try again. |
| I've tried but the product won't sell. | So far I've not been able to sell it. But I know it is good and I'm going to find the formula. |
| The market is saturated: 75 percent already sold. Better get out. | 25 percent of the market is still not sold. Count me in. |
| Their orders have been small. Cut them off. | Let's map out a plan for selling them more of their needs. |
| Five years is too long to reach the top ranks. | That leaves me thirty years to serve at a high level. |
| The competition has all the advantage. | No one ever has *all* the advantages. Let's figure a way to beat them at their own game. |
| Nobody will ever want that product. | In its present form it may not be salable; let's consider some modifications. |
| Let's wait for a recession, then buy stocks. | Let's invest now. Bet on prosperity, not depression. |
| I'm too young (old) for the job. | Being young (old) is a distinct advantage. |
| It won't work, let me prove it. (Image: dark, gloom, failure.) | It will work, let me prove it. (Image: bright, hope, victory.) |

**Four ways to develop the big thinker's vocabulary:**

1. **Use big, positive, cheerful words to describe how you feel.** Answer every "How are you?" with "Just *wonderful*! thanks, and *you*?" or "Great." "I'm tired" makes you feel worse. Become known as a person who always feels great.
2. **Use bright, cheerful, favorable words to describe other people.** Have a big, positive word for every absent third party ("He's really a *fine* fellow"). Avoid "the petty cut-him-down language": third parties hear of it, and it cuts *you* down.
3. **Use positive language to encourage others.** Compliment people at every opportunity: appearance, work, achievements, families; a good word for your spouse every day. "Praise, sincerely administered, is a success tool."
4. **Use positive words to outline plans to others.** "Here is some *good* news. We face a genuine opportunity" makes minds sparkle; "Whether we like it or not, we've got a job to do" makes them dull. Promise victory and win support. "Build castles, don't dig graves!"

## See what can be, not just what is

Big thinkers "train themselves to see not just what is but what can be." "Visualization adds value to everything."

- **What gives real estate value?** A rural Realtor spent two days on a run-down farm, walking it, studying neighbors and highways, asking "What's this farm good for?", and wrote three plans: a riding stable, a tree farm, a tree-and-poultry farm. He sells faster and higher than competitors because "people naturally pay more for acreage *and* an idea."
- **How much is a customer worth?** A clerk refused to waste time on "a $1.98 customer." The store's research showed the typical customer spent $362 a year; once supervisors valued customers on an annual basis, service improved. "It's repeat business that makes the profit." A cafeteria checker who insisted on 49 cents for turkey marked 39 lost a student's $250 a year of lunches: she saw one thin dime.
- **The blind milkman** skipped a household using one quart every two days, not noticing a baby on the way; another milkman now sells them seven quarts every two days. The teacher who sees the unruly child as he can be gets results; rehabilitation workers on skid row see a reconstructed citizen.
- **What determines how much you're worth?** A twenty-six-year-old rate clerk with under $200, an old car and a cramped apartment was "determined not to let what I haven't got stop me": he pictured himself as the executive in the suburban home he would be in a few years, and felt and thought bigger. "It isn't what one has that's important. Rather, it's how much one is planning to get that counts." "The price tag the world puts on us is just about identical to the one we put on ourselves."

**The "practice adding value" exercises:**

1. **Add value to things.** Ask, "What can I do to 'add value' to this room or this house or this business?" A thing "has value in proportion to the ideas for using it."
2. **Add value to people.** The higher you rise, the more your job is "people development." Ask, "What can I do to 'add value' to my subordinates?" "To bring out the best in a person, you must first visualize his best."
3. **Add value to yourself.** Hold a daily interview with yourself: "What can I do to make myself more valuable today?" Visualize yourself as you can be, and specific ways will suggest themselves.

**Get the big view of your job.** Harry, hired at twenty-six as a printing company's accountant, was its president five years later because he took an interest in the whole company: a fringe-benefit program that cut turnover, a cost study showing a $30,000 machinery investment would pay, ideas for the sales department during a slump, help for every new employee. He was no meddler, backstabber or order-giver; "he made company business his business." "I'm doing my job and that's enough" and "that's no concern of mine" are small thinking. See the company's interest as identical with your own. The next promotion depends mostly on how you think toward your *present* job.

## Think above trivial things

- **Speeches:** most people prepare a list of petty don'ts (stand straight, hide the notes, mind the grammar, straighten the tie) and flop. The big things are "*knowledge of what he's going to talk about and an intense desire to tell it to other people.*" The real test is whether the audience got the points.
- **Quarrels:** at least 99 percent start over petty matters, like a complaint about dinner that escalates to money, in-laws and old promises. Before complaining, accusing, reprimanding or counterattacking, ask "**Is it really important?**" (the cap off the toothpaste, coming home late, a driver cutting in).
- **The smallest office:** J. M., given the smallest of four new offices, felt discriminated against, grew resentful, undermined colleagues and was fired within three months. The company was short of space and the person assigning offices may not have known which was smallest. "No one in the organization, except J. M., regarded his office as an index of his value." The same goes for your name last on the route sheet or the fourth carbon of a memo.
- **Even stuttering is a detail:** a stuttering applicant, asked why he could sell, said he learned fast, liked people, liked the company and wanted to make money, and that his impairment didn't bother him, "so why should it bother anybody else?" He was hired and did well.

Three procedures:

1. **Keep your eyes focused on the big objective.** In selling it is sales, not winning arguments ("Yes, but I sure convinced the customer he was wrong"); in marriage, peace and happiness, not "I could have told you so"; with employees, developing their full potential, not their minor errors; with neighbors, respect and friendship, not impounding the dog. "Better to lose a battle and win the war."
2. **Ask "Is it really important?"** or "Is it important enough for me to get all worked up about?" At least 90 percent of quarrels and feuds would never happen.
3. **Don't fall into the triviality trap.** In speeches, problems and counseling, think of the things that make the difference, not surface issues.

## Test: measure the size of your thinking

| Situation | Petty thinker | Big thinker |
|---|---|---|
| Expense accounts | Raises income by chiseling on expense accounts | Raises income by selling more |
| Conversation | Talks about the negative qualities of friends, economy, company, competition | Talks about their positive qualities |
| Progress | Believes in retrenchment or at best the status quo | Believes in expansion |
| Future | Views it as limited | Sees it as very promising |
| Work | Looks for ways to avoid work | Looks for more things to do, especially helping others |
| Competition | Competes with the average | Competes with the best |
| Budget problems | Saves by cutting necessary items | Increases income and buys more necessary items |
| Goals | Sets goals low | Sets goals high |
| Goals vision | Sees only the short run | Is preoccupied with the long run |
| Security | Is preoccupied with security problems | Regards security as a natural companion of success |
| Companionship | Surrounds himself with petty thinkers | Surrounds himself with persons with large, progressive ideas |
| Mistakes | Magnifies minor errors into big issues | Ignores errors of little consequence |

Chapter summary: don't sell yourself short ("conquer the crime of self-deprecation"); use the big thinker's vocabulary; stretch your vision and add value to things, people and yourself; get the big view of your job; think above trivial things.

## Creative thinking

Creative thinking is not reserved for science, art or the superintelligent. A low-income family plans a son's university education; a family turns the street's worst lot into its beauty spot; a minister doubles Sunday evening attendance; someone simplifies record keeping, sells the "impossible" customer, keeps the children constructively busy or prevents a "certain" quarrel. "Creative thinking is simply finding new, improved ways to do anything."

### Step one: believe it can be done

"To do *anything*, we must first believe it can be done." **The jails experiment:** Schwartz asks a group whether jails could be eliminated in thirty years. For ten minutes they "prove" it impossible. Then he asks them to believe it can be done, just for the experiment, and asks "how could we begin?" Ideas start slowly and then pour out (youth centers, ending poverty, spotting potential criminals early, training police in reform); he has tabulated seventy-eight. "*When you believe something is impossible, your mind goes to work for you to prove why. But when you believe, really believe, something can be done, your mind goes to work for you and helps you find the ways to do it.*" Leaders who don't believe in permanent peace, and economists who think depressions inevitable, close their minds to the creative solutions. The same holds for liking a person, solving a personal problem or buying a larger home. "Belief releases creative powers. Disbelief puts the brakes on."

A twenty-four-year-old credit clerk, three semesters short of a degree, with a second child coming and no money, called going back "impossible." Schwartz told him to make up his mind he would go back, let that thought dominate, think how to do it while supporting his family, and return in two weeks. He found a trade-association scholarship, rearranged his hours, won his wife's support, budgeted with her, and graduated into a management-trainee job.

1. **Eliminate the word *impossible* from your thinking and speaking vocabularies.** "*Impossible* is a failure word"; it sets off a chain of thoughts proving you right.
2. **Think of something special you've been wanting to do but felt you couldn't. Now make a list of reasons why you can do it.**

### Fight traditional thinking

A writer proposed merging counties laid out in horse-and-buggy days, which would give better service for less money; not one of thirty people he asked saw merit in it. "The traditional thinker's mind is paralyzed": "It's been this way for a hundred years... Why risk a change?" Status-quo-ers opposed the automobile and the airplane. A 1900 "scientific" sales principle (find the one best way to sell, never deviate) nearly ruined its company. "There is no one best way to do anything... There are as many best ways as there are creative minds." "Nothing grows in ice."

**The tradition-paralysis test:** propose an idea such as turning the postal system over to private enterprise, holding presidential elections every two or six years, retail hours of 1 to 8 P.M., or raising the retirement age to seventy, and watch the reaction. Soundness is not the point. About 95 percent will laugh it off; the one in twenty who says "That's an interesting idea; tell me more about it" has a mind turned to creativity.

"Traditional thinking is personal enemy number one" for a creative success program. Three ways to fight it:

1. **Become receptive to ideas.** Destroy the thought repellents "Won't work," "Can't be done," "It's useless," and "It's stupid." An insurance executive called himself "the best sponge in the insurance industry."
2. **Be an experimental person.** Break up fixed routines: new restaurants, books, theaters, friends, a different route to work, a different vacation. If you work in distribution, learn production, accounting and finance; it prepares you for larger responsibility.
3. **Be progressive, not regressive.** Not "That's the way we did it where I used to work" but "How can we do it better than we did it where I used to work?"

### Ask yourself daily, "How can I do better?"

Absolute perfection is unattainable, so there is endless room for improvement. The successful person doesn't ask "Can I do it better?"; he knows he can, so he asks "*How* can I do it better?"

A former student opened her fourth hardware store in four years on $3,500 of starting capital, crediting her "**weekly improvement program**": she divided her job into four elements (customers, employees, merchandise, promotion), jotted down improvement ideas all week, and every Monday evening spent four hours reviewing them and working out how to use the solid ones, asking questions like "What can I do to attract more customers?" and "How can I develop regular, loyal customers?" It produced her store layouts, a suggestion-selling method that sold two of three customers something unplanned, a credit plan during a strike, a slack-season contest, and a line of carded toys that brought in children and their parents. "It isn't so much what you know when you start that matters. It's what you learn and put to use after you open your doors that counts most." Make progress your most important product.

**Daily exercise:** before work each day, spend ten minutes thinking "How can I do a better job today?" Ask, "What can I do today to encourage my employees?" "What special favor can I do for my customers?" "How can I increase my personal efficiency?"

### Ask yourself, "How can I do more?" Capacity is a state of mind

Mrs. S., who "simply haven't got time" for a job, had to work full-time after her husband was disabled, and found her efficiency up 100 percent: many tasks didn't need doing, the children could and wanted to help, and she cut store trips, TV and telephoning. "*Capacity is a state of mind.* How much we can do depends on how much we think we can do."

A young bank executive took on a departed colleague's whole job when two peers pleaded overload. That evening he wrote down every efficiency idea he could: routine calls channeled to one hour, outgoing calls placed in another, conferences cut from fifteen minutes to ten, all dictation at one time, details handed to his secretary. Within a week he was handling twice the letters, 50 percent more calls and half again as many meetings; the jobs were combined under him with a raise. The employee who says "I'm just too busy" is not held against; the boss simply keeps looking until he finds someone equally busy who takes it on, and that one forges ahead.

The success combination: *do what you do better* (quality) and *do more of what you do* (quantity). Two steps:

1. **Eagerly accept the opportunity to do more.** Being asked is a compliment; accepting makes you stand out. Accept civic assignments too.
2. **Concentrate on "How can I do more?"** The answers come: better planning and organization, intelligent shortcuts, dropping nonessentials.

"If you want it done, give it to a busy man." People with plenty of free time make ineffective partners. Ask, "What can I do to expand my output?"

### Practice asking and listening

"The bigger the person, the more apt he is to encourage *you* to talk; the smaller the person, the more apt he is to preach to you." **Big people monopolize the listening; small people monopolize the talking.** Top leaders spend more time requesting advice than giving it: "How do you feel about it?" "What do you recommend?" "What would you do under these circumstances?" A leader is "a decision-making human machine" whose raw material is other people's ideas, not as ready-made answers but as sparks. A vice president, due to tell a seminar how he'd solved his hardest problem, instead presented it, asked for ideas and had a stenographer record them, to "harvest some ideas." "To sell John Brown what John Brown buys, you've got to see things through John Brown's eyes." "Your ears are your intake valves." "We learn nothing from telling."

The three-stage program:

1. **Encourage others to talk.** Draw them out: "Tell me about your experience..." "What do you think should be done about...?" "What do you think is the key point?" You gain raw material and friends at once.
2. **Test your own views in the form of questions.** Use the what-do-you-think-of-this-suggestion? approach. Don't announce a fresh idea "as if it were handed down on a gold tablet"; do a little informal research and end up with a better idea.
3. **Concentrate on what the other person says.** Listening means letting it penetrate, not waiting for a pause to take over. Evaluate it; that is how you collect mind food.

### Stretch your mind: get stimulated

Executive programs pay off less in formulas than in the *stimulation* of exchanging ideas; a sales manager came back from a one-week school and made a series of changes, none of them taught there. An accountant, invited to a real-estate luncheon outside his field, heard a forecast of demand for "gentlemen-size farms" of two to five acres, asked friends whether they'd want one, then bought a worn-out fifty-acre farm for $8,500 (one-third down), planted pine seedlings because "people want trees," surveyed ten five-acre plots and sold them by direct mail to young executives at $3,000 each. Working evenings and weekends he sold all ten in six weeks: $30,000 income, $10,400 costs, $19,600 profit.

Two ways to build stimulation into your life:

1. **Join and meet regularly with at least one professional group in your own occupational area.** "A mind that feeds only on itself soon is undernourished."
2. **Join and participate in at least one group outside your occupational interests.** It broadens your thinking and stimulates your on-the-job thinking.

### Harness and develop your ideas

An oak drops enough acorns for a forest, and the squirrels eat most of them. "Ideas are highly perishable," and the squirrels are negative-thinking people.

1. **Don't let ideas escape. Write them down.** "Memory is a weak slave" for new ideas. Carry a notebook or cards; a traveler keeps a clipboard beside him. "Fence them in."
2. **Review your ideas.** Keep an active file (a cabinet, a drawer, a shoe box) and go through it regularly. Discard what has no value; keep anything with promise.
3. **Cultivate and fertilize your idea.** Think about it, tie it to related ideas, read anything akin to it, investigate all angles, and put it to work when the time is ripe.

**Shape up the idea on paper**, as the architect makes a preliminary drawing, the ad writer a storyboard, the writer a first draft. On paper you can see its loopholes, and ideas must be "sold" to someone (customers, employees, the boss, investors). Of two insurance salesmen, the one who talked taxes and options lost Schwartz; the one who charted his recommendations sold him. "An idea written or in some sort of picture or diagram form has many times more selling power than the idea presented only in oral form."

Chapter summary: believe it can be done, and drop "impossible," "won't work," "can't do," "no use trying"; don't let tradition paralyze your mind; ask daily "How can I do better?"; ask "How can I do more?"; practice asking and listening; stretch your mind by mixing with people of different occupational and social interests.
