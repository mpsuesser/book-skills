# People and leadership

Two chapters: think right toward people, and how to think like a leader. Both rest on one rule: "Success depends on the support of other people." Today a person "either supports you willingly or he doesn't support you at all."

## Think right toward people

The executive, salesman, dean, politician, writer and chain-store magnate all depend on the support of others; it is the only hurdle between you and what you want. The answer is to **think right toward people**. When selection committees discuss candidates, "in at least nine cases out of ten, the 'likability' factor is the first thing mentioned," weighted far more than technical skill, even for professorships ("Will he fit in?" "Will students like him?"). "A person is not pulled up to a higher-level job. Rather, he is lifted up." "Every friend you make lifts you just one notch higher. And being likable makes you **lighter to lift**."

Successful people have a plan, sometimes written, for liking people. Lyndon Johnson's ten-point formula, in substance:

1. Learn to remember names; inefficiency here may mean your interest isn't outgoing enough.
2. Be a comfortable person, an old-shoe kind of individual, so there is no strain in being with you.
3. Acquire a relaxed, easygoing quality so things don't ruffle you.
4. Don't be egotistical; guard against giving the impression you know it all.
5. Cultivate being interesting, so people get something of value from being with you.
6. Study to get the "scratchy" elements out of your personality, even ones you're unaware of.
7. Sincerely try to heal, on an honest basis, every misunderstanding you have had or now have. Drain off your grievances.
8. Practice liking people until you learn to do so genuinely.
9. Never miss an opportunity to congratulate anyone's achievement, or to express sympathy in sorrow or disappointment.
10. Give spiritual strength to people, and they will give you genuine affection.

There is no get-even attitude in it, no let-the-other-fellow-come-to-me, no I-know-it-all.

**Don't try to buy friendship; it's not for sale.** A gift given without sincerity is a payoff or a bribe. A trucking-company president returned a case of liquor from a tire-recapping firm he had dropped for poor work, whose salesman told him to "tell Santa to be real good to you." Buying friendship loses two ways: you waste money, and you create contempt.

### Take the initiative in building friendships; leaders always do

"Let him make the first move" is the average person's rule. "The most important person present is the one person most active in introducing himself" ("Hello, I'm Jack R."). A friend: "I may not be very important to him, but he's important to me." In an elevator experiment Schwartz made a pleasant remark to twenty-five consecutive strangers and got twenty-five positive responses. A pleasant word makes the stranger one degree better and pays you back, like warming up a car on a cold morning.

Six ways to win friends by initiative:

1. Introduce yourself to others at every possible opportunity: parties, meetings, airplanes, at work.
2. Be sure the other person gets your name straight.
3. Pronounce his name the way he pronounces it.
4. Write his name down, and be sure you spell it correctly (people have a "fetish" about it); get his address and phone number if possible.
5. Drop a personal note or phone new friends you want to know better. Most successful people follow through this way.
6. Say pleasant things to strangers.

Mr. Average waits. "Don't be afraid to be unusual."

### Accept human differences

Ted, a sales applicant, disliked people because they fell short of perfection: their grammar, messy cigarette habits, poor taste in clothes. Schwartz's three suggestions:

1. **Recognize that no person is perfect.** The "most human quality" of people is that they make mistakes.
2. **Recognize that the other fellow has a right to be different.** "Never play God." Don't dislike people for their habits, clothes, religion, parties or automobiles: "You don't have to approve... but you must not dislike him for doing it."
3. **Don't be a reformer.** Live and let live; people dislike being told "you're wrong," so sometimes keep your opinion to yourself.

Ted later found the old annoyances amusing. "No person is all good and no person is all bad."

### Channel P and Channel N

The mind is a mental broadcasting station with two equally powerful channels: **Channel P** (positive) and **Channel N** (negative). When Mr. Jacobs reviewed an employee's work with compliments plus suggestions, Channel N broadcast "Jacobs is out to get you... fight back"; Channel P broadcast "Those suggestions he made to me seem pretty sound... The old boy did me a favor. Tomorrow I'll go in and thank him for his constructive help." The longer you are tuned to one channel, the harder it is to switch, and one thought sets off a chain reaction (his accent leads to his politics, religion, car, wife, haircut). Manage your thought broadcasting station: make Channel P your listening habit. When Channel N cuts in, say "stop," and switch channels by thinking of one positive quality about the person; that sets off a positive chain.

Others will try to switch you: a coworker on another employee, a neighbor on someone's domestic problems, a customer on a competitor. "Thoughts breed like thoughts," and you risk adding "Yes, and that's not all. Did you hear..." Two ways to block it:

1. **Switch topics quickly and quietly:** "Pardon me, John, but while I think of it, I've been meaning to ask you..."
2. **Excuse yourself:** "Sorry, John, I'm late now," or a deadline.

Refuse to let others prejudice your thinking about a third person.

### Like the person you want to influence

Nine of ten insurance prospects believe they don't need more insurance; the author of a book on selling insurance couldn't sell it. A top agent's secret: "I like, really like the guy I'm selling." Pretending fails ("You can't even fool a dog"; insincerity spells p-h-o-n-y). Along with the usual facts, he gathers "some sound reasons why I can like the prospect," reviews them whenever he thinks of the prospect, and builds a likable image before saying a word about insurance, so the meeting ends with both on the same side of the table. A difficult prospect told him "don't ever come back"; he held eye contact for five seconds and said softly, "But Mr. S., I'm calling tonight as your friend." The man bought a $250,000 endowment policy.

Sol Polk, the appliance king of Chicago: customers "should be treated like they are guests in my home." Substitute employees for customers and it works as well. "Think first class about everyone around you." Two consultants spent a forty-minute drive mocking a soft-drink client ("the only thing holding up Mr. F. is fat"; his son's only qualification was "he speaks English"); the conference went cold, a two-sentence letter canceled the account, and the client hired an out-of-town firm. "Printing ourselves with negative thoughts for only forty minutes cost us a contract."

### Practice conversation generosity

The two-day experiment: in every group, note who talks most and who is most successful. It is "rarely the same person." **Conversation generosity** means encouraging the other person to talk about himself: his views, accomplishments, family, job, problems. It wins friends, and it teaches you about people, which you need to influence them, since "the average person would rather talk about himself than anything else." A New York agency puts its copywriters behind store counters one week a year to listen to buyers; companies hold "terminal interviews" with departing employees; sales managers prefer the salesman who listens and asks questions. "Don't be a conversation hog. Listen, win friends, and learn."

**Courtesy is "the finest tranquilizer."** A major cause of stress is negative feelings toward other people; practice courtesy all the time.

### When things don't go your way

The real test of thinking right toward people comes when you are passed over, lose a club office or are criticized. "How you think when you lose determines how long it will be until you win." Benjamin Fairless, of U.S. Steel, never hated a teacher (discipline was his own fault), liked every boss, and did more than was expected; when passed over he never blamed "office politics," reasoned that the other person deserved it, and asked "What could I do to make myself deserving of the next opportunity?" He never berated himself. Two things to do:

1. Ask, "What can I do to make myself more deserving of the next opportunity?"
2. Don't waste time and energy being discouraged or berating yourself. Plan to win next time.

Chapter capsule: (1) make yourself lighter to lift; (2) take the initiative in building friendships; (3) accept human differences and limitations; don't be a reformer; (4) tune in Channel P, the Good Thoughts Station, and don't let others prejudice your thinking about a third person; (5) practice conversation generosity; (6) practice courtesy all the time; (7) don't blame others when you receive a setback.

## How to think like a leader

A leader is not pulled up but "lifted" by the people beside and below him. Leadership is getting others to do things they wouldn't do if they weren't led. Four rules work anywhere: the executive suite, social clubs, home.

1. **Trade minds** with the people you want to influence.
2. **Think: What is the human way to handle this?**
3. **Think progress**, believe in progress, push for progress.
4. **Take time out to confer with yourself** and tap your supreme thinking power.

### Rule 1: trade minds

Ted B.'s TV commercials for children's shoes were loved by 4 percent of viewers ("one of the best"), people like Ted in income, education and sophistication; 96 percent were indifferent or hated them ("wacky," the kids go to the bathroom or the refrigerator, "too uppity up," "too clever"). He should have asked, "If I were a parent, what kind of a commercial would make me want to buy those shoes?" and "If I were a child, what kind of a commercial would make me go tell my Mom or Dad that I want those shoes?" Joan, twenty-four, an assistant buyer for a low-to-medium-price store, from a well-to-do home, bought what she liked ("Oh, they'll love this. I do") and was gone in eight months. "To get others to do what you want them to do, you must see things through their eyes."

- A credit executive rewrote insulting collection letters into ones that would move him to pay if he owed, and collections hit a record.
- A political candidate lost using a vocabulary few voters understood; his opponent spoke the language of farmers and factory workers, addressing himself "to Mr. Typical Voter, not to Dr. College Professor."
- A fuse that never blows, priced at $1.25, was launched in a mass campaign expecting ten million sales and was dead in six weeks. It should have gone to upper-income buyers through selected magazines; the executive saw it through high-income eyes, not the mass market's.

The trading-minds question: "What would I think of this if I exchanged places with the other person?"

Practice trading minds:

| Situation | Ask yourself |
|---|---|
| Giving work instructions | Looking at this from the viewpoint of someone who is new to this, have I made myself clear? |
| Writing an advertisement | If I were a typical prospective buyer, how would I react to this ad? |
| Telephone manners | If I were the other person, what would I think of my telephone voice and manners? |
| A gift | Is this gift something I would like, or is it something he will like? |
| The way I give orders | Would I like to carry out orders if they were given to me the way I give them to others? |
| Child discipline | If I were the child, considering his age, experience and emotions, how would I react to this discipline? |
| My appearance | What would I think of my superior if he were dressed like me? |
| Preparing a speech | Considering the background and interests of the audience, what would I think of this remark? |
| Entertainment | If I were my guests, what kinds of food, music and entertainment would I like best? |

The procedure:

1. Consider the other person's situation: his interests, income, intelligence and background may differ from yours.
2. Ask, "If I were in his situation, how would I react to this?"
3. Then take the action that would move you if you were the other person.

### Rule 2: be human

Three ways to lead:

- **The dictator** decides without consulting anyone and won't hear subordinates' side, for fear of losing face. He gets faked loyalty; the best people leave and the rest plot.
- **The cold, mechanical "rule-book operator"** goes by the book in everything, though every rule is only a guide for the usual case. People hate being treated as machines, and "machines" develop only part of their energy.
- **Being human.** John S., an aluminum-industry engineering head, helped a transferee find housing, held office birthday parties (thirty minutes, an investment), honored employees' minority-faith holidays, remembered illnesses, and complimented people on off-the-job accomplishments. When he had to dismiss an employee, he explained why a new situation would be to the man's advantage, arranged a vocational-guidance consultant, set up interviews at other companies, and saw him relocated in eighteen days. His maxim: "Whoever is under a man's power is under his protection, too." "Anybody... can hire a man. But the test of leadership is how one handles the dismissal." He gave job security and got loyalty and no gossip.

**Bob W.'s four-step criticism formula.** Bob W. built a garage upholstery shop of 1931 into a furniture plant of over three hundred employees. When someone's work needs correcting:

1. Talk to them privately.
2. Praise them for what they are doing well.
3. Point out the one thing, at the moment, they could do better, and help them find the way.
4. Praise them again on their good points.

People are "reminded that they are not only pretty good, they can be even better." "I've been betting on people all my life." When Jim, drunk, splashed lacquer around the plant, Bob drove him home, told his wife he wouldn't be dismissed ("I'm Mr. W."), and told coworkers to forget it and be kind. Years later Jim led the workers against an outside union's unrealistic demands.

Two ways to put the be-human rule to work:

1. **In every difficult people matter, ask "What is the human way to handle this?"** (disagreements among subordinates, an employee's problem). Steer around sarcasm, cynicism, taking people down a peg, putting them in their place. Being human always pays, sooner or later.
2. **Let your actions show you put people first.** Take an interest in their off-the-job accomplishments; treat everyone with dignity; remember that people's primary purpose in life is to enjoy it. The more interest you show, the more they produce. **Praise subordinates to your supervisor**: put in plugs for them; "a man big enough to be humble appears more confident," and "a little modesty goes a long way." **Praise subordinates personally** for cooperation and every extra effort: "Praise is the greatest single incentive you can give people, and it costs you nothing." Rub people the right way.

Give people the treatment you like to receive; it earns you the "write-in vote" when leaders are chosen.

### Rule 3: think progress

"He stands for progress. He's the man for the job." **Status-quo-ers** (everything's-all-right-let's-don't-upset-the-apple-cart) outnumber **progressives** (there's-lots-of-room-for-improvement). Two things to do:

1. **Think improvement in everything you do.**
2. **Think high standards in everything you do.**

- A sales manager choosing a successor among three salesmen asked each what he'd do. Two said in effect "Everything is okay... Don't go refiguring anything." The third had ideas for new business, service, wasted time, compensation incentives and an ad campaign: "We've got a good outfit, but I believe we can make it better." He got the job.
- In a one-room country school (eight grades, forty children), one teacher had chaos (pupils locked her out, brought dogs in); she didn't care, set no goals, lost her temper and didn't like teaching. Her successor brought pride, respect and judgment, gave pupils responsibilities (blackboards, erasers, grading practice), set high positive standards, liked the children as individuals, and found discipline easy because she was well disciplined herself. "The teacher set the pace." Crack World War II units were led by officers with high standards fairly enforced.
- Employees mirror their superior's habits, ethics and self-control. Organizations are "rebuilt from the top down, not from the bottom up. Change the thinking at the top, and you automatically change the thinking at the bottom." In a new leader's first few weeks the group tests "how much rope will he give me?" and adjusts to his standards.
- The quatrain: "What kind of world would this world be, / If everyone in it were just like me?" Substitute company, club, community, school or church. **Carbon copies:** "Subordinates tend to become carbon copies of their chief... be sure the master copy is worth duplicating."

**"Am I a progressive thinker?" checklist**

- **Toward my work:** (1) Do I appraise my work with "how can we do it better?" (2) Do I praise my company, its people and products at every opportunity? (3) Are my personal standards for quantity and quality of work higher than three or six months ago? (4) Am I setting an excellent example for subordinates and associates?
- **Toward my family:** (1) Is my family happier than three or six months ago? (2) Am I following a plan to improve my family's standard of living? (3) Does my family have an ample variety of stimulating activities outside the home? (4) Do I set an example of "a progressive" for my children?
- **Toward myself:** (1) Am I a more valuable person than three or six months ago? (2) Am I following an organized self-improvement program? (3) Do I have forward-looking goals at least five years ahead? (4) Am I a booster in every organization I belong to?
- **Toward my community:** (1) Have I done anything in the past six months that improved my community? (2) Do I boost worthwhile projects rather than object, criticize or complain? (3) Have I ever taken the lead in a worthwhile improvement? (4) Do I speak well of my neighbors and fellow citizens?

### Rule 4: managed solitude

Leaders spend considerable time alone with their thoughts: Moses, Jesus, Buddha, Confucius, Mohammed, Gandhi; Franklin Roosevelt recovering from polio; Truman on his Missouri farm; even Hitler (writing *Mein Kampf* in jail), Lenin, Stalin and Marx in prison. Universities let professors lecture as few as five hours a week so they can think; executives spend much of their time in uninterrupted thought. Leaders use solitude "to do their superthinking."

**Mr. I-can't-stand-to-be-alone** avoids being alone, prowls for people, fills time with small talk and gossip, and when physically alone turns on TV, newspaper, radio or phone to "occupy my mind for me." Scared of his own thoughts, he grows shallow, makes ill-considered moves and lacks firmness of purpose.

Schwartz had thirteen trainees closet themselves one hour daily for two weeks and think constructively about anything. All found it practical: one averted a break with an executive; others settled a job change, a marriage, buying a home, choosing a college. All understood themselves better. "Decisions and observations made alone in managed solitude have an uncanny way of being 100 percent right!" "When the fog is lifted, the right choice becomes crystal clear." An associate, unclear on a basic problem, got up at 3:30 A.M., fixed coffee, sat on the sofa and thought until 7, and reversed her stand; the new stand proved completely correct.

The procedure: set aside at least thirty minutes daily to be completely by yourself, at a time when your mind is fresh and free from distractions (early morning or late evening). Use two kinds of thinking:

- **Directed thinking:** review the major problem in front of you; the mind studies it objectively and tends toward the right answer.
- **Undirected thinking:** let the mind select what to think about; the subconscious taps the memory bank and feeds the conscious mind. Good for self-evaluation: "How can I do better? What should be my next move?"

"Managed solitude pays off." "The main job of the leader is thinking. And the best preparation for leadership is thinking."

Chapter summary: (1) trade minds: "What would I think of this if I exchanged places with the other person?" (2) the be-human rule: ask "What is the human way to handle this?", put people first, give the treatment you like to receive; (3) think progress, believe in progress, push for progress, remembering that subordinates become carbon copies; resolve "At home, at work, in community life, if it's progress I'm for it"; (4) confer with yourself in managed solitude every day.
