# Action and setbacks

Two chapters: get the action habit, and how to turn defeat into victory.

## Get the action habit

There is a shortage of top-flight people, and "plenty of room at the top." The missing ingredient is the ability to get things done. Executives filling a big job ask: "Will he do the job?" "Will he follow through?" "Is he a self-starter?" "Can he get results, or is he just a talker?" "An only fair idea acted upon, and developed, is 100 percent better than a terrific idea that dies because it isn't followed up." John Wanamaker: "Nothing comes merely by thinking about it." Everything worthwhile is "an idea acted upon."

### Activationists and passivationists

People come in two classes. The **activationist** is active, a doer, who follows through. The **passivationist** is a "don'ter" who postpones until he has proved he shouldn't or can't, or until it is too late. Mr. Activationist takes the vacation, goes to church, writes the note of congratulation, starts the business, changes work at forty; Mr. Passivationist plans to, finds a "good" reason not to, and debates himself out of it. The by-products: the activationist gains confidence, inner security, self-reliance and income; the passivationist loses confidence and self-reliance and lives in mediocrity. "Mr. Activationist does. Mr. Passivationist is going to do but doesn't."

### Waiting for perfect conditions

Many passivationists wait until everything is 100 percent favorable. "To wait for the perfect set of conditions is to wait forever."

- **G. N.**, a Chicago accountant in his late thirties, wanted to marry but always found something wrong with the woman. When he finally found one, he drew up a four-page document of stipulations (religion, children, friends, her job, where they'd live, income, and half a page of habits she must break or acquire). She replied that "for better or for worse" was good enough for her, and ended it. "You can be too careful." "The test of a successful person is not an ability to eliminate all problems before they arise, but to meet and work out difficulties when they do arise." Make "an intelligent compromise with perfection," and cross bridges as you come to them.
- **J. M.**, in his twenties with a wife and child, paid rent equal to a mortgage payment. He told his wife, "How would you like to buy a new home next week?" (only about half of those who say they want a home ever buy one). The house needed $1,200 down; he couldn't borrow it normally without encumbering his mortgage credit, so he arranged a private loan from the builder, repaid at $100 a month plus interest. The family cut expenses $25 a month, and he asked his boss not for a raise but for a chance to earn $75 more, which came as ten extra weekend hours. Three lessons: the resolution to act set his mind to finding the ways; the new confidence makes future action easier; and the family gained a standard of living it might never have had if he'd waited for perfect conditions.
- **C. D.**, in the Customs Division of the Post Office, wanted to import low-cost gifts and toys and knew more about it than many importers. Ten years later he was still there, with a fresh reason each year: lack of money, a recession, a new baby, a need for temporary security, trade restrictions.

Two things to do instead of waiting:

1. **Expect future obstacles and difficulties.** Driving from Chicago to Los Angeles, you map the route and check the car, eliminating as much risk as you can, but you can't eliminate it all. "We can't buy an insurance policy against all problems."
2. **Meet problems and obstacles as they arise.** Cross bridges when you come to them.

### Ideas have value only when acted upon

A professor had planned for years to write a biography. He was "too busy," had "responsibilities," and buried the idea in a **mental graveyard**, having visualized only the work, the sacrifice and the reasons it might fail. "Ideas in themselves are not enough." "Every day thousands of people bury good ideas because they are afraid to act on them. And afterward, the ghosts of those ideas come back to haunt them." Two thoughts to keep: give your ideas value by acting on them, whatever they are; and act to gain peace of mind. "It might have been" causes terrible psychological pain; an idea acted on brings mental satisfaction.

### Use action to cure fear

"Action feeds and strengthens confidence; inaction in all forms feeds fear. To fight fear, act. To increase fear—wait, put off, postpone." A paratrooper instructor: "It's the waiting to jump that gets a fellow"; the longer a man postpones, the more scared he gets. Edward R. Murrow perspired before every telecast; his stage fright vanished once he began. A father whose five-year-old feared green monsters didn't say "Don't worry": he inspected the windows with the child and put a plastic gun beside the bed "just in case," and the boy was asleep in four minutes. Positive action works the way a placebo pill does. Salesmen who circle the block or have one more cup of coffee only feed their fear.

- Dread making a phone call? Make the call.
- Fear a medical checkup? Go.
- Fear discussing a problem with your superior? Discuss it.

"Do what you fear, and fear disappears."

### Start your mental engine mechanically

A young writer waited for inspiration. A productive writer uses "mind force": he sits at the desk, picks up a pencil, makes mechanical motions, even doodles, and the mind gets on track; ideas from out of the blue are "bonuses." "Action must precede action. That's a law of nature." Even a thermostat or an automatic transmission must be set before it works. A door-to-door branch manager: "the only way to start is to start... park your car. Get your sample case. Walk to the door. Ring the bell. Smile. Say 'Good morning,' and make your presentation, all mechanically." By the second or third call the mind is sharp. On a cold morning in a warm bed, throw off the covers and put your feet on the floor. "People who get things done... don't wait for the spirit to move them; they move the spirit."

Two exercises:

1. **Use the mechanical way for simple but unpleasant chores.** A mother picks up dishes as she leaves the table instead of letting them pile up. Today, pick the one thing you like to do least and do it without deliberating.
2. **Use the mechanical way to create ideas, plan and solve problems: use a pencil and paper.** "A simple pencil is the greatest concentration tool money can buy," better than a fancy soundproof office. The "mind is not designed to think one thought and write another"; writing "writes" on the mind, so you remember longer and more exactly, and you can think amid noise. Write, doodle, diagram.

### Now

"*Now* is the magic word of success. *Tomorrow, next week, later, sometime, someday* often as not are synonyms for the failure word, *never*." Bill and Janet took home $1,000 a month and spent $1,000, always planning to save "when we get a raise." Janet saw that "we don't save because we think we can't," and an ad showed that $100 a month for fifteen years comes to $18,000 plus $6,600 interest. They began taking 10 percent off the top: it is easier to spend what's left after saving than to save what's left after spending. Franklin: "Don't put off until tomorrow what you can do today."

**Get the speak-up habit.** At a conference of six men, one contributed and two echoed him; the other three were **conference vegetables**, who said afterward in the hall "I felt like saying..." and "Why didn't someone suggest...?" Executives want comment; the silent one "hides his light under a bushel."

**Overpreparedness.** Joe College sits down to study, then watches TV, calls his girl, takes an incoming call, plays Ping-Pong, showers and has a snack; he opens the book at 1 A.M. and tells himself "I studied till 2 A.M." He is a victim of getting ready: office chats, coffee breaks, sharpening pencils, clearing the desk. Break it by telling yourself: "I'm in condition right now to begin. I can't gain a thing by putting it off. I'll use the 'get ready' time and energy to get going instead."

### Seize the initiative

A machine-tool executive calls people with initiative the "ball carriers on our team." "Initiative is a special kind of action. It's doing something worthwhile without being told to do it." A drug-firm missionary salesman crusaded for market research, won permission to issue a monthly "Facts of Drug Marketing" report, was relieved of regular duties after a year, and now has two assistants, a secretary and about three times his old income.

1. **Be a crusader.** "Pick up the ball and run." When a new subdivision stalled because a few don't-care families let their homes slide and the attitude spread, Harry L. started a crusade: cleanup of vacant lots, garden clubs, tree planting, a playground, a community pool. A one-man crusade wins support if the idea is good: a new department or product, a church building, school equipment.
2. **Be a volunteer.** Many hold back for fear of what associates will say: being laughed at, called an "eager beaver," accused of "bucking for a raise." Ask, "Which group do I want to have accept me: the group that laughs because it is secretly jealous or the group that is making progress by doing things?" The volunteer stands out and shows ability and ambition. Leaders are "ten times out of ten" activationists, and people place confidence in the one who acts. No one is ever praised because "he doesn't disturb anyone."

Chapter summary: (1) be an activationist, a doer, not a don't-er; (2) don't wait until conditions are perfect; (3) ideas have value only when acted upon; (4) use action to cure fear; (5) start your mental engine mechanically; (6) think in terms of now, and be an "I'm starting right now" person; (7) get down to business, pronto, without wasting time getting ready; (8) seize the initiative: be a crusader and a volunteer. "Get in gear and go!"

## How to turn defeat into victory

On skid row Schwartz found men from every background, all defeated, each eager to tell his "private Waterloo." **Mr. Mediocre** gives the same reasons; he is supercautious and surrendered "in a reasonably clean, socially 'accepted' way." **Mr. Success** comes from every background too. The three match on everything except "their response to defeat": the skid-row man stayed down; Mr. Mediocre got to his knees, crawled away and ran; Mr. Success "bounced up, learned a lesson, forgot the beating, and moved upward."

A management consultant lost ten years' savings in his first six months, lived in his office, turned down "good" jobs, and heard no a hundred times as often as yes. Through seven hard years he never complained: "I'm learning"; "it's not taking something out of me; it's putting something into me instead." Everyone in *Who's Who* has been pounded by losing situations. "It is not possible to win high-level success without meeting opposition, hardship, and setback. But it is possible to use setbacks to propel you forward."

### Salvage something from every setback

The Civil Aviation Administration investigates every crash (air travel has one fatality per 10 billion miles flown): it reconstructs the wreck, asks "What caused this crash?", and fixes the whole fleet. Doctors hold postmortems. One sales executive devotes a sales meeting a month to reconstructing lost sales; coaches review the game film. The principle: **salvage something from every setback.** The first impulse is emotional upset, which misses the lesson.

A failing senior at Wayne State told Schwartz he "could name fifty people" who succeeded without the course. Schwartz paused forty-five seconds before answering ("when you've been sniped at, one fine way to prevent a war of words is to take a long pause before answering"), then said the course content wouldn't make or break him, "but your attitude toward this course may," and that outside "they grade you just as we grade you": no one promotes you or pays you more for second-class work. The student re-enrolled, passed, and was glad he'd failed, having learned that "if you don't produce, you don't get where you want to go." Find the lesson, apply it, "and then look back on defeat and smile."

Lionel Barrymore broke a hip in 1936 and played eighteen more years of roles from a wheelchair. W. Colvin Williams, blinded by a mine on March 15, 1945, became a minister and counselor who called blindness an asset: "I can never judge by appearances... always give a person a second chance." "Defeat is only a state of mind, and nothing more." One investor called early losses "part of the cost of my education," where others concluded the market is gambling. "Being licked is valuable if we learn from it." Next time something goes wrong, calm down and find out what caused the trouble.

### Be your own constructive critic

People are quick to take credit and quick to blame others: salesmen blame customers, executives blame employees, spouses blame each other. "More often than not we trip ourselves." Condition yourself to be objective: "Put yourself in a glass tube and look at yourself as a disinterested third party would." Risë Stevens lost the Metropolitan Opera "Auditions of the Air" and wanted to hear it was unfair or a matter of connections; her teacher said, "My dear, have the courage to face your faults." She asked "Why did I fail?" and "How can I win next time?", admitted her voice range, languages and repertoire of roles fell short, and worked on them; facing her faults helped her succeed and win friends. "Being self-critical is constructive... Blaming others is destructive." Professionals seek out their faults and correct them. Treat a mistake as "Here's another way to make me a bigger winner," never "another reason I'm a loser." Orville Hubbard: "A failure is a man who has blundered but is not able to cash in on the experience."

### Stop blaming luck

"That's the way the ball bounces" is said as if bounces were luck. The bounce is determined by the ball, the way it was thrown and the surface it struck: physical laws. A CAA investigator, doctor or coach who said it would be replaced. Hubbard, mayor of Dearborn for seventeen consecutive years, lost the mayoral nomination three times, a state-senate race three times and a congressional race once, and studied each loss as political education. "If you lose, learn." Blaming "hard luck" or "bad luck" is a child seeking sympathy. "Blaming luck never got anyone where he wanted to go."

### Blend persistence with experimentation

A literary consultant's client had written sixty-two stories and sold none; every one followed the same format, and he never experimented. "Persisting in one way is not a guarantee of victory. But persistence blended with experimentation does guarantee success." Edison ran thousands of experiments. Seven of eight oil wells drilled are dry holes, and they drill a new well rather than sink one hole to ridiculous depths. "Stay with your goal. Don't waver an inch from it. But don't beat your head against a wall." Keep the bulldog persistence; vary the approach.

Two ways to experiment:

1. **Tell yourself, "There IS a way."** "All thoughts are magnetic": "I'm beaten" attracts the negatives. Marriage counselors make no progress until one partner believes happiness is possible; an alcoholic is doomed until he believes he can beat it; many new businesses fail at the **TAR barrier** ("things are rough"). Belief converts the negative energy of "let's quit" into the positive energy of "let's keep going." "A problem... becomes unsolvable only when you think it is unsolvable."
2. **Back off and start afresh.** An engineer designing a novel aluminum structure gets his best ideas while watering the lawn with a garden hose. Eisenhower used weekend vacations to keep his mind free of inconsequential details so he could think on basic principles. An associate takes a seventy-two-hour out-of-town trip with his wife once a month. When you hit a snag, don't throw the project over; back off, refresh yourself (music, a walk, a short nap), and return.

### See the good side

A credit-company employee laid off as the "least valuable" in his department called it a "blessing in disguise" and soon found a better job at more pay. "You see in any situation what you expect to see." "All things do work together for good if you'll just develop clear vision."

The five guideposts: (1) study setbacks to pave your way to success; (2) have the courage to be your own constructive critic, which makes you a professional; (3) stop blaming luck, and research each setback; (4) blend persistence with experimentation; (5) there is a good side in every situation; find it.
