# Actionable Gamification: the Octalysis Framework

**Gamification** is "the craft of deriving fun and engaging elements found typically in games and thoughtfully applying them to real-world or productive activities." Chou calls it **Human-Focused Design**: it "optimizes for human motivation in a system as opposed to optimizing for pure functional efficiency." **Function-Focused Design** is the factory that assumes people act because they are required to. Games mastered Human-Focused Design first because "no one ever *has* to play a game": the moment a game stops being fun, players leave. Design every system as if that were true of it.

The book's central instruction: *focus on motivation by thinking through the 8 Core Drives instead of focusing on features and functionalities.*

- **The PBL Fallacy** is the belief that adding Points, Badges, and Leaderboards makes a boring product exciting. PBL is the **shell** of a game; the **Core Drives** are its **essence**. "It's not just what game elements you put in - it's how, when, and most importantly, why these game elements appear." Users often feel insulted by shallow shell mechanics. "Learn from the design; don't copy the shell."
- **Bad designer vs good designer.** The bad designer starts with "what popular game mechanics and game elements should I use?" (monsters, swords, crops). The good designer starts with "how do I want my users to *feel*?" Inspired, proud, scared, anxious? Only then does the good designer pick elements. "Game elements are simply there to push and pull on their users' behavioral core drives."
- **The zero-motivation rule.** Everything people do is driven by one or more of the 8 Core Drives. "If there are none of these Core Drives behind a Desired Action, there is no motivation, and no behavior happens."
- **Desired Action**: the specific behavior the designer wants. **Win-State**: the scenario where the user has overcome some challenge. Every Desired Action, once committed, should lead to a Win-State, and "after every Desired Action must come a delightful moment."
- **Anti Core Drive**: a motivation to *not* take the Desired Action. Badly framed designs create them. For example, a sign saying many visitors steal petrified wood makes theft the norm, and theft rose.

## The 8 Core Drives

Octalysis is an octagon with one Core Drive on each side. Refer to them by number ("a Core Drive 6 design, which enforces Core Drive 3"). Each Core Drive's position tells you its nature (next section).

| # | Core Drive | Chou's definition | Signature Game Techniques |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Epic Meaning & Calling** | A person "believes they are doing something greater than themselves and/or were 'chosen' to take that action." | Narrative #10, Humanity Hero #27, Elitism #26, Beginner's Luck #23, Free Lunch #24 |
| 2 | **Development & Accomplishment** | The "internal drive for making progress, developing skills, achieving mastery, and eventually overcoming challenges." | Status Points #1, Achievement Symbols #2, Leaderboards #3, Progress Bars #4, Rockstar Effect #92 |
| 3 | **Empowerment of Creativity & Feedback** | Users engage in a creative process: they repeatedly figure out new things, try combinations, see results, and adjust. This is "Play." | Boosters #31, Milestone Unlock #19, Plant Picker/Meaningful Choices #11, Poison Picker/Choice Perception #89 |
| 4 | **Ownership & Possession** | People are motivated because they feel they own or control something, and they want to increase and improve it. | Build-From-Scratch #43, Collection Sets #16, Exchangeable Points #75, Monitor Attachment #42, Alfred Effect #83 |
| 5 | **Social Influence & Relatedness** | Activities inspired by what other people think, do, or say: mentorship, acceptance, feedback, companionship, competition, envy. | Mentorship #61, Brag Buttons #57, Trophy Shelves #64, Group Quests #22, Social Treasures #63, Social Prods #62, Conformity Anchor #58, Water Coolers #55 |
| 6 | **Scarcity & Impatience** | "Wanting something simply because it is extremely rare, exclusive, or immediately unattainable." | Dangling #44, Anchored Juxtaposition #69, Magnetic Caps #68, Appointment Dynamics #21, Torture Breaks #66, Evolved UI #37 |
| 7 | **Unpredictability & Curiosity** | "Constantly being engaged because you don't know what is going to happen next." | Glowing Choice #28, Mystery Boxes #72, Easter Eggs #30, Lottery/Rolling Rewards #74, Oracle Effect #71 |
| 8 | **Loss & Avoidance** | The "motivation to avoid something negative from happening": losing past work, admitting it was all useless, or missing a fading opportunity. | Rightful Heritage #46, Evanescent Opportunities #86, Countdown Timers #65, Status Quo Sloth #85, FOMO Punch #84, Sunk Cost Prison #50 |

What each Core Drive is good for, and where it breaks:

- **CD1** is the prime White Hat drive. It is best communicated in Discovery and Onboarding, and it strengthens the other seven. It turns passive users into evangelists and makes them "highly forgiving of your flaws": a Waze user who sees a map error thinks "I need to go fix it!" **Believability is key.** If the mission feels disingenuous, it backfires as a manipulative slap (a gasoline company claiming that pumping with it protects the planet). CD1 creates no urgency, so it needs other drives to move people now.
- **CD2** is the easiest drive to design for and is where PBL lives. "A badge or trophy without a challenge is not meaningful at all." Seeing progress is not the same as *feeling* accomplished. The most important accomplishment is to make users **feel smart**: "people do not take actions that are necessarily the most economical, but actions that make them feel the smartest." **The 4-Second Rule**: "if a user spends four seconds on an interface and can't figure out what to do, they feel stupid and will start to disengage emotionally."
- **CD3** sits in the "golden corner" (White Hat and Right Brain) and is the hardest drive to implement. It powers **Evergreen Mechanics**: the designer no longer needs to add content because "the user's mind becomes the evergreen content." Poker, Golf, Chess, and Mahjong last for centuries because of it. The rule: "make sure that there isn't one standard way to win." Provide meaningful choices that allow drastically different strategies to the same Win-State. Feedback is required: "simply expressing creativity without receiving any feedback is actually a torturous experience." If you block beneficial creativity, users "use their creativity to find loopholes."
- **CD4** is the strongest Left Brain drive. It includes the **Endowment Effect** (owners value things far above what buyers pay), identity and consistency, and ownership of a process or project. Building CD4 "often reinforces the power of Core Drive 8." When designed badly it makes people selfish and kills curiosity and creativity.
- **CD5** is a double-edged sword. When people are prone to envy, "make sure there is a realistic path for them to follow." Otherwise envy turns into denial ("the whole thing is stupid anyway"). Show users how their peer group behaves; the more closely they relate to that group, the more they comply. Friend-spamming that looks like self-gain destroys trust.
- **CD6** is irrational and powerful. People chase what moves away from them, want what they can't have, and value what is hard to obtain. "When in doubt, Dangle about." The goal must stay "very challenging... but not impossible." If people believe it is impossible, they fall into a CD8 Self-Denial Mode ("it's probably for losers anyway"). Scarcity is perceived, not objective, and relative: a jar that drops from 10 cookies to 2 is valued more than a jar that always held 2.
- **CD7** catches the brain whenever events fall outside a pattern it recognizes. The Skinner Box shows that unpredictable results, not points, drive obsessive behavior. Ask: "Is there any way to add a little bit of randomness and chance to the process?" CD7 retains users into the Endgame. Paired with CD6 it becomes addictive; paired with CD8 it amplifies fear.
- **CD8** creates urgency and obsession and leaves users uncomfortable over time. Losses weigh about twice as much as gains. The user must know *exactly* what action prevents the loss. Otherwise CD8 becomes an Anti Core Drive and they deny the problem ("probably not that big of a problem anyway").

**The hidden 9th Core Drive, Sensation**, is the physical pleasure of an action (food, massage, drugs). Octalysis leaves it out because the framework covers psychological drives. Sensation can still serve as a reward or Feedback Mechanic.

For each Core Drive's full principles, every named Game Technique with its rules and caveats, and the cases behind them, read [core-drives.md](core-drives.md). Read it when ideating for a specific Core Drive, choosing or critiquing a technique, or when a technique named here needs its definition.

## The natures: Left Brain vs Right Brain, White Hat vs Black Hat

A Core Drive's position on the octagon gives its nature. Treat the natures as design facts: they predict how motivation behaves over time.

### Left Brain (extrinsic) vs Right Brain (intrinsic)

- **Left Brain Core Drives** are **CD2, CD4, and CD6** (logic, ownership, analytical thought). They tend toward **Extrinsic Motivation**: motivation "derived from a goal, purpose, or reward" when the task itself is not necessarily interesting.
- **Right Brain Core Drives** are **CD3, CD5, and CD7** (creativity, sociality, curiosity). They tend toward **Intrinsic Motivation**: enjoying the activity itself, to the point of paying to do it. A casino visitor says "I lost $200, but I had so much fun!"; nobody says that about a certain $40 for five hours of factory work.
- "Left Brain Core Drives are by nature goal-oriented, while Right Brain Core Drives are experience-oriented." The labels are symbolic, not neuroanatomy.
- **Intrinsic test**: "if the goal or objective were removed, would the person still be motivated to take the Desired Action?" Hanging out with friends and solving creative puzzles pass. Accumulating points, goods, or mastery progress fails. By this test, shooting hoops 1,000 times a day for Mastery is extrinsic in Octalysis.
- **Motivation vs rewards** (Michael Wu). Doing something for any reward, even an intrinsic reward, is not intrinsic motivation. CD2 can hand out intrinsic rewards and still be extrinsic motivation.
- **Overjustification Effect**: "I become primarily engaged with the reward which subsequently eradicates and replaces the intrinsic motivation." Once the reward stops, motivation drops below where it began. Rewards also narrow focus. In the candle problem, rewarded groups took 3.5 minutes longer. Extrinsic rewards improve routine, mechanical tasks and hurt creative or cognitive ones.
- **Education** shows the trap. A child's innate desire to learn (CD7, CD3) is swapped for grades and diplomas (CD2, CD4), and students respond with minimum work, cheating, and teenagers asking "When do people *ever* use advanced math after graduating from school?"
- **Social Norms vs Market Norms.** "Our brains are either using the Social Norms, or the Market Norms." Paying someone for a favor flips them into market norms, and small payments insult. Gifts keep social norms unless their dollar value is named.
- **Where extrinsic rewards earn their place**: monotonous routine work, and creating initial interest in Discovery among "too busy" people. The reward must not insult the time it asks for; a weak reward does worse than no reward.
- **The transition** (Michael Wu's progression): attract with **Extrinsic Rewards** (gift cards, money, merchandise, discounts), move through **Intrinsic Rewards** (recognition, status, access), and arrive at **Intrinsic Motivation** for long-term engagement. If you keep users on extrinsic rewards, the system goes stale, overjustifies, and burns out.

**Three ways to make an experience more intrinsic** (add CD3, CD5, CD7):

1. **More social.** Users invite friends intrinsically only after they are sold on the value, at the **First Major Win-State**, where users first exclaim "Wow! This is awesome!" Find exactly where the First Major Win-State is and count how many minutes it takes to reach it; every second before it loses users. Place the invite or rating request right after it; asking for all of a user's friends at sign-up is the "gigantic mistake" Chou names. Make share messages believable and editable, not fake promotional copy. Best of all, make the Desired Action itself social through collaborative play, Group Quests, and Water Coolers.
2. **More unpredictability.** Use controlled randomness: varied feedback dialogue, random tips, variable rewards. **Keep the action behind a variable reward short and easy**, like pulling a slot lever or refreshing a feed. When the action must be long (a year of work toward a raise), make every possible reward appealing and known up front ("Italy, France, or Denmark").
3. **More meaningful choices.** Ask "Is there a way to allow my users to take multiple routes but still reach the same goal?" Give **two to three meaningful options** at any point. Too many cause decision paralysis, which makes users feel stupid; Chou calls this an Anti Core Drive within CD2, "the Google+ Problem." Several Boosters offered as rewards turn extrinsic rewards into strategy. Airline miles and coupon stacking are intrinsic for people who enjoy optimizing them.

### White Hat vs Black Hat

- **White Hat Core Drives** are the top of the octagon: **CD1, CD2, CD3**. They "make us feel powerful, fulfilled, and satisfied... in control of our own lives." Their critical weakness: "it does not create a sense of urgency." People always intend to act and never quite do.
- **Black Hat Core Drives** are the bottom: **CD6, CD7, CD8**. They "make us feel obsessed, anxious, and addicted." They create urgency and leave a bad taste. When users can wean themselves off, they leave. Zynga's Black Hat "Data-Driven Design" produced great short-term metrics, then an exodus in late Scaffolding and the Endgame. Farmville users eventually hit a **"Black Hat Rebound,"** where they burn out and find the courage to leave.
- **CD4 and CD5 can be either**, depending on design. Ownership can bring wellbeing and control, or possessions can own you. Social influence can bring friendship and appreciation, or peer pressure.
- **Predictive rule**: "If there aren't any Black Hat techniques, it is likely there won't be any breakout success; if there aren't any White Hat techniques, users will quickly burn out and leave for something better." Consider all 8 Core Drives.
- **Which to lead with.** Long-term engagement such as workplace motivation runs on White Hat: meaning, a path to mastery, and meaningful autonomy. Google built this with its mission, engineer levels, 20% time, and project ownership. Design as though "the moment your experience is no longer engaging, people will leave." Quick transactions such as sales, eCommerce, closing deals, and fundraising need Black Hat urgency: "Black Hat creates urgency and closes deals." Investors stay excited on White Hat and commit only when convinced they will lose the deal. Morf Media turned risk-averse clients' CD8 around: every day their employees remain non-compliant increases the risk, so it became riskier *not* to buy the compliance training.
- **Careful transitioning** (procedure). "First setup a White Hat environment to make users feel powerful and comfortable, then implement Black Hat designs at the moment when you need users to take that one Desired Action for conversion... This is when you transition quickly back to White Hat motivation to make them feel good about their experience." In Battle Camp, a troop spends 8 hours on a boss, then faces a choice: pay $10 for energy or lose the 8 hours. Right after paying, players get achievements, rewards, and teammates cheering "You are our hero!"
- **No Buyer's Remorse.** After a Black Hat close, reassure buyers that it was the smartest purchase (CD2), that legions of others made it too (CD5), and that it improves the world (CD1). TOMS and donation programs that send photos and thank-you letters do this. "If you want good Endgame design, you *must* immerse your users in White Hat Gamification techniques."
- **Bad shifts.** A $3 late fee at Israeli daycares moved parents from wanting to be *good* and responsible parents (CD1, CD5) to a weak, purchasable CD8, and lateness rose. It stayed higher after the fee was removed. A penalty needs proportional weight; $1 per minute late works.
- **Black Hat with a clear conscience.** Black Hat is not unethical in itself, and people welcome it for goals they want but lack the willpower for (the SnuzNLuz alarm donates to a cause you hate when you snooze). Chou's **litmus test** for manipulation: "Is there full transparency on its intended purpose?" and "Does the user implicitly or explicitly opt-in to the system?" Gamification moves people on the fence; doubling conversion from 8% to 16% still leaves 84% unmoved. It is not mind control.

**Ultimate Loss vs Executable Loss** (the CD8 dosing rule). "Dangle the threat of a large setback (the Ultimate Loss), but should only implement (if at all) small marginal setbacks (the Executable Loss) to emotionally train the user in taking the Ultimate Loss more seriously."
- Keep the Executable Loss under 30% of what the user has invested, ideally under 15%; 2-5% is generally enough.
- Above 30%, users are demoralized and quitting is very likely.
- Use CD8 "only at critical bumps where you really need the user to take the Desired Action," followed by White Hat.
- It is "always easier to start off stern and then become lenient." A second chance after a failure turns CD8 into CD5 gratitude. If second chances are taken for granted, follow through, or the people who perform get demoralized.

## The four Experience Phases (Level II Octalysis)

The reason someone uses a product on Day 1 differs from the reason on Day 100. If at any phase none of the 8 Core Drives are present, "there is no reason for the user to move on to the next phase, and the user simply drops out." Analyze and design each phase separately.

| Phase | Question it answers | Typical drives and phase rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Discovery** | Why would people even want to try the experience? It starts when they first *hear* about it, not at sign-up. | Usually CD7 (news, people talking). CD1 and CD6 exclusivity land here. The **FOMO Punch** works here. Use a confident price and well-structured exclusivity. |
| **Onboarding** | Users learn the rules and tools to play. | Usually CD2: "it is critical to make users feel smart and not overwhelm them." Apply the 4-Second Rule, Desert Oasis, Glowing Choice, and Evolved UI with two or three starting options. Get users to the First Major Win-State fast. |
| **Scaffolding** | The regular journey of repeated actions toward a goal. | Usually CD5 and CD6 (a goal not yet reachable). CD3 and CD4 build here; this is where habits form. |
| **Endgame** | How do you retain your veterans? | Usually CD8 (don't lose status). Durable Endgames rest on White Hat and Right Brain, especially CD3. Build Status Quo Sloth through enjoyable activity loops. Reserve the Sunk Cost Prison for moments when a user is lured away, and pair it with White Hat. |

Games are popular for 3 to 8 months; an impeccable Endgame lasts for decades or centuries.

**Level III Octalysis** adds player types: separate Octalysis charts and separate Desired Action tracks for each type. Types can be Bartle's (see [other-frameworks.md](other-frameworks.md)), or splits such as sales vs marketing staff, or loyal vs nonchalant vs new customers. **Level I** analyzes the whole experience across the 8 Core Drives. Level I is enough for most organizations.

## Fields and forms

- **Four application fields:**
  - **Product Gamification** makes a product engaging. "You are telling me all the things your users *can* do. But you have not explained to me *why* the user would do it."
  - **Workplace Gamification** is "the craft of creating environments and systems that inspire and motivate employees." Paychecks (CD4) and fear of firing (CD8) get just-enough work. Gallup found 13% of employees engaged. Charles Coonradt's *The Game of Work* gives five reasons hobbies beat work: clearly defined goals, better scorekeeping and scorecards, more frequent feedback, a higher degree of personal choice of methods, and consistent coaching. For employees, play "*must* be voluntary."
  - **Marketing Gamification** is "the art of creating holistic marketing campaigns that engage users in fun and unique experience." Build an ecosystem rather than one action and one response, and start with why the user would engage.
  - **Lifestyle Gamification** applies the 8 Core Drives to daily habits, health, productivity, and learning. Wearables and the Quantified Self supply Feedback Mechanics and Triggers. It can be as tactical as rolling a die to decide how to reward yourself (CD7).
- **Explicit Gamification** is obviously game-like, and users opt in (FoldIt, McDonald's Monopoly). It allows more playfulness but can feel childish in enterprise settings and costs more. **Implicit Gamification** weaves the Core Drives invisibly into the UX, like a well-designed doorknob (LinkedIn's progress bar, eBay, Amazon). It is easier and fits most contexts, but it invites lazy design. Neither is better; "gamification should never be a cookie-cutter solution." "You can gamify anything that involves human motivation, as long as it is not already a game."

## Procedure A: the Octalysis Audit (an existing experience)

1. **Name the Desired Action and how success is measured.** Done when you can state the exact behavior and the metric it moves.
2. **Walk all 8 Core Drives.** For each one, list the Game Techniques and features that activate it toward the Desired Action, and rate its strength from 0 to 10 on "How strong does this Core Drive motivate towards the Desired Action." 0 means the drive is absent. 10 means it cannot be improved and almost everyone exposed to it acts. Done when every drive has evidence and a rating.
   - **Calibrate honestly.** Most Function-Focused products score 0 on most drives and 1-2 on a few. Successful products score 3-4 on a few and a few 8-9s. Industry-dominating products have one or two 9s, and a 10 is rare. A CD1 of 10 is something people would die for; a CD8 of 10 is something they would kill for. Students inflate their own projects to 7-9s ("a Core Drive 4 bias").
   - **The Octalysis Score** is the sum of the squared ratings, with a maximum of 800. Most successful games score above 350, most nongame products below 150, and products insensitive to Human-Focused Design below 50. "The Score is a Smoke Screen": the least actionable output. Whether a drive is a 2 or a 3 doesn't matter, as long as you know it isn't a 7 or an 8.
3. **Read the natures.** Is the profile Left or Right Brain, White or Black Hat? Predict the consequences: no urgency, burnout risk, overjustification, no Black Hat for a breakout. Done when you can say how motivation will evolve over time.
4. **Repeat the walk for each Experience Phase** (Level II). Done when every phase has its active drives and its drop-off risk named.
5. **Find the leverage.** Because the score squares ratings, being "extremely strong in a few Core Drives" beats a little of everything: two 9s score 162, while eight 1s score 8. Pick the *right* drives for the scenario. Adding a little of a missing drive is easy, but pushing a strong drive from 8 to 9 takes expert research and A/B testing. Done when the weak drives that matter are chosen.
6. **Brainstorm improvements through each chosen Core Drive** (a ten-minute Level I brainstorm is instructive), then judge them. "For every motivating feature you design, you must consider the unintended consequences and negative ramifications before implementing it." Rarely implement everything. Done when each idea names its drive, phase, and Desired Action, and its risks are weighed.

## Procedure B: designing from scratch

1. **Fill in the Octalysis Strategy Dashboard.** It has five items, each of which becomes a game element:
   - **Business Metrics → Game Objective.** List quantifiable metrics in order of importance, with final metrics on top and means-to-an-end metrics below; "the enabled is more important than the enabler." Each interface can optimize for only *one* Business Metric. Test the order: "if my top three Business Metrics are doing amazingly well, while the other Business Metrics are doing modestly, would this project still be successful?" Metric up means the campaign succeeded; metric flat means it failed. "No fluffy things that can't be A/B Tested."
   - **Users → Players.** Name the target user types, and the Core Drives that move each.
   - **Desired Actions → Win-States.** List every small action in chronological order, grouped by Discovery, Onboarding, Scaffolding, and Endgame, so the journey leads naturally into the Endgame. "Every designed element needs to motivate users towards these Desired Actions. If it does not, the element is a distraction and should be thrown away."
   - **Feedback Mechanics → Triggers.** Feedback Mechanics are "information delivery mechanisms that communicate to the user that their actions are meaningful": progress, urgency, unpredictability. First list the mediums (site, email, app, events, social channels). Then list candidate mechanics tagged with their Core Drives, for example Countdown Timers (6, 8), Leaderboards (2, 5, 6, 8), and Exchangeable Points (2-8). "All Feedback Mechanics should become *Triggers* that promote the Desired Actions further, or else it should not be there."
   - **Incentives → Rewards.** List rewards in order from abundant to scarce for the giver (Status Points … signed book … personal time … a license). Also list what the core offering itself delivers. This list is "your arsenal" during ideation.

   Done when all five items are written. The Dashboard stays "a living document": new ideas add new Desired Actions and rewards back into it.
2. **Run Level I Octalysis Ideation.** Go through all 8 Core Drives in turn and generate ideas that move users toward the Desired Actions in each phase. Use [core-drives.md](core-drives.md) for techniques. Done when every Core Drive has ideas or a stated reason it is left out; Chou, for example, declines to design a Sunk Cost Prison into his own site.
3. **Repeat the process.** "Once you go through the 8 Core Drives, don't stop there. Do it again." More sophisticated, cross-drive designs emerge, such as **Chain Combos** (#40), where several drives' benefits compound. Done when a second pass has run and its new ideas are folded into the Dashboard.
4. **Narrow the funnel.** Many ideas going into the funnel is good; many coming out signals a lack of focus. Next refine reward schedules, activity loops, balanced economies, and wireframes. Done when a focused subset is chosen, with its unintended consequences considered.

Octalysis "allows you to ask the right questions... but you still need to answer them in a sophisticated and empathetic way. If you just treat Octalysis as a checklist, your design will only have the *shell* but not the *essence*." Draw on games you have actually played; Chou calls designing from surveys of other people's play **Secondhand Sushi**. Test a concept by explaining it to a buddy and watching whether they are truly excited, merely polite, or bored.
