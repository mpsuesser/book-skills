# Other frameworks seen through Octalysis

Chou built Octalysis entirely from studying games. Every game is an experiment in motivation, because no one *has* to play it; work and taxes, by contrast, run on "tainted motivation." He studies clones: when two near-identical games diverge into hit and flop, the cause is a subtle difference, such as how many seconds pass before a message pops up, the order in which challenges appear, or how scarcity is controlled. He also plays successful games seriously while resisting the pressure to spend, and notes each moment of compulsion. Because "we all have the same brains," sound models converge. Use the 8 Core Drives to see what each model covers and what it leaves out.

| Framework | Octalysis mapping | What it misses or where Chou differs |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-Determination Theory** (Ryan & Deci): Competence, Autonomy, Relatedness | Competence is CD2, Autonomy is CD3, Relatedness is CD5. **Pink's *Drive*** adds Purpose (CD1) and renames Competence as Mastery. | All White Hat. It can't explain gambling or the pull of exclusivity (Black Hat, the "non self-determined" motivations). It suits long-term workplace and creative work. Short-term bursts from deadlines, scarcity, and competition sit outside it and risk burnout. |
| **Bartle's Four Player Types** | **Achievers**: CD2 and CD6 (some CD3, CD4). **Explorers**: CD7 (seeds of CD2, CD3, CD6). **Socializers**: CD5 (some CD3, CD7 gossip, territorial CD4). **Killers**: CD2 and CD5 (some CD3, CD8 fear of looking weak, CD4 counting wins). | CD1 is available to every type and is the reason to be in the game at all. No type is built around CD8, because purely Black Hat players never enter a voluntary world. Bartle himself warns that the types may not fit outside virtual worlds. **Amy Jo Kim's** verbs are Explore, Create, Compete, Collaborate. **Marczewski's** workplace User Types are Disruptors, Philanthropists, Free Spirits, Socializers, Achievers, and Players. Philanthropists, driven by CD1 to help others, are often punished at work, while Players chase CD4 bonuses. |
| **Lazzaro's 4 Keys to Fun** | **Hard Fun** (*Fiero*, triumph over adversity): CD2 and CD6. **Easy Fun**: CD3 and CD7 (clay, Lego, dice). **Serious Fun**: CD1 and CD4. **People Fun**: CD5 and some CD8 (avoiding looking bad, hide-and-seek). | Adding randomness makes a game easier for casual players and penalizes hardcore ones. A parent can play dice against a five-year-old honestly, but has to go easy at chess. |
| **Flow** (Csikszentmihalyi): challenge balanced to skill. Too hard brings anxiety; too easy brings boredom. | From hardest to easiest: **CD8** (struggling to survive, anxiety), **CD6** (challenged, beatable by clearing a Moat #67), **CD2** (moderate, competent), **CD3** (the heart of Flow, especially when it alternates with CD2), **CD4** (mindless collecting, which risks boredom), **CD5** (chat-room easy), **CD7** (no skill needed, which is relaxation rather than boredom). | CD1 sits outside the skill-challenge axis and "powers the reason why people may engage with any experience in the first place." Skill grows over time, so an experience that stays the same across all four phases ends in boredom. |
| **Fogg Behavior Model** (B=MAT): Motivation, Ability, Trigger. All three are needed for action. **Tiny Habits**: two push-ups after each bathroom visit. | Fogg's six motivators (seeking pleasure, avoiding pain, seeking hope, avoiding fear, seeking social acceptance, avoiding social rejection) map to CD2, CD4, CD5, CD7, CD8, and the hidden CD9, Sensation. Triggers are the **Feedback Mechanics** of the Strategy Dashboard, and each trigger carries a Core Drive: points and badges carry CD2, countdown timers CD6, spinning fortune wheels CD7, friends' Likes CD5. A trigger whose drive is unknown is an "empty signal." | Fogg starts with Ability; Chou starts with Motivation. "Games don't necessarily make things easy - they make things motivating and engaging." Someone who wants to be healthy isn't thereby motivated to use *your* app. Fix the micromanaging boss rather than easing the dreadful job. Sometimes making things *harder* (CD6) raises motivation, as in dating. Ease still matters in Onboarding, where complexity makes users feel stupid (Anti Core Drive 2). Heuristics such as Anchoring, Illusory superiority, the IKEA effect, and Loss Aversion explain CD6 and the Endowment Effect. |
| **McGonigal** (*Reality is Broken*) | **Epic Meaning** is CD1. **Social Fabric** is CD5. **Urgent Optimism** ("the moment of hope just before our success is real") is CD2 and CD6, a White Hat and Black Hat Left Brain combination. **Blissful Productivity** ("being deeply immersed in work that produces immediate and obvious results") is CD2, CD3, and CD4. | CD7 is nearly absent, so add suspense and surprise. |
| **Hook Model** (Nir Eyal): Trigger, Action, Variable Reward, Investment | External triggers (emails, people) and internal triggers (boredom leads to opening Facebook) work with Appointment Dynamics. Variable rewards are CD7. Investments (photos, tags, folders) are CD4 and feed **Status Quo Sloth** and the **Sunk Cost Prison**. | "Variable rewards are one of the most powerful tools." |
| **Kahneman** | Loss aversion is CD8: people avoid risk unless the gain is about double the loss. In the **Fourfold Pattern**, people are risk-averse about low-probability losses and risk-seeking about likely ones. | |

Other works Chou recommends reading through this lens: Marczewski's User Types, Prospect Theory, and the research of Dan Ariely, Michael Wu, and Robert Cialdini.
