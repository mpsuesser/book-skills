# Core Drives in depth: principles, Game Techniques, cases

A **Game Technique** uses Game Elements, including Game Mechanics, to drive one or more Core Drives. The number after each technique is Chou's catalog number. A technique without a Core Drive behind it is shell without essence. *Progress Wars*, a parody where users click to fill meaningless progress bars, shows what happens: almost no engagement.

---

## CD1 Epic Meaning & Calling

**Principles**
- People take on grunt work and give up money for a higher vision. Wikipedia volunteers police the site because they are "protecting humanity's knowledge"; obscenities are removed in an average of 1.7 minutes. Editors are almost nine times more likely to donate than people who only read it (28% vs 3%). The motive is "playing your part for the greater good," not what feels good to the individual.
- **Sell a vision, not the product.** Apple's "1984" and "Think Different" ads never talk about computers; you "be part of it" by buying a Mac. People who identify as an "Apple Person" do what Apple People do. If the company breaks trust in the vision, people go back to comparing specs.
- **"Mjolnir is Not Just a Tool."** Even a product that is "just a tool" can carry a calling. Early Waze onboarding showed a snake monster named *Traffic* fighting cute Wazer knights, with the tagline "Beat Traffic, Together."
- **It must feel authentic.** Filial piety (孝) works in Chinese families only when the parents visibly honor their own parents. Zombies in a board meeting would fail; fantasy calling needs a context in which people are prepared to believe it.

**Techniques**
- **Narrative #10**: gives context about **why** to play. Zamzee, a kids' activity wearable, wrapped exercise in fantasy quests (run the stairs 15 times to earn your first spell), and kids moved 59% more. The narrative works even when the action is only loosely tied to it.
- **Humanity Hero #27**: tie the system to a world mission for buy-in during Onboarding. TOMS gives a pair of shoes for every pair bought. FreeRice donates 10 grains of rice per correct answer and has given 93 billion grains. "Tie your system to a cause that many people care about, you can build an entire business on the goodwill of others."
- **Elitism #26**: users form a proud group around ethnicity, beliefs, or interests, and act to secure the group's pride. Frustrating a rival group makes both groups act more. UCLA vs USC rivalry is instilled from freshman orientation (Onboarding) and drives school spirit, ticket sales, and alumni donations. On Kiva.org, Christian and atheist lending groups both increased their giving.
- **Beginner's Luck #23**: "Calling makes people think they are uniquely destined to do something." Example: a powerful sword on day one. Add Trophy Shelves #64 (CD5, implicitly showing off) and a Moat #67 (CD6, the sword can only be equipped after beating a hard level) to make users obsessed.
- **Free Lunch #24**: a freebie that is normally not free, given to a selected group and bound to a larger theme, so recipients feel special. Spoleto in Brazil gave a free lunch on International Women's Day to any woman who said she was beautiful.

## CD2 Development & Accomplishment

**Principles**
- Games break challenges into stages so there is always progress, and boss fights recognize the accomplishment. A game is "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles."
- **People pay to play, not to purchase.** On eBay, winning an auction beats eleven other bidders, so buyers pay 10% more and feel they **won**. Gamers pay for "the feeling of being awesome" (weapons, boosters), not for "Congratulations! You have won!"
- **Adding effort can add accomplishment.** Betty Crocker took powdered egg out of its cake mix so bakers added a fresh egg. They felt they were really baking, and sales broke out.
- **Feel smart.** Google's lone search box beat Yahoo's paralyzing portal. Google+ made users feel dumb and survived only through brute-force distribution. Amazon's green zone with its orange buy buttons is a Desert Oasis, and its crossed-out List Price makes buyers feel smart. In Ariely's *Economist* test, adding a print-only decoy at $125 that nobody chose raised revenue 44.6%.
- **UX vs Octalysis.** Usability work assumes users already want to act and makes acting easier. Octalysis creates the motivation to act in the first place, sometimes by withholding or adding difficulty.

**Techniques**
- **Progress Bars #4**: "Our brains hate it when incomplete things are dangled in front of our faces." LinkedIn's profile-completeness bar raised completeness by 20%. They are common in Onboarding.
- **Status Points #1**: points that keep score of progress, toward the Win-State internally and as feedback externally. They come in two kinds: **Absolute** (a total over the whole journey) vs **Marginal** (set for one challenge or period, then reset), and **One-Way** (only rise) vs **Two-Way** (can fall). Points rules communicate your values and tell users whether this is "a game worth playing." Awarding points for marketing on your behalf, for "every little stupid thing," or for money given to the site owner insults users. Base points on something meaningful that users want; otherwise they become "meaningless counters meant to stress people out." (Exchangeable Points #75 are under CD4.)
- **Achievement Symbols #2**: badges, stars, belts, trophies, uniforms. They "must symbolize 'achievement'" and "merely reflect achievement, but are not achievements by themselves." A "Clicked On My First Button" badge insults; a Medal of Honor inspires. Ask "Do you make your users feel accomplished?", never "Do you have badges?"
- **Leaderboards #3**: rank users by criteria tied to the Desired Actions. Being 25 points behind a leader at 25,000,000 kills motivation. Leaderboards need **Urgent Optimism**: users must believe they can win and feel they must act now. Variations:
  - place the user in the middle, seeing the players just above and below
  - **Group Leaderboards**, where no one wants to be the team's laggard
  - frequent resets, for renewed hope
  - micro-leaderboards among friends or similar users (top five of 22 friends rather than #95,253 of a million)

  "If there's no chance of achievement, there is no action."
- **Rockstar Effect #92**: "make users feel like everyone is dying to interact with them." It works when the status is *earned*. Twitter's one-way follow made followers an achievement; the Ashton Kutcher vs CNN race to 1 million followers gave Twitter free press.
- **Desert Oasis #38**: "visually nothing else is prominent besides the main Desired Action." Pair it with **Glowing Choice #28** (under CD7) so the Desired Action is never unclear.

## CD3 Empowerment of Creativity & Feedback

**Principles**
- Starcraft players start every match from scratch, with nothing accumulated but skill. Professionals reach 300-400 actions per minute, and the game became televised eSports. Great CD3 design removes **gamification fatigue**, where games are played for 2-8 months and then abandoned.
- Tic-Tac-Toe is boring once it is solved, which is the ceiling on CD3. Chess is effectively infinite (about 10^120 variations over 40 moves); champions' styles differ (Capablanca harmonious, Alekhine aggressive), and players find their own style through *meaningful choices*.
- **"The General's Carrot" in education**: give the user a goal plus a variety of tools and methods to strategize with. People are unmotivated when they don't understand the purpose, can't identify the goal, or lack meaningful tools. Kids memorize Pokemon stats but not the periodic table: cards serve strategies, beating friends, and ownership, while the table serves only a test.
- **Crowdsourcing**: FoldIt players solved an AIDS-related protein structure in 10 days after 15 years of failed attempts. CD3 plus CD1 sustains long-term engagement.
- **Workplace**: employees leave to start companies because their creative ideas get no feedback. Pink's autonomy over what, how, who, and when beats a raise. In a Cornell study of 320 small businesses, autonomy-granting firms grew 4x and had a third of the turnover.
- **Draw Something** reached 35 million downloads in 7 weeks and sold to Zynga for about $200M. It declined when repeated words killed creativity, which means it was not evergreen. Users who juggle too many games feel out of control, and "once you feel like you are not in control of your gameplay, you fall into Black Hat motivation." A gameable system can be fine if it is designed for it.
- The VW Piano Staircase raised stair use 66%: a strong Discovery design with a limited Endgame (few tunes).

**Techniques**
- **Boosters #31**: something that helps the player reach the Win-State more effectively, often limited by conditions (lost when hit, or time-limited like Mario's invincibility Star, which adds CD6). They are a strong monetization route. **Turning the Funnel Around** (Superbook): kids played games and rarely reached the Bible content, which was the #1 metric. Bible challenges in the Episode Guide now raise a "Knowledge Score" multiplier on game points, displayed as "Knowledge Score Multiplier: 0%" so kids yearn for it. Going to the content first became the smart strategy, and the funnel inverted.
- **Milestone Unlock #19**: players set an internal stopping point at milestones. A Milestone Unlock "open[s] up an exciting possibility that wasn't there before," such as new skills to test or a new plant that counters the toughest zombie just beaten. Players keep going from 11 PM until 4 AM.
- **Plant Picker / Meaningful Choices #11**: choices are truly meaningful when no option is obviously superior, like choosing what to plant in a garden. **The 100-player test**: if all 100 players reach the Win-State the same way ("do action A, get points, do action B, get badges, do action C, win!"), there are no meaningful choices. A 30/30/40 split shows some. All 100 choosing differently is great, and Lego is the ultimate state. Plants vs. Zombies achieves this with a limited pre-picked set of plants, sun resources, and limited squares, which produces many viable strategies. Its sequel's overpowered and useless plants turned Plant Pickers into Poison Pickers. Farmville Art, where players create their own endgame, is an Evergreen Mechanic. Add **triangularity** (Schell): a choice between playing safe for a small reward and taking a big risk for a big reward.
- **Poison Picker / Choice Perception #89**: people like something more when they get to choose, even between unappealing options ("Do you want to eat your vegetables before or after your chicken?"). It is easier than real Meaningful Choices but can offend when blatantly meaningless. Schell's indirect-control methods give "the feeling of freedom": constraints, goals, interface, visual design, characters, and music.

## CD4 Ownership & Possession

**Principles**
- Numbers become engaging once they represent your own money ("I instantly became a genius"). Collections matter as pieces of a set, and possessions provide emotional comfort.
- Ownership is a feeling or agreement. The Yap islanders traded a stone disc that sat at the bottom of the ocean. The Pet Rock sold 1.5 million units but gave no feedback, so people moved on to pets. Tamagotchi sold 76 million because of ownership plus feedback, meaningful choice (diet decides the dinosaur), and death after a 12-hour lapse.
- **Endowment Effect**: owners demand far more than buyers will pay. Duke basketball ticket winners asked about $2,400; non-winners offered about $170. Imagined ownership works too: the longer someone is the top bidder, the harder they counter-bid. Trials and money-back guarantees let consumers own first. People who treat an item "for exchange" (traders) lose the bias.
- **Identity and consistency**: people prefer things that echo their own names (Dennis the dentist), and they herd after their own past choices. Racetrack bettors grow more confident right after betting, as "a good bet" becomes "my bet."
- **Commitments**: asking people two weeks earlier to display a 3-inch "BE A SAFE DRIVER" sign raised acceptance of a big "DRIVE CAREFULLY" billboard from 17% to 76%. Asking "will you please call if you have to cancel?" cut restaurant no-shows from 30% to 10%. Written commitments beat mental ones, and public ones are the most stubborn (CD5). An insurance rep's question "tell me exactly why you've chosen to purchase your insurance with [our company]" raised sales from 9% to 19%.

**Techniques**
- **Build-From-Scratch #43**: involve users early in creating something (decorate the house, design the avatar) so it becomes "my thing." This is the IKEA effect. A pre-made item with "you can redecorate" yields less ownership. If building delays the First Major Win-State, offer quick templates and customization later, or make the building itself the First Major Win-State.
- **Collection Sets #16**: give items as part of a themed set, and users want to complete it. In Geomon, a Four-Season Deer could be caught only in its calendar season; players traded and paid for it although it wasn't powerful, and when the game shut down they raised $700,000 to save it. McDonald's Monopoly lets every user progress toward a reward while the company controls its budget: "giving them collection pieces will result in longer-term engagement" than handing out physical items directly. If users expect the full reward, for example because of advertising, a set piece insults.
- **Exchangeable Points #75**: points that can be redeemed, traded with other players, or traded outside the system. They differ from Status Points, which only rise and only measure (CD2). "Just having an economy" does not create engagement. Balance the labor-to-time-to-tradability-to-reward ratios and keep adjusting them like a central bank. Users should never feel currency is excessively abundant; control it through the true scarcity of *time*. If labor isn't rewarded with commensurate perceived value, the economy loses legitimacy.
- **Monitor Attachment #42**: people come to own what they constantly monitor and then want it to improve. Mere exposure makes things familiar, safe, and desirable. Bloggers check Google Analytics a dozen times a day (CD7) and then try new titles and posting times (CD3). "If you could design your experience where users are constantly monitoring the progressive output of something (even if the numbers are going down at times), you have a good shot."
- **Alfred Effect #83**: "users feel that a product or service is so personalized to their own needs that they cannot imagine using another service." Users stay even when competitors have better technology or prices. Waze asks "would you like to go to work now?" at 8:30 AM and offers the gym on Wednesday nights. Make the learning visible early so users stay long enough to notice it.

## CD5 Social Influence & Relatedness

**Principles**
- **Envy needs a path.** In Parallel Kingdom, a mentor was assigned at the 40-minute mark, just as Chou was leaving. The mentor arrived later ("He will contact you the next time he signs-in"), which made him believable as a real player, and gave a +7 sword. Watching the mentor led to "I wish I could be like that one day," and two months of play.
- **Social norm framing.** "Many past visitors have removed the petrified wood from the park" raised theft to 7.9%, against 3% with no sign. "Please don't remove the petrified wood from the park" cut it to 1.67%. A hotel-towel sign saying 75% of guests reuse towels ("Join Your Fellow Guests...") made guests 25% more likely to reuse theirs, and "nearly 75% of guests who stayed in *this* room" did better still. "77% of their neighbors... your community's popular choice" cut energy use 10%. Most people think they are above average (68% of faculty rate themselves in the top 25%), so showing them where they really stand changes behavior.
- **Leaders vs teams.** A leader should be driven by CD1, and team members are often driven by CD5 (pleasing the leader, not being the slacker). A leader who runs only on pleasing the team is an "insecure leader."
- **Competition in organizations is an oxymoron.** It shows a temporary surge in the top 10% while hiding the sinking motivation and anti-collaborative stress of the other 90%. Examples include GE's rank-and-yank and Microsoft's stack ranking. Measure collaboration instead: NBA teams with more assists win about 72% of the time, and "whatever is measured will be improved." Prefer **cooperative play**, which strengthens competition where it matters, in the marketplace. Arbitrary competition can serve a short sales campaign.
- **When competition works** (Mario Herger): when people focus on mastering a task, are gain-oriented (becoming the winner), care about the team's welfare, face an even matchup with a real chance, and know their competitors (friends rather than strangers). It fails in learning environments, under prevention focus (not being the loser), when creativity is required, and in skewed matchups.

**Techniques**
- **Mentorship #61**: systematized matching by compatibility, starting as early as recruiting ("If you manage to get an offer here, I will be your mentor"). It gives veterans an Endgame role. **Customer-support redesign**: veterans flip an "I am available for mentorship" switch and chat with newcomers on site, with "Talk to an actual customer rep" as a fallback. Reward mentorship hours with Boosters (photo kits, home pickup) rather than fee discounts. Everyone wins.
- **Brag Buttons #57 & Trophy Shelves #64**: bragging is explicitly broadcasting an accomplishment; a Trophy Shelf shows it off implicitly (diplomas on a wall, crowns and gear from difficult milestones). Most brag buttons are ignored because no strong CD2 is behind them. Place them at Major Win-States, when users actually feel awesome. Both need Relatedness: the audience must understand the difficulty.
- **Group Quests #22**: require group participation before anyone reaches the Win-State. They drive collaboration and virality. WoW raids need 40 players; Groupon deals unlocked only if "over 200 people buy this deal"; Kickstarter works the same way.
- **Social Treasures #63**: gifts or rewards only other players can give. In Farmville, "Give to Friend" items cost the giver nothing and were unobtainable any other way, so players invited friends ("just create a Farmville account, and I can give you a Goat too!"). Candy Crush lives work the same way, and so does a real-world *vote* in a popularity contest.
- **Social Prods #62**: an "action of minimal effort to create a social interaction, often a simple click": pokes, likes, +1s. LinkedIn Endorsements are Social Prods, deliberately easy, abundant, and meaningless, which finally gave users *something to do*. LinkedIn Recommendations, which take work, are Social Treasures.
- **Conformity Anchor #58**: displays how close users are to the social norm. Opower showed households their usage against similar neighbors and saved 2.6 terawatt hours. Top savers then drifted up toward the norm, so Opower added one smiley face for above average and two for the top tier.
- **Water Coolers #55**: a place to chat about non-work topics. It broadcasts the social norm (in Battle Camp, "We only accept coiners" normalized spending) and connects veterans with newcomers. Forums mingle an existing community but don't create one, and an empty forum is negative social proof. Build the community first, or you have "an office with a water cooler but no employees."

## CD6 Scarcity & Impatience

**Principles**
- **Exclusivity draws people in.** South Park's "Cartmanland": "So much fun in Cartmanland, but you can't come!" In *Up in the Air*, Ryan Bingham chases 10 million miles because "More people have walked on the moon." Geomon's Laurelix phoenix, catchable only above 110°F/40°C, was owned by three players, and Chou was still wondering months after quitting whether he could catch one on a trip.
- **Persuasively Inconvenient.** Oren Klaff's **Prizing** rests on three behaviors of the "croc" brain: we chase that which moves away from us, we want what we cannot have, and we only place value on things that are difficult to obtain. The infomercial line "If operators are busy, please call again" beat "Operators are waiting." Making everything maximally easy for a prospective date backfires.
- **Perceived Scarcity vs Objective Scarcity.** Higher prices signal higher value. A weaker PR firm charging $10K won $30,000 more than one charging $8K. A free audit is doubted; charge for it. Knee braces sell better at $49.99 than at $24.99.
- **Applying it.** Launch startups with confident premium pricing or well-structured exclusivity. Show users "what they may want but can't have - just yet." Customers rarely fail to buy because they can't afford something; they judge the perceived value not worth the cost in time, energy, or reputation. Faking limits or shutting servers on purpose breeds denial.

**Techniques**
- **Dangling #44**: regularly show an appealing thing users want but can't have yet, like the Farmville mansion. Players will spend $5 to skip 20 hours: "spending money, just so they could play *less* of the game." A club open only to royal blood creates an Anti Core Drive; adding "OR individuals who have previously ran a marathon" motivates.
- **Anchored Juxtaposition #69**: "place two options side by side: one that costs money, the other requiring a great amount of effort in accomplishing the Desired Actions which will benefit the system." Many users grind for hours and then pay anyway, so both outcomes serve the business. Both options must exist. "Pay now, or go away" provokes denial ("never gonna pay those greedy bastards"), and offering only effort makes users see that it benefits the system rather than them. Dropbox offers paid space or 500 MB per invited friend. Boosters make excellent rewards on the effort path.
- **Magnetic Caps #68**: "limitations placed on how many times a user can commit certain Desired Actions, which then stimulates more motivation to commit them." "Limit 12 Per Person" raised purchases 30-105% over "No Limit Per Person," because "by drawing limits, we're drawn *towards* the limit." **Procedure**: find the current upper bound of the metric and set the cap there (if 90% choose fewer than five hobbies, cap at five or six). Let power users unlock higher caps as they prove commitment (Evolved UI), with a cap still at the top. Abundance from lifting a cap is only temporary, so the designer "should always control the flow of scarcity."
- **Appointment Dynamics #21**: "utilize a formerly declared, or recurring schedule where users have to take the Desired Actions to effectively reach the Win-State," as with Happy Hours. eMart's "Sunny Sale" made a sculpture's shadow form a QR code coupon only from noon to 1 PM, and noon sales rose 25%.
- **Torture Breaks #66**: "a sudden and often triggered pause to the Desired Actions," with a relative timestamp ("Return 5 hours from now"). An Appointment Dynamic is an absolute time people look forward to. Breaks drive obsession rather than limiting play: users check back at 3, 5, and 6 hours. Pair with "pay $1 to remove the Torture Break," or ask a friend (Social Treasure), as Candy Crush does with lives.
- **Evolved UI #37**: start Onboarding with two or three options (not one, because CD3 loves choice), and unlock more features as Win-States are reached, until veterans hold a rich interface. Twenty features at the start produce decision paralysis. Withholding features is emotionally hard for companies. Gmail Labs is a small example.
- **Accidental scarcity** works too: Twitter's Fail Whale and flaky servers kept people checking. A system that is "sometimes working" is addictive; one that is down indefinitely loses interest.

## CD7 Unpredictability & Curiosity

**Principles**
- The lazy brain delegates to autopilot (Kahneman's System 1) until a threat or new information breaks the pattern. Klaff says people listen until they fit you into a pattern, so keep supplying the unexpected. Schell defines fun as "pleasure with surprises."
- A 10% chance of $100 often engages more than a sure $10. A known reward carries only the emotional value of the reward itself.
- **Sweepstakes and contests.** Effort must match the prize: an effortful essay for a simple prize (Tires Plus) becomes an Anti Core Drive. Dove's theme-fit "Real Beauty" contest made winners the "Faces of Dove." Coca-Cola Hong Kong's "Chok" had viewers shake their phones during the TV ad to catch caps; time, win, and prize were all unknown, and it was the city's most successful campaign in 35 years.
- **LuckyDiem for La Quinta, "Play & Stay"**: a slot Spin button (a Desert Oasis), recharging tokens (CD6), an instant "10 Free Nights" grand prize alongside accumulating points (CD2, CD4), and a dangled redeem button. It reached a K-Factor of 5.3 and a 712% sales lift. (K-factor = *i* × *c*: invites sent per user times the conversion rate of each invite. Above 1 is viral growth.) This is the *shell*; months of planning and balancing produced the result.
- **Mystery as marketing.** Blendtec's "Will It Blend?" lifted revenue 700%. Adobe's "Real or Fake" quiz showed Photoshop's power even through wrong answers. Google's "I'm Feeling Lucky" button is estimated to forgo $110 million a year in ads and is kept for playfulness; it is named for luck, not "One-Click Search." Woot sold one unknown product a day at midnight in limited quantity, and even its "Bag of Crap" became coveted.
- **Taiwan's Uniform Invoice Lottery** turned every receipt into a lottery ticket, so consumers demanded receipts and cash tax evasion fell. Tax revenue rose 75% in its first year. Governments can motivate instead of punishing harder.

**Techniques**
- **Glowing Choice #28**: an indicator that shines amid a complex environment and points to the next Desired Action, like WoW's glowing exclamation point over a quest-giver or a question mark on a key feature that disappears once clicked. It contrasts with Desert Oasis, which clears everything around the action. Candy Crush shows a possible move after a few idle seconds, because "feeling a sense of progress and ultimately losing is much better than feeling stuck and confused." "*Never allow your users to accidentally stumble upon a bad experience*"; users "should have to think hard and *decide* not to take the Desired Action." A dead end after a click penalizes the Desired Action.
- **Mystery Boxes / Random Rewards #72**: "participant receives an unknown reward by completing a required action." Loot drops sustain the Endgame. **Fixed-Action Rewards**, such as "Earned Lunch" (CD4), have known steps and known rewards.
- **Easter Eggs / Sudden Rewards #30**: "Mystery Boxes are unexpected rewards based on a certain *expected* trigger, Easter Eggs are rewards based on *unexpected* triggers." They spread by word of mouth, and users theorize about the trigger and repeat the actions they suspect earn it. Example: a small chance that Chase "picks up the tab" on a debit purchase.
- **Lottery / Rolling Rewards #74**: "somebody has to win during each period," and staying in raises your chances. For small groups this means Employee of the Week or a vacation draw, although cultures that frown on individual recognition resist it. At large scale, use low barriers, a substantial reward, and slim odds; brains can't tell one in ten million from one in a hundred million. Winners become fans for life.
- **Oracle Effect #71**: a prediction about the future keeps users engaged to see whether it comes true ("Will it be there?"), as with Waze's alert about a stalled car ahead.
- **Refreshing Content #73**: keeps the experience from going stale. It is one of the Right Brain elements to move toward after extrinsic hooks.

## CD8 Loss & Avoidance

**Principles**
- Farmville crops wither if not harvested in time: a triple loss of time, effort, and resources. Chou's tech-averse mother got up at 5 AM to harvest. CD8 raised daily active users until the Black Hat Rebound.
- **Affection Held Hostage.** A street artist threatened to smudge a finished portrait ("such a shame if this nice drawing got destroyed!"). Chou paid more than the price he had refused and never bought from a street vendor again. CD8 is "extraordinarily powerful in getting someone to take the Desired Action, but in the long run, it demoralizes the user's experience." It suits one-off transactions, not relationships that need later Desired Actions.
- **Sunk cost.** A poker player calls an all-in rather than give up chips already committed ("Pride is an expensive thing to possess"). Companies put good money after bad.
- **Playful fear.** *Zombies, Run!* uses small doses of Black Hat (being chased while running) inside a CD1, CD2, CD4, and CD7 experience.
- **CD8 flips other drives**: fear of a cause collapsing (CD1) or of looking like a loser (CD5). Look for places to add proactive loss-avoidance.
- **Avoiding the Avoidance**: in a tetanus study, only the group given a fear pamphlet *plus* a specific plan got vaccinated. Pair every threat with an exact action.
- **Kahneman's Fourfold Pattern**: people are risk-averse about low-probability losses and risk-seeking about high-probability ones. "Fear is what motivates us to stay alive, but hope is what many of us ultimately live for."

**Techniques**
- **Rightful Heritage #46**: "first makes a user believe something rightfully belongs to them..., and then makes them feel like it will be taken away if they don't commit the Desired Action." "Sign-up now to receive 3000 free credits" is ignored. The sequence "Great! Your actions have earned you 500 credits!" … "You now have 3000 credits. Sign-up to save your credits for later!" converts. Equity vesting with a one-year cliff keeps employees until the cliff, and they leave right after it without White Hat motivation. Granting raises only to employees holding outside offers rewards the undesired action.
- **Evanescent Opportunities #86**: an "opportunity that will disappear if the user does not take the Desired Action immediately," like Diablo's Treasure Goblin or a donor matching gifts for the next hour.
- **Countdown Timers #65**: a "visual display that communicates the passage of time towards a tangible event." It beats an expiration date. Show the smallest appropriate interval, often seconds. Near a goal, a timer combines with the **Last Mile Drive #53** (so close that users rush to finish), as in last-minute eBay bidding.
- **Status Quo Sloth #85**: the "lazy tendency of behavioral inertia." When a prospect says "I'll definitely sign up tomorrow," the true meaning of "tomorrow" is "never." Build it into the Endgame through engaging activity loops and investments (the Hook Model), so that quitting takes energy.
- **FOMO Punch #84**: Fear of Missing Out applies "Core Drive 8: Loss & Avoidance against itself," because fear of missing what could be yours breaks inertia. Jobs asked Sculley, "Do you want to spend the rest of your life selling sugar water, or do you want a chance to change the world?" It is strongest in Discovery. For voluntary workplace systems, communicate the mastery, recognition, and fun people miss. The Dutch Postcode Lottery's street prize plays on fear of watching the neighbors win.
- **Sunk Cost Prison #50**: "you invest so much time into something, that even when it's no longer enjoyable, you continue to commit the Desired Actions because you don't want to feel the loss of giving up on everything." Make sure users are accumulating things they know will be lost if they leave. Facebook holds "all your friends hostage." Deploy it when a user has a quick urge to leave, such as a competitor's limited promotion, and always pair it with White Hat (the impact they would give up). Ask regularly what makes users reluctant to let go.

## Core Drive combinations to design with

- CD2 usually results from CD3, CD4, or CD6, and leads into CD5 when users share the achievement.
- CD3 unlocks CD2, CD5 (collaborative play), and CD7. CD6 is often the first source of CD3: scarcity forces strategy.
- CD6 with CD7 is a great engine for online consumer action. CD6 with CD8 produces extremely urgent action.
- CD4 pulls with CD6, secures with CD8, obsesses with CD7, and sparks CD3 innovation.
- CD5 adds fun to CD3 and CD7, meaning to CD1, and a benchmark to CD4.
- Watch for drives that sabotage each other, such as extrinsic CD4 killing CD3 creativity.
- **Chain Combos #40**: when users master several drives, the benefits compound into "hyper-powered gameplay."
