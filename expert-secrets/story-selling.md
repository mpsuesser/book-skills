# Story selling

"Your goal isn't to try to sell anyone anything. Your goal is to guide them to their own decision." You can't hand someone the answer; plant it with a story so they come up with it themselves, and then the buying decision is theirs. A start-up whose pitch video tried to sell people on signing up switched to an Epiphany Bridge story and reached 1.5 million users in six weeks with no ad spend.

## The Epiphany Bridge

To tell it, get back into the state you were in before your aha, the spark phase, because that is the state your dream customers are in now. Recall the experience that caused the epiphany. "These big epiphanies always come from a guide," a person or inspiration from God. Brunson's: when Google raised his ad costs, a guide (Mike Filsaime) showed him the one-time offer, the upsell after the sale; one in three bought it, and that paid for the ads. Ask: "What was the epiphany that your guide gave to you?" and "What was the core Epiphany Bridge story that made you believe in the new opportunity that you are sharing with others?" Remember what happened, what was around you, and how you felt.

**Emotion, then logic.** "People don't buy logically, they buy based on emotion. Then they use logic to justify the purchase decision they've already made." A Ferrari is bought on emotion and status, then justified to the family with mileage, the sale price and the warranty. The epiphany is the bridge from the emotional side to the logical side: once emotionally bought in, people geek out, learn the terminology and the science, and sell themselves logically. Logic before the epiphany, **technobabble**, annoys and offends. "Logic doesn't sell. Emotions sell." Telling the story, you become the guide giving them the epiphany your guide gave you.

### Effective storytelling

- **Oversimplification.** Speak at about a third-grade level, as news stations do. In the 2016 Republican primary debates the winner spoke at a third-to-fourth-grade level; rivals spoke at eighth and ninth. "Using big words may make you feel smarter, but it will not influence others."
- **The "kinda like" bridge.** For every word or concept above a third-grade level, relate it to something they already know, as you would for a child: ketosis is "kinda like a way to lose weight by eating fat"; ketones are kinda like millions of little motivational speakers running through your body, or Pac-Man's power pellet. When someone doesn't know what a word means, they stop paying attention to everything said after it. Use the bridge at any point of friction.
- **Making them feel.** A flat retelling ("they killed my mom. I was really mad, so I blew the whole place up") is how most people tell stories. Fiction writers describe the room (the light, how it looks and feels) and then go deep into how the character feels. "You have to explain how you feel, and when you do that people will start to feel what you were feeling." Example: the office next to the kitchen, Post-it notes on the wall, kids washing up for dinner, a shooting pain in the stomach, pressure on the shoulders, sweaty palms but freezing, no money for Christmas. Unless the listener is in the state you were in at the aha, they miss the point ("I guess you had to be there").

## The Hero's Two Journeys

The Epiphany Bridge script descends from the storytelling frameworks:

- **Joseph Campbell's Hero's Adventure** (17 stages): Call to Adventure; Refusal of the Call; Supernatural Aid; Crossing the First Threshold; Belly of the Whale; Road of Trials; Meeting with the Goddess/Love; Temptation; Atonement with the Hero's Father; Peace and Fulfillment before the Hero's Return; Ultimate Boon; Refusal of the Return; Magic Flight; Rescue from Without; Return; Master of Two Worlds; Freedom to Live.
- **Christopher Vogler's Hero's Journey** (12 stages): The Ordinary World; The Call to Adventure; Refusal of the Call; Meeting the Mentor; Crossing the First Threshold; Tests, Allies, Enemies; Approach the Innermost Cave; The Ordeal; The Reward; The Road Block; The Resurrection; Return with the Elixir. (In the first Harry Potter: the cupboard; the Hogwarts letter; he doesn't believe he's a wizard; Hagrid; and so on to returning home happy to belong.)
- **Michael Hauge's second journey.** Campbell and Vogler describe the **journey of achievement**; Hauge adds the more important **journey of transformation**, the death of the hero's old identity and rebirth with new beliefs. Lightning McQueen gives up the race to push the King over the line; Rocky doesn't beat Apollo Creed. Neither wins the desire; both transform, and that invisible journey is the real one.

### The plot: character, desire, conflict

"Every good story is about a captivating character who is pursuing some compelling desire and who faces seemingly insurmountable obstacles to achieving it" (Hauge). The same three elements tell a story in 60 seconds or 60 minutes. Four phases:

1. **Separation from the ordinary world** (about the first 25 percent). Make the audience care:
   - **Build rapport**: give the hero at least two of: a victim of some outside force (we root for them), in jeopardy (we worry), likable (we want to be with them), funny (we connect), powerful (we want to be like them).
   - **Introduce the desire**: toward pleasure, **to win** (heart, fame, money, competition, prestige; really a status increase) or **to retrieve** (obtain something and bring it back); away from pain, **to escape** (get away from something painful) or **to stop** (keep something bad from happening).
2. **The journey, the conflict and the villain.** The first journey has a visible finish line; the second matters more, and many heroes never reach the desire or give it up. Conflict makes the emotional connection, and the number one tool for conflict is a villain: "The worse the villain is, the stronger the bond the audience will have with the hero." The villain can be a person or a false belief system; vilify the belief system and defeat it to give the audience the truth.
3. **The mentor/expert/guide** (Yoda, Gandalf). The guide gives the epiphany and the frameworks; the journey is the hero using the guide's plan to defeat the villain.
4. **The achievement and the transformation.**

## The Epiphany Bridge script, part by part

The 14 questions are in SKILL.md. What each part does:

- **Backstory.** Start where the listener is now, from where you were before the aha. An expert's accomplishments make them hard to relate to, so step down from the expert positioning to the beginning when you struggled with the same things. "When they see that you were once where they are now, they will have faith that you can take them where they want to go."
- **Desire.** To win, retrieve, escape or stop. The **external desire** drives the journey of accomplishment and people share it readily ("lose weight", "start my own company"). The **internal desire** drives the journey of transformation, from fear to courage; it is harder to know and share, but that vulnerability builds rapport faster than anything, because the audience has the same internal struggle.
- **Old vehicles.** Take them to the moment of frustration: the other vehicles you tried that hit a wall, the reason you (and they) will try something new.
- **The journey.** The desire turns from something you *should* do into something you *must* do, and you leave comfort for the unknown. **The call** is a feeling or impression, something learned, or an event that made you try again despite past failures. **The villain** is often the false belief holding you back or a belief system hurting the market: the person or idea you'll be throwing stones at. **What if** is what's at stake: the worst case if you fail.
- **New opportunity.** Help them feel what you felt. **The guide** may be a person, inspiration from God, or an idea that came while moving forward. **The epiphany** is the missing piece, and the goal is that they have the same aha. **The new opportunity** is the tangible thing you were given or created: this is where the opportunity switch happens, old vehicles for the new.
- **Framework.** The **strategy** you built from the guide's lesson; **your results** as the human guinea pig; **others' results** as case studies.
- **Achievement and transformation.** The payoff: the results the new opportunity brought, and who you became, the resolution of the internal struggle, "the death of the hero's identity and the rebirth of your new belief systems."

**Worked example ("So... what is a funnel?").** Backstory: in college, newly married, wanting to earn enough (external) for his wife to quit her two jobs and stay home with a family (internal); old vehicles, eBay and websites that cost more than they made. Journey: the call was seeing people sell how-to products, so he made a potato-gun DVD; the villain, Google raising his ad costs above what one sale a day brought in; what if, he quits wrestling or she keeps working two jobs. New opportunity: his guide showed him one extra step, an upsell, like fries and a Coke with the burger; one in three took it and the ads paid for themselves; the epiphany, "the secret to success online wasn't what I was selling, but how I was selling it." Framework: funnels tested in market after market; his results (leads, eight figures, charity) and others' results chosen to match the audience (a dentist hears about a dental practice). Achievement: millions a month; transformation: his wife home with five kids, hundreds of entrepreneurs freed. The typical response: "Do you think a funnel would work for what I do?"

**The 30-second Epiphany Bridge.** Skim each of the five phases without answering all 14 questions: wanted to support my wife; sold potato-gun DVDs until Google shut me down; a mentor showed me upsells and I discovered funnels; built funnel frameworks; made a lot of money, my wife retired and stays home with the kids. Inside a presentation, tell one big Epiphany Bridge story, then many smaller ones, each breaking another false belief. Stories are simple at the core; depth comes from settings, emotions and characters. Their purpose is to break old belief patterns and rebuild them with new ones.

## The four core stories

"My goal in my marketing is to rewrite the story that's inside of people's heads" (Craig Clemens). The stories people believe form their identity; change the stories and the identity shifts. The only real goal of marketing is to find the false beliefs and false stories that keep customers from success and rewrite them. The number one job is getting them to believe the new opportunity is the key to their result: if they believe, they'll put in the work. "If they already had the correct story in their mind, they would have already achieved their desired result."

### How a story is rewritten

**Experience → Story → Belief.** Beliefs exist to keep us safe and protect status, and they also hold us back; the same experience can produce different stories. When you present a new opportunity, the subconscious checks it against existing stories; if it doesn't fit, they're skeptical, and you need a better story that gives the epiphany. The process:

1. **Chains of false belief**: the belief keeping them from the new opportunity (if stuck, recall the beliefs *you* held before your journey).
2. **Experience**: the experience that likely created it.
3. **Story**: the story they now tell themselves.
4. **New Epiphany Bridge**: the story that rewrites it, usually from your life (someone else's works), showing you once believed the same thing.

Examples:
- Weight loss: belief "If I try to lose weight, I'm going to be miserable"; experience, cut carbs last year and was miserable; story, "I have to give up things that make me happy if I want to lose weight"; new bridge, I thought so too, until I learned about ketosis and that I can lose weight "by drinking ketones instead of cutting carbs."
- Network marketing: belief "If I join a network marketing/MLM program, I could lose my friends"; experience, tried to sign up family and they got mad; story, "People have to bug their friends and family to have success in network marketing"; new bridge, I thought so too, until I learned that I can generate leads online and "grow my team without involving my friends or family."

"Stories are the keys to belief": tell stories that show the truth and they sell themselves. Brunson tells 50 or more stories in the first 60 minutes of a presentation, almost one a minute, each from under a minute to ten minutes, each breaking a false belief.

### The four stories in every selling presentation

1. **The origin story** of you and the new opportunity: the Epiphany Bridge of how you discovered it, told closest to the full script, usually first, with the most time on backstory, journey, guide, frameworks, results and transformation. It breaks their beliefs in the other opportunities they're using. Afterward they should believe the new opportunity is the best way to the result.
2. **The vehicle framework story**: how you learned or earned the framework itself, followed by its strategy (the what).
3. **The internal beliefs story**: how you discovered you could do it yourself, plus examples of others just like them. (They believe in funnels but think they aren't technical enough.)
4. **The external beliefs story**: breaking the outside force they think will stop them. (They could build a funnel but can't drive traffic.)

### Building a story inventory

An expert can talk for hours without notes because of a story inventory. Use a **false belief patterns** worksheet: columns Chains of False Belief → Experiences → Stories → New Epiphany Bridges; row groups for the Origin Story, Vehicle Story, Internal Belief Story and External Belief Story.

1. **Chains of false belief**: list every false belief about the new opportunity, the vehicle frameworks, their own ability and external forces; dozens for each. Your own past beliefs are the best source.
2. **Experiences**: beside each, the experience that created it.
3. **Stories**: the exact story they tell themselves.
4. **New Epiphany Bridges**: an Epiphany Bridge story for each false belief, yours or others'.

Then practice telling each with the Epiphany Bridge script. The better you get at the stories, the better you persuade and the faster you know what to say when an objection comes up.
