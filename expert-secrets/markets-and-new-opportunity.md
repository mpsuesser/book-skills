# Markets and the new opportunity

Decide who you serve before you create offers. Offers that fail are usually good products: the owner doesn't know who they're selling to, where those people already buy, or what positioning to use. With the right market positioning, even C- or D-level funnels and sales skills make it almost impossible to fail. "There's no relationship between being good and getting paid. However, there's a huge relationship between being good at marketing and getting paid" (Joe Polish).

## The three core markets or desires

**Health, wealth and relationships.** Ask: "Which of these three desires are my future dream customers trying to receive when they buy my product or service?"

- **A product that fits more than one**: the message still focuses on one. "Any time you try to get your potential customer to believe in two things, your conversions will usually cut in half (most times by 90 percent or more)."
- **A product that fits none**: "If your product doesn't fit into one of the three core desires, then your sales message at least must!" A razor ad that ends with the man winning the woman sells relationships.

### Submarkets

The core markets are red oceans (bloody with sharks); blue oceans have no competition. Inside each core desire sit **submarkets**: wealth holds finance, investing, real estate, sales; health holds diet, nutrition, strength training, weight loss; relationships hold love, dating advice, marriage advice. Ask what submarkets people use to try to get the desire met, and which one your offer fits. Then three qualifying questions:

1. **Would people in this submarket be excited about the new opportunity and frameworks you will present?** You will pull them out of the submarket into your new niche.
2. **Are they irrationally passionate?** First, are you? Signs in the market:
   - **Communities**: forums, Facebook groups, YouTube channels, podcasts, blogs.
   - **Vocabulary**: its own language ("split testing", "squeeze pages"; "ketones", "blood tests").
   - **Events**: conferences, seminars, summits, masterminds. Without them, it's hard to get people to a webinar.
   - **Other experts**: celebrities and gurus already thriving selling information. "You don't want to be the first celebrity in a market."
3. **Are they willing and able to spend money on information?** Kids who play video games are willing but not able (no credit cards); engineers may be able but not willing (one coach spent a year with few results, switched markets, and succeeded "overnight").

### Niches and your own category

A **niche** is a specific way to fulfill the submarket's desire: health → nutrition → ketogenic, paleo or carnivore diet; health → weight loss → for women, college students, couples; wealth → real estate → short-selling, flipping houses, flipping houses on eBay; wealth → online business → Amazon, Shopify, Facebook ads; relationships → parenting → toddlers, teenagers, marriage after the kids leave; relationships → dating → how to talk to a girl, recovering after a breakup, knowing if you're in love. List all the niches inside your submarket.

The mistake is to pick an existing niche: it is someone else's blue ocean, and by the third to fifth entrant it's bloody. Create your own **category** and become its **category king** (from *Play Bigger*): the king takes 70 to 80 percent of a category's profits and market value, is not necessarily first but is the best marketer, is almost impossible to dethrone, and leaves everyone else fighting over 20 to 30 percent. Landing-page builders, email, marketing automation and split testing each already had a king, so ClickFunnels created the category of sales funnels. Ask: "What new category can you create and become its category king?"

## Market selection and positioning

**The market is a location, not a person.** "A market is a place for buyers and sellers to meet and exchange goods and services." At a farmers market you're not the market; you're a dream customer for the vendors. Ask not "Who is your market?" but "Where is your market, and who is your dream customer who's already going there?" Until you have your own market, fish for frustrated customers in the red oceans where your dream customers already congregate.

### Fishing in the red oceans: target only one group

| Group | Who they are | Tell | What switching requires |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Diehard** | The believer with the logo tattooed on their forehead | You've entered a feature war with them | A major identity shift; selling to them is futile and expensive. Sales copy written for Diehards is a common error |
| **The Satisfied** | Use the product, aren't in love; switching feels more painful than staying. Most people | They price-shop you, look up the value of everything in your offer, guess how long switching will take | A significant price or value benefit |
| **The Frustrated** | Use it and hate it; actively seeking something better, may feel they've exhausted all options | They list what they hate about their current product without being asked | Finding them, earning their trust, a small amount of education |

Decide ahead of time to sell only to the Frustrated: the shortest sales cycle, buyers looking for reasons to give you money, and fulfillment you'll enjoy.

### Competitive or complementary positioning

"Market positioning is how people fit you into what they already know."

- **Competitive**: anchor off what they know and throw rocks at it. Dove stepped out of the soap market and sold back into it ("It's not soap; it's Dove!"), positioned as a moisturizer that also cleans. ClickFunnels called out frustrated website owners: "It's not a website; it's a funnel." A good positioning tagline declares how you fit into what customers already know and hands you a natural sales message.
- **Complementary**: complement what's already there. Sunkist sold a juice extractor for 10 cents with a bundle of oranges ("Drink an Orange") and grew the market without throwing rocks.

### Do your homework

Find 20 or 30 other gurus in the market, consume everything they offer, see what they teach and where you can carve a unique spot. Learn the market's history and competitive landscape, where you fit, where to create your category, and where the frustrated dream customers gather.

## The new opportunity

The iPod didn't promise a better CD player; it opened a new category with a new opportunity, your whole music collection in your pocket. Movements appeal not to people bolstering a cherished self but to those "who crave to be rid of an unwanted self" (Eric Hoffer). "Our goal is not to fix what's not working. Our goal is to replace what's not working with something altogether different."

### Why people don't want improvement offers

- **Improvement is hard.** They've tried it and it didn't work, so the pain is known. A new opportunity has no known pain: "Experience is a handicap" (Hoffer).
- **Desire vs. ambition.** Everyone has desire; less than 2 percent of people are ambitious. Improvement offers sell to the ambitious and exclude the other 98 percent; new opportunities play on desire.
- **Memories of poor past decisions.** Improving forces them to admit past choices were wrong; a new opportunity justifies past failures.
- **Commodity pricing.** Improvements compete on price in a red ocean. "There is a huge strategic advantage of being the most expensive" (Dan Kennedy); in a new opportunity's blue ocean, price resistance goes out the window.
- **Status**, the biggest reason.

### Status

Status is the only thing that causes people to move or not move. Every buying decision runs the question "Is this thing I'm considering going to increase my status or decrease it?" (school, dating, marriage, cars). Status rises through the appearance of intelligence, power, wealth, style, physical appearance or happiness: focus the message on one core desire but stack as many status increases as apply. Fear of a status decrease ("What if I try this and it decreases my status? I will feel stupid.") stops the sale; 27 failed diets means a very high fear. People accept a temporary decrease (spending money) when the brain judges it temporary and the future gain higher. Status works at both ends: people buy a modest car for fear of what others will think.

Exercise: under your new opportunity, list five status increases and five status decreases. Add increasers and remove decreasers with a stronger offer, money-back guarantees, risk reversals and done-for-you options. An improvement offer always carries a large decrease: admitting the earlier choice was wrong.

### Why people crave new opportunities

- **New discovery**: sharing something new raises status immediately (why people forward viral videos).
- **No pain of disconnect**: they admit no bad decision.
- **Dream replacement**: fear of failure kills dreams; "where there is no vision, the people perish." A new opportunity gives a new dream.
- **Greener pastures**: you don't convince them their grass is green or fix it; you let them follow you to the other side of the fence.

### How to create a new opportunity

The same four steps reposition an existing product.

1. **What is the result your dream customers are trying to achieve?** Check its core desire. Use Dan Sullivan's question: "If we were having this discussion three years from today, and you were looking back over those three years, what has to have happened in your life, both personally and professionally, for you to feel happy with your progress?" Talk to dream customers until you know the result they want most.
2. **What vehicle are they currently using to get it?** The niches are the vehicles, and yours is rarely their first (the average woman in one weight-loss community diets five times a year; people chasing extra income try eBay, Craigslist, Amazon, Shopify). "They aren't coming to you because they aren't trying; they're coming to you because the current vehicle isn't getting them to their destination." Write down every vehicle they've tried without success.
3. **The opportunity switch.** Hand them the keys to a new vehicle rather than making the old one "er." For funnels, Brunson declared websites dead, showed the money spent for little return, threw rocks at every other vehicle (websites, autoresponders, marketing automation, CRMs, landing-page builders), told people it wasn't their fault, and then switched them to funnels. "Opportunity switching takes consumers out of the pain they're currently in and gives them hope for a new future through a new vehicle." The switch can go niche to niche or submarket to submarket (real estate investors into internet marketing). Everything on the front end of the value ladder (books, videos, webinars, podcasts) is an opportunity switch, and break-even front-end funnels can fish colder submarkets (local service businesses, ecommerce sellers, network marketers, freelancers). Write ads, lead magnets and front-end funnels through this lens.
4. **The opportunity stack.** "Every business should have one and only one opportunity switch." Brunson's early habit of making every offer a new switch confused customers, lost trust, made lists less responsive and nearly crashed the business. At his first big event, instead of a new opportunity he offered a certification inside the funnel opportunity, hoping for 50 sales; more than 150 people rushed the table. Every offer after the first should "take the new opportunity that dream customers already believed in, and package it in different ways to help serve them at a higher level." ClickFunnels stacks the funnel opportunity as software, a 30-day challenge, copywriting software, a live event, an agency course, a membership, a newsletter, a coaching program and a book; a health company might stack supplements, coaching and meal plans.
