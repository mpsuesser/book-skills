# Particular occasions: rules 32–34

Letters and emails, public speaking, and English for nonnative speakers.

## 32 Use Standard Forms in Letters

- **Capitalize by the standard rules** in email. Never "i went to boston"; it makes you "look like a self-indulgent little idiot."
- **Reread every email slowly** for typos, "and reread to cool the tone. You will never regret not sending an email the very moment you finish it. Wait a day, and reconsider whether you actually want to make an enemy for life."
- **Salutation**: "Never start emails with 'Hi Deirdre' or 'Hi professor.'" Follow the conventions of old-style letters:
  - "Dear Ms. McCloskey" or "Dear Professor McCloskey," followed by a colon in a business letter or a comma if somewhat less formal.
  - "Dear Deirdre," always with a comma, on a first-name basis.
- **Closing**, on a new line: "Sincerely," for a formal letter; "Regards," if informal; "Love," if on intimate terms and you are a woman, or "Warm regards" if you are a man not actually in love with the person. Then your name on its own line: first name or whole name according to familiarity.
- **Writing to a famous person**: not "You don't know me, but . . ." nor "You will not remember me, but . . ." These feel humble but are "emotionally coercive and childish and undignified." Signal familiarity in the salutation ("Dear George"), or remind gently ("It was so pleasant to meet you when . . ."). If you have never met, introduce yourself briefly.
- **Handwriting**: if you print, develop an adult set of print letters and stick to it. Write lowercase and uppercase in their correct roles (no scrunched little capitals for lowercase), extend the tails of *b, d, f, h, k, l, t* above the halfway line and of *g, j, p, q, y* below the baseline, and let capitals fill the line.

## 33 Treat Speaking in Public as a Performance

- "Learn to do it by doing it." McCloskey stutters and got over the terror; you can become relaxed about public speaking too.
- **Make yourself heard.** A small voice is "not... an attractively modest habit. It is simply hard to hear, and irritating."
- "Any speaking, like any writing, is a performance. You need to treat it theatrically." Improvisation classes or Toastmasters help.
- **"Never tell your audience the outline of what you are going to say."** "I'm going to tell you about buzz, buzz, buzz" bores them. "Just *do* the buzz, buzz, buzz."
- **Jokes only if you are a natural comic.** The test: "do people regularly laugh uproariously at your jokes?" If not, stop trying.
- No nervous snickering or giggling.
- **Memorize what you are going to say**, "not word for word... but as a matter of mastering what you are asserting, in outline. Then let your knowledge or passion about the subject fill in the gaps. Stand and deliver." Keep written outlines out of your hand, and "*never, ever stand reading your PowerPoint with your back to the audience*."
- A brief opening introduction of yourself is all right if charming or to the point; it can put the audience at ease. "After such brief introductions... *never again* talk about yourself. Talk about the subject." The exceptions are a personal experience right on point, or a talk whose subject *is* you.
- "PowerPoint is evil" (Tufte's squib of that title). "Death by PowerPoint has become the usual method of execution of audiences in the world."
- **For workshops**, see the ten-minute rule in [house-rules.md](house-rules.md).

## 34 Advice for Nonnative English Speakers

**Pronunciation** mistakes common among excellent nonnative speakers:

- *rhetoric*: "RET-or-rik," no breathy *r* and no *s* at the end; not like "politics."
- *salmon*: "sa-mon," with a silent *l*. English spelling is insane because of the Great Vowel Shift of the 1400s combined with keeping the historical spellings. One advantage is that spelling needn't follow any one dialect's pronunciation.
- *architect*: "ar-ki-tekt."
- *Keynesian*: "KANES-ian," three syllables, rhyming with "brains."
- *debt*: "det." *heir*: like "air." *sword*: "sord." *answer*: "AN-sir."
- *contribute*: American "con-TRIB-yute."
- *persuade*: "per-SUADE."

**Usage**:

- **Loan/borrow and teach/learn** are distinct: you loan to a friend, who borrows from you; you teach students, who learn from you.
- "Explain them X" should be "explain X **to** them." Plain "explain X" is fine when no learners are named.
- "He accepted to" should be "he agreed to."
- **The continuative** is overused: "My father is playing the piano" says he is playing right now. For the habit, write "My father plays the piano."
- Languages drift. "Except for that" in place of "except that" is spreading among native speakers; it makes no sense, but it is coming.
