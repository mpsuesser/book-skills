# House Rules: teaching, grading, and workshopping

McCloskey's handouts from teaching writing in every course she gave, plus her practice in editing others and running workshops. Use them for a fast screen of a draft, for grading, or for coaching a writer.

## Top ten signs of not yet having a grownup writing style

10. Using *the fact that* or *due to*.
9. Using *the former* or *the latter*.
8. Using *this* or *these* too much when *the* or *such a* would do.
7. Not spellchecking.
6. Not double spacing.
5. Not getting the citation punctuation right.
4. Not inserting that second comma in A, B, and C.
3. Still thinking that a semicolon is the same as a colon.
2. "Still, using, too, many, commas."
1. Still not using two spaces after a final stop (period, exclamation point, question mark).

## Another ten signs

1. Using commas religiously after introductory clauses.
2. "This . . . this . . . this."
3. Starting sentences with "However,".
4. Using the word *process*.
5. Using *not only . . . but also*.
6. Not grasping the difference between a colon and a semicolon.
7. Not reading out loud: silliness, rhymes.
8. No marks on the paper: no last proofreading.
9. Not using two spaces after a period, !, or ?.
10. Full justification.

## The handout rules

- **Serial comma**: X, Y, and Z; X, Y, or Z. Without it the reader may take "X and another thing combining Y and Z."
- **Two spaces after a period, one after a comma**: "the old convention of typewriters and... easier to read."
- **Colon versus semicolon**: "when B *follows from or illustrates* A, then use a colon (:)...; when B is *parallel or similar to* A, use a semicolon (;)."
- **No comma after an initial phrase**: "Considering the options the B choice is best." Add one only when the sentence would otherwise confuse, which almost never happens when the next phrase starts with *the* or *this*. Put commas in by ear.
- **People take *who* or *whom***, not *that* or *which*: "someone who works for the FBI"; "The people *who* we saw."
- **Singular *they***: don't use it to dodge *he* or *she*. Often no such bind exists: "In order for a business to prosper they need to find a market" should be "it needs." "When you do have a he/she problem, pick one and go with it." Never *and/or* or *he/she*.
- **Opinions**: get "I believe," "I think," and "In my opinion" out of your writing. Self-deprecation is good in conversation and "fatal to an argument in prose." *I* is fine "if your opinion is the point." "When you are talking about *I* you aren't talking about the subject, are you?"
- **Citation punctuation**: the period goes after the citation, "is ten to one (Jacobsen, 282)." No ellipses before and after a quotation: ". . . buzz, buzz . . ." is wrong. In American printing the period goes inside the quote marks.
- **Repetition**: "Don't repeat. Ask yourself: Am I just filling in here?"
- **Start sentences with *And* or *But*** freely, and with *But* rather than *However*.
- **Humor**: "Don't be afraid of being funny, if you can pull it off."
- **Form**: no title pages, no padding. Always staple, and always indent the first line of a paragraph. "Little carelessnesses... get your reader off to a bad start. Imagine that reader as your boss."
- **Reading out loud** corrects many infelicities ("unhappy turns of phrase").
- **Grading marks**: a circle means something is wrong (a bad word choice, or a word contrary to "the Holy Writ of *Economical Writing*"). A check mark means "Hmm. Good point!" A suggested word means you misused yours.

## Diagnostic quiz

Each item plants one or more faults for the writer to name and fix. The fault is given after the arrow.

1. "The President went to see the Chinese premier, so that the Chief Executive could discuss the Korean situation with the Chinese leader." → elegant variation (McZ 20), and *situation* (McZ 26).
2. *Landes thinks "Europe is neat". (Landes, p. 467)* → citation and quotation punctuation (McZ 22).
3. "During the 19th century, the United Kingdom included Great Britain and Ireland." / "Unfortunately, Landes is Eurocentric." → the needless introductory comma (McZ 22), and *unfortunately* as cheap opinion (McZ 26).
4. "England, Scotland and little Wales make up the island of 'Great Britain'." → the missing serial comma, the scare quotes, and the period outside the quote mark (McZ 22, 29).
5. "This paragraph has a problem. This author doesn't even notice that she has this problem..." → this-ism (McZ 30).
6. "Not only is using the phrase *due to* unnecessarily fancy, but it also is a sign of childish writing." → *not only . . . but also* (McZ 17).
7. A paragraph typed with one space after each period → sentence ends blur (McZ 22).
8. "The process of the process of writing is a difficult process. This process of rewriting is important in the process." → *process* (McZ 26). Rewrite without it.

## Editing other people's writing

- **Detailed copyediting** of someone else's draft "isn't effective rhetorically" when the writer resists being edited. "Sitting down with students and going slowly over a paragraph, word by word, does help."
- **The professional attitude**, which daily journalists learn the hard way, is that "the (competent, tasteful) editor knows best": "if a good writer and editor... tells me something is wrong, it is."
- **The reader is always right** ("Give the lady what she wants"). Length is no fault "if each sentence and each paragraph is worth the candle."
- **Your own drafts**: "I reread obsessively, cold, following the Graves and Hodge technique of stopping when I can't follow it or I see an infelicity." Word processing tempts you "to preserve one's golden prose even when it's actually a brick. Waiting long enough so that you see your prose as foreign helps."
- **Even without overcoming their fears**, writers can raise their prose with the tricks alone, starting with "leave off anticipating ('This paper is organized as follows')."

## Workshops

- **"The key is to love your colleagues."** Stay together long enough to drop the academic pose ("Heh, I'm the expert here") and learn to listen. Love lets the writer whose work is tested "accept criticism gracefully, since she knows it is meant in love." What works is cooperation, not competition.
- **The ten-minute rule** (from the Project on Rhetoric of Inquiry at Iowa): the author may not present the paper. She gets ten minutes at the outset to add what she wants, and then discussion begins. The audience therefore has to read the paper beforehand. A presented or read-aloud paper lets the author filibuster, leaves a few minutes for discussion, and does nothing to improve the paper.
- **Before the workshop**: "Getting someone to criticize a piece of writing early is a good practice." It is "better to be criticized harshly by friends in private, and fix what's wrong, than to be massacred in public."
