# The paragraph and the sentence: rules 14–24

Rules for paragraphs, for the nonprose parts of a paper (tables, graphs, equations, footnotes), and for sentences. Sentence rules matter most in revision.

## 14 A Paragraph Should Have a Point

- "The paragraph should be a more or less complete discussion of one topic."
- **Something new in every paragraph**: "Don't let a paragraph go by in which you do not tell the reader something she doesn't know or doesn't at present believe." A surprising detail or a fresh short quotation will do. Without it she starts skipping.
- **Paragraphing is punctuation**, like stanzas: a pause after a bit of discussion, a shift of tone, a break for the reader.
- **Gerald Graff's trick**: "make the last sentence a simple, street-talk encapsulation or punctuation of what you've been saying, a sentence Mom could understand instantly. The paragraph can be as technical as you want as long as in the last sentence you come down a notch or two." It makes the paragraph sing.
- Paragraphs that are too long exhaust the reader. One-sentence paragraphs give "a breathless quality" and are "a cheap trick" of sportswriters.
- **Block quotations** (more than four typed lines, indented, no quotation marks around the whole) have two legitimate jobs:
  - **Give the devil his due**: quote at length an argument you will then "rip it to pieces, word by word." Mild criticism can't follow a big quote.
  - **Give an angel her voice**: quote something "*strikingly* well expressed with which you entirely agree."
  - Routine explanations in other people's words, long or short, "convey the impression that you think with your scissors."
- **Plagiarism** is "using other people's turns of phrase with the intent of claiming them as your own." Don't.

## 15 Make Tables, Graphs, Displayed Equations, and Labels on Images Readable by Themselves

- "Tables and graphs are writing, and the usual rules of writing therefore apply." Ask: **"Are these data necessary? Would I drone on in a similar way in prose?"** Don't give annual income from 1900 to 1980 when the issue is growth over the span. Give statistics "in the simplest form consistent with their use." The calculator's eight digits accomplish nothing; most questions need only rough figures.
- **The rule**: "the reader should be able to understand the point being made by the table without referring to the main text." State the point as a declarative sentence in the title. If you can't, you don't yet know the point: think again.
- Headings in words, not computer acronyms: not "LPDOM" but "Logarithm of the Domestic Price." The aim is to be clear, "not to be Phony Scientific."
- Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*: "Mobilize every graphical element, perhaps several times over, to show the data." Avoid **chart junk** (colors, shading, and boxes that only ornament). "Have a point."
- **Deirdre's Rules**:
  - Titles that announce the theme: "All Conferences Should Happen in the Midwest," not "A Model of Transport Costs."
  - "Meaningful, spelled-out names for lines, points, and areas": "Rich Budget Line," not "Locus QuERtY."
  - Equations in words: "Quantity of Grain = 3.56 + 5.6 (Price of Grain) − 3.8 (Real Income)," not "Q = 3.56 + 5.6P − 3.8Y, where Q is quantity of grain, P its price, and Y real income." "Anyone can retrieve the algebra from the words, but the reverse is pointlessly harder." Halmos: "I deny that anybody thinks in [such] terms."
- **In humanistic fields**, modernize old spelling and punctuation in quotations, as the Oxford Shakespeare does. "The past is a foreign country, but the foreignness should be exhibited in its strange behavior and strange ideas, not in its conventions of printing."

## 16 Footnotes and Other "Scholarly" Tics Are Pedantic

- "A footnote should be subordinate. That is why it is at the foot." Footnotes are no substitute for good arrangement: "If the idea doesn't fit maybe it doesn't belong. Anything worth saying in your essay or report is worth saying in the main text." Never break a sentence with a footnote mark, which invites the reader to look away.
- **"Footnotes should guide the reader to the sources. That is all."** Citing the textbook in the mantle of The Scholar looks foolish. Citing the commonplaces of the culture ("to be or not to be") "doesn't pass the LOL test."
- **Cite the exact page.** A bare "(Fish 2001)" lets the reader neither build on the source nor check it, and finding the page makes you reread and confirm you got it right.

## 17 Make Your Writing Cohere

- **The Rule of Coherence**: "Make writing hang together." The reader can follow writing that hangs together, from phrases to whole books, and can't follow irrelevancies.
- **Transitive writing** links each sentence to the one before by repeating its words: (AB)(BC)(CD), not (ABZYZ)(MNOP)(BJKL). "To do it you must violate the schoolmarm's rule of not repeating words." Use pronouns (*it*, *them*) to relieve monotony, and **polyptoton** (the same root in different forms: *linking*, *linkages*; *repetition*, *repeat*).
- An editor to a cub reporter: "It doesn't much matter what your first sentence is... But the third damn well better follow from the first and second." Once you start a metaphor or a tone, carry it through. Reread, "unifying the tenses of the verbs, unifying the vocabulary, unifying the form."
- **The clumsy way** begins every sentence with a linking word, a habit of Ciceronian Latin and Greek that fails in English. It pushes the reader around, telling her when to take a sentence illustratively ("indeed"), adversatively ("yet," "however," "but"), sequentially ("furthermore," "therefore"), or concessively ("to be sure").
- **"Not only . . . but also"** is a **calque** (literal translation) of Cicero's *non solum . . . sed etiam*. "It marks you as incompetent. The best writers never use it."
- "English achieves coherence by repetition, not by frantic signaling."

## 18 Use Your Ear

- Sentences all of one length and construction make a monotonous paragraph, unless the repetition has a dramatic reason ("I have a dream").
- **Gardner's Rule** (from the novelist John Gardner): an English sentence has three parts, subject, verb, and object. Vary sentences by how much you put into each, and "in each sentence choose only *one* of the three parts for elaboration." "A sentence with too much in all three of its parts can ruin a paragraph": complex subject, simple verb, simple object.

## 19 Write in Complete Sentences

- Use fragments "only occasionally, and only for a reason" (a dramatic surprise). The point is not school grammar but "not raising expectations that you don't fulfill."
- **The test**: could it stand alone as an isolated remark? Said by a stranger on the street, "As a fluent speaker of English," leaves you waiting for the rest.

## 20 Avoid Elegant Variation

- "The first duty in writing a sentence is to make it clear. **Use one word to mean one thing.**" The positive rule is Strunk and White's, "Express parallel ideas in parallel form." The negative rule is Fowler's, "Avoid elegant variation." Parallel form lets the reader get what she expects and fit the novelties into what she already knows.
- **Elegant variation** "uses many lovely words to mean one thing," until neither reader nor writer knows what is meant. One paper on economic development used, in two pages, "industrialization," "growing structural differentiation," "economic and social development," "social and economic development," "development," "economic growth," "growth," and "revolutionized means of production."
- It comes "draped in five-dollar words": "indifferent harvests of 1815 and calamitous volume deficiencies of 1816" means bad crops and very bad crops. It "wastes the reader's attention in the solving of stupid puzzles."
- The fix: "When uncertain what word or phrase to use, look back to the past few paragraphs for the same idea, and use the same word or phrase. Repeat!" "The democratic side of the Allies... against the other half" must repeat either "side" or "half."
- Finish parallels: "Osso *sailed* to Sicily and *founded* the Cosa Nostra, Mastrosso *travelled* to Naples and *set up* the Camorra" varies the verbs and breaks the pattern.
- Variation also implies distinctions that don't exist: in "concerned not only with what happened but also with why events turned out as they did," the reader wonders whether "what happened" and the "events" differ.
- **The reverse fault, pointless repetition**: the same word in different senses. "Ideology *runs* the show" followed by a mother who "*runs* into a burning house" should become "controls." Repeating "very much" in successive sentences links nothing. Rereading cold catches both.
- "But don't vary your words just to please Ms. Jones."

## 21 Watch How Each Word Connects with Others

- First drafts are "jammed with elegant variation, traffic signals, illogical sentences, non-sentences, misquotations, boilerplate, monotonies, and jingles." Johnson: "What is written without effort is in general read without pleasure." **Check and tighten.**
- **Test every sentence for redundancy.** "The book often identifies what exactly was wrong with economic orthodoxy": "often," "identifies," and "exactly" all convey "regular precision." Drop all but one.
- **Gapping** is carrying words over from one construction into its parallel. Inspect every opportunity and decide by reading cold: "and for two centuries has worked" or "and for two centuries it has worked"; "to admire, or at least tolerate" or "or at least to tolerate"; "the beautiful and damned" or "the beautiful and the damned."
- **Modify parallels symmetrically**: "Smith and Milton Friedman, not Rousseau and Thomas Piketty." Famous names stand alone, less famous ones get first names. "If you have one first name for the not-so-famous, you have to have the other too."
- **Epithets** ("the famous economist Milton Friedman"): give them when the reader needs them, always with *the*. The *Time*-magazine "famous economist Milton Friedman" is not yet domesticated in English.
- Gerunds ("supplying") and infinitives ("to supply") substitute for each other; use the swap to avoid pointless repetition or to keep a parallel.
- **Put modifiers close to the word they modify.** The passive sometimes helps: "what the core of economics was actually saying—see *Human Action*" becomes "what was actually being said in the core of economics—see *Human Action*."
- **Use the few resources of inflection** English has (number, gender in pronouns, verb forms) to fix reference. "Owners of the original and indestructible powers of the soil earned from them pure rents, and that tenant farmers were willing to pay them..." leaves "them" ambiguous. "An owner... earned from them pure rents, and that the tenant farmers were willing to pay him..." makes each pronoun agree with only one referent. Assigning *she* and *he* to two people (writer and reader, say) does the same.
- **Capitals** make a word a concrete Proper Noun: "the Coase Theorem" is more vivid than "the proposition that property rights matter to allocation in the case of high transaction costs," and a capitalized Point in a diagram is easy to point at. Capitals have "an Ironic Air," fun only in moderation.

## 22 Watch Punctuation

- **Spacing**: in a manuscript, one space after a comma, semicolon, or colon, and two after a period, question mark, or exclamation point. "Just do it. Don't argue." The publisher's typesetting removes them.
- **Quotation marks**: in American printing "the close quote goes *outside* the comma or the period, thus." With author-date citations: "how one treats a quotation" (McCloskey 2019). The period comes after the citation, never "...mark". (McCloskey 2000). British printing puts the period outside the quote mark and uses single quotes, with double quotes inside them.
- **Brackets** hold parentheses within parentheses, or your own insertions into someone else's sentence by way of comment or clarification.
- **The dash** is "a parenthesis spoken in a louder tone of voice," a super comma. It is overused to patch a badly organized sentence. Use the em dash, not the shorter en dash.
- **Colon and semicolon**, grossly overused in academic writing. "The colon (:) means roughly 'to be specific'": an illustration follows. "The semicolon (;) means roughly 'likewise' or 'also'": a parallel addition. The semicolon also separates long items in a series and is a "period lite" for splicing two sentences, since it contains both a comma and a period. McCloskey removed dozens of both from the book, splitting the joined sentences in two: "Doing so adds to clarity."
- **The Oxford Comma** (serial comma), rigorously: "Faith, hope, and charity." Without it, hope-and-charity reads as one item. Legal cases have turned on it.
- **The Comma Splice** joins two clauses that could each stand alone with only a comma. They need a period, semicolon, or colon, or a conjunction: not "The citizens lived in fear, the result was poor economic growth," but "The citizens lived in fear. The result was poor economic growth," or "...fear, and the result was poor economic growth."
- **Too many commas**, placed by rule (always after an if-clause or an introductory phrase), slow the pace for no gain and end up separating subject from verb. **The revision trick**: delete most commas before *the*, since *the* signals a new phrase well enough.
- **"Punctuate by ear rather than by rule."** Put a comma where the spoken pause wants it, or where you stumble on rereading without it. Use commas heavily only for a deliberately ponderous tone, and lightly for most writing. Flaubert spent a weekend striking a comma, then putting it back.

## 23 The Order Around Switch Until It Good Sounds

- English word order carries meaning ("man bites dog"), yet much can still be moved. "Tinker with the sentence until it sounds good." **Cultivate the habit of mentally rearranging the order of words and phrases of every sentence you write.**
- **Floating adverbs**: "which is again merely another notation for X" becomes "which again is merely another notation for X," so the modifiers don't pile up; "the elasticities are both with respect to the price" becomes "both elasticities are with respect to the price." If no order works, "give up the sentence as a bad idea."
- **Auxiliary verbs** (*should*, *can*, *might*, *had*, *is*) "add a little water" to a clotted sentence: "Looking through a lens-shape magnified what you saw" becomes "Looking through a lens-shape would magnify what you saw."
- **Parenthetical remarks** that break the flow go next to the word they relate to, or at the end if they are important.
- **"The end is the place of emphasis."** The reader leaves with the last word ringing in her ears. The beginning is second in emphasis. "Dump less important things in the middle, or in the trash." Examine every sentence to see whether the main idea comes at the end. Corollary: trivial matter at the end weakens the sentence. Not "That putting trivial things at the end will weaken the sentence is a corollary of the rule," which ends on the rule, already finished, but "A corollary of the rule is that putting less important things at the end will weaken the sentence."
- **Straggling**: "adding one more idea at the last minute" makes even a grammatical sentence hard to read (the young man of Japan "whose limericks never would scan").
- **Increasing length**: line up a series by increasing length, aware that this can conflict with putting the important matter last. Doublets and triplets live by it: "Keynes and the Keynesians," not "The Keynesians and Keynes"; "Faith, hope, and charity," not "Charity, faith, and hope." Use them sparingly: "write with a rifle, not a shotgun."

## 24 Read, Out Loud

- Reading aloud lets you hear the writing as others hear it. It settles when to use contractions (*you'll*, *it's*) in semiformal prose and catches unintended rhymes ("at times your lines will chime").
- **The rule**: "don't write anything that you would be embarrassed to read out loud to the intended audience." Hemingway: "The writer needs a built-in, shockproof bullshit detector." Blush at an over-fancy sentence or a jargony word, and change it.
- **Train the ear**: read the best old books, read poetry aloud and memorize some, and keep reading good writing after school. The good journalists "got that way... by reading the real stuff, Shakespeare and Ring Lardner."
