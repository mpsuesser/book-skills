# The piece: rules 1–13

Rules for the whole paper, report, or essay: getting it written and getting its arrangement, audience, and tone right. They matter most at first composition. The governing ideas behind rules 1–5 are in `SKILL.md`; this file carries their working detail.

## 1–5: trade, thinking, rules, clarity, taste

- **Learnable like algebra**: "anyone can learn to delete a quarter of the words from a first draft."
- **Signs of the amateur**: "not only . . . but also" is amateurish; "process" is vacuous; "as we will see" (newspaper version: "more on that later") is "pointlessly anticipating." Even if obscurity paid, "Being bad is bad."
- **Good thinking**, and so good writing, "is accurate, symmetrical, relevant to the thoughts of the audience, concrete yet usefully abstract, concise yet usefully full of good ideas. Above all it is self-critical and honest."
- **The good rulebooks**: Strunk and White, *The Elements of Style* (fundamental: "Express parallel ideas in parallel form"); Graves and Hodge, *The Reader over Your Shoulder*; Joseph Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*. Further: Lanham's *Revising Prose* and *Revising Business Prose*, Booth, Colomb, and Williams's *The Craft of Research*, Lucas's *Style*, Barzun's *Simple and Direct*, Gowers's *The Complete Plain Words*, Becker's *Writing for Social Scientists*, Thomas and Turner's *Clear and Simple as the Truth*. **The test of a style book** is "whether you read the book with pleasure." Grammar checkers in word processors "are useless. Turn them off."
- **Ms. Jones's rules, with what replaces each**:
  - *Never repeat a word within three lines* breeds **elegant variation** ("consumer," then "household," then "agent"). Repeat the word (rule 20).
  - *Never write "I"* leads to a "we" fit for "kings, editors, and people with tapeworms." The sound core: when talking about "I" you're not talking about the subject. "Talk about the subject, class."
  - *Writing well is writing swell* leads to "a splendidly dignified bureaucratese."
  - *No split infinitives*: split freely ("to really understand").
  - *No preposition at the end*: "a service for which people were willing to pay" or "a service which people were willing to pay for." Usually moving the preposition out of final position is better, "though it's best to judge by sound, not by rule." Churchill: "This is the sort of impertinence up with which I will not put."
  - *Jonesian Arrangement*: it "drains the drama from your writing. It is b-o-ring." Churchill demanded every proposal on a half sheet of paper.
  - Ms. Jones was right about the **misplaced modifier**: "thousands—some following the advice of their accountants—who interrupted their holiday activities to line up" puts the accountants in line. Move the modifier: "I'm traveling a lot, to Las Vegas in a couple of weeks, for example, a strange place" becomes "I'm traveling a lot—in a couple of weeks, for example, to Las Vegas, a strange place."
- **The one-more-thing effect**: drafting inserts afterthoughts; revision straightens them out. "That it is New York, and you will help me enjoy it (as I always do), is good" becomes "It is good that it is New York, and you will help me enjoy it, as I always do."
- **Learn by imitation of a list of good writers**: "You can't define good style without a list of good writers." McCloskey's: Orwell, Mary McCarthy, Tom Wolfe, Annie Dillard, Virginia Woolf, and the *New Yorker*; in economics Keynes, Robert Solow, George Stigler, Thomas Schelling, Paul Krugman, and Adam Smith. "You know writers are 'good' by reading them with delight."

## 6 Classical Rhetoric Guides Even the Economical Writer

- **Choose a subject** "that fits the assignment yet also stirs something in your soul." Love it, hate it, or "gin up such emotions for the time being."
- **Rhetoric** is not a frill or a device for lying but "the whole art of argument": in Wayne Booth's words, the art "of discovering warrantable beliefs and improving those beliefs in shared discourse."
- Its three parts are **invention** (framing arguments worth listening to: theory and observation), **arrangement**, and **style**. This book teaches style.
- **Arrangement** is little examined. The official academic and bureaucratic arrangement runs introduction, outline of the paper, theory, model, results, suggestions for future research, and summary again, enforced by handbooks like the APA *Publication Manual*. It hides "which experiments failed, what mathematics proved fruitless, why exactly the questions were asked." Consider the alternatives: a dialogue, or a report on the actual sequence of discovery.

## 7 Fluency Can Be Achieved by Grit

- Style "begins with mere fluency, getting something down on paper. And it ends with revising again and again, until you've removed all the snares and blemishes."
- Against the urge to do the dishes or check the email: "Locate chair. Apply rear end to it. Locate writing implement. Use it."
- **Taste creates doubt.** Every writer meets "the dark night of the soul" ("Isn't it all pretty stupid?"). The only help is "a cheerful hope that more work will raise even the rubbish up to your newly acquired standards," then raising the standards and acquiring "a better doubt at a still higher level of taste." **Irrational cheerfulness** is good to have.

## 8 Write Early Rather Than Late

- "The teachable trick is getting a first draft. Don't wait until the research is done to begin writing." Write a page here, a section there, a crucial phrase. "Research is writing."
- **The File** (C. Wright Mills, "On Intellectual Craftsmanship"): the whole body of notes, kept like a journal, which "should become thick and rich, and should be occasionally dumped out and rearranged."
- **One idea per card or per paragraph**, "even if the idea is only a single line. It's a mistake to economize by cramming several ideas into a space." Title each note with its gist. Spill notes as paragraphs, print and cut them up, spread them on the floor, and seek the best arrangement.
- **Read through the file (invention) for an outline (arrangement).** Sort notes into related stacks, and add notes for transitions and new ideas. Arrangement is detective work (patterns in the data) and dramatic work (the audience's response). "Make it interesting."
- **The broad outline** is a goal that gives the writing "dramatic direction." Revise it repeatedly. Write today's small part, not necessarily the beginning: "You can always throw away the pompous and vacuous introduction you write at first." "The paper should end up as a story, because readers normally read from beginning to end."
- **The numbering trick**: number each paragraph (out of 999) in the order you think might work, leaving gaps: 100 for the opener, 050 for something better still, 900 and 950 for late items. Use the same number of digits throughout, sort, then write the sequence out, combining paragraphs, and delete the numbers.
- **When stuck, write a sub-outline** of the next few sentences or paragraphs and tick the points off. "Never stop for outlining or cleaning up... if you're *not* stuck."
- **Outline points are substantive, not formal**: not "Introduction" or "Concluding Paragraph" but "Historians pay too little attention to the sexual division of labor," or in telegraphic style "div. lab."
- Write an inspiration down at once. "Don't let the nonroutine moment pass... Pay the gas bill in a dull moment."

## 9 You Will Need Tools

- Keep at hand an outline covered with revision notes, scratch paper for trying out phrases, and The File. Enjoy your tools without becoming compulsive: be the professional "who can do any job on command to deadline anywhere with any equipment whatever."
- **Toggle** (Lanham): switch medium (dictation, longhand, screen) or change the typeface, and the draft shows itself in a new light.
- **A good dictionary**, one that gives word origins, marks Americanisms, gives pronunciation, and distinguishes levels of usage from plain to fancy (*Merriam-Webster*, *Collins*, *Webster's New World*, the *OED*). Read the definitions and origins, not just the spelling. Usage traps: *disinterested* is not *uninterested*, *infer* is not *imply*, and "the hoi polloi" doubles the article.
- **Spellcheck everything**, then give "that last, slow rereading," since *their*/*there*/*they're* and *weather*/*whether* slip through. A few proofing marks on the page are fine: "You're writing a report, not a copy of the Torah."
- **A real thesaurus** (the word processor's is useless) to find "the precise word within a more or less fuzzy region." Swift: "Proper words in proper places make the true definition of a style."
- Check quotations' exact words. Quotation websites collect what people *think* someone said.
- **A commonplace book**: a personal file of well-expressed ideas, named for the *loci communes* (*koinoi topoi*), the shared materials of invention.

## 10 Keep Your Spirits Up, Forge Ahead

- Nothing to say? "Read more, converse more, calculate more." Most research proves irrelevant to the final paper, another reason to write early.
- **So What?** is the guiding question: "Answer that question in every sentence, and you will become a great scholar, or a millionaire. Answer it once or twice in a ten-page paper or report, and you'll write a pretty good one."
- **Say it out loud.** "If people wrote more the way they spoke, their writing would have more vigor." "Don't write entirely silently, or you will write entirely stiffly." Good modern prose has "the rhythms of actual speech—intelligent and honest actual speech." "We exaggerate the power of words to conceal a shameful intent. Generally the words expose it."
- **"Regard the outline as an aid, not a master."** When stuck: look at the outline and revise it, reread what you've written, read the last bit out loud, talk to yourself about where it is going, explain it to a friend (imagined, then real), imitate a good speaker's way of talking, "write a sentence parallel to the one just written, fill out the idea." "Don't break off when on a burn."
- Easy writing is often undisciplined writing. McCloskey wrote well in college while trying only to sound clever; her style "sharply deteriorated" in graduate school once she resolved to tell the truth, and took decades to recover.
- **Start each session** by rereading a chunk "as though you were a first-time reader, noting where your reading pace is checked."
- **The hot tip**: at the end of every session or substantial break, "always write down your thoughts, however vague, about what will come next," at the end of the text, to be crossed off as used. Piaget: "It's better to stop in the middle of the sentence."

## 11 Speak to an Audience of Human Beings

- Rewriting takes devotion (Hemingway rewrote the last page of *Farewell to Arms* sixty times), but it lacks the anxiety of invention.
- **The implied reader**: "Ask who they are, aim the draft toward them, and keep hauling yourself back to facing them in revisions. Choose a reader and stick with her." Don't explain that oil is "a black, burnable fluid" and then write for an organic chemist. "If you've started with a preschooler as an implied reader, you have to keep her around."
- Choose an imagined reader or a real one (a respected colleague, "good old Professor Smith," the friend down the hall), someone with high standards "but with some sympathy for the enterprise." **Imagine one master spirit**: "How would Sutch see this?" "If it embarrasses you, when you are imagining how Sutch would read it, the stuff *is* embarrassing." The one reader keeps the prose at one level of difficulty.
- **Get the good ring**: revise right after reading admirable prose, and for half an hour you pick up its practices by ear.

## 12 Avoid Boilerplate

- "Your writing must be interesting." Choosing yourself as audience tends to dullness. "Restatements of the well-known bore readers. Routine mathematical passages bore readers. Get to the point that some skeptical but serious and somewhat sympathetic reader cares about, and stick to it."
- **Boilerplate** "is all that is prefabricated and predictable" (after the prefabricated steel plates of a steam boiler). Its forms:
  - **Excessive introduction, summarizing, and anticipation.**
  - **Redoing many repetitive cases** "what can be done just as well with a single well-chosen one."
  - **Needless conversions**, such as a metric figure after every British measure, which treat the reader as an ignoramus.
  - **Theoretical chatter copied out of a textbook.** "Apply it to the case at hand." When it is impenetrable for prestige it becomes **filigreed boilerplate**, "obscure and uncandid." Not "Consider two cities, A and B, trading an asset, X. If the prices of X are the same in market A and in market B, then arbitrage may be said to be complete," but "New York and London in 1870 both had markets for Union Pacific bonds. The question is, did the bonds sell for pretty much the same in both places?"
  - **"This paper . . ."**, "that all-purpose filler for the bankrupt imagination." Open instead the way Barzun and Graff advise for reviews: "the opening statement takes the reader from where he presumably stands in point of knowledge and brings him to the book under review." In journalism it is **the hook**. Not "This paper discusses the evidence against a large effect of monopoly on income," but "Every economist knows by now that monopoly greatly reduces income. Every economist appears to be mistaken."
  - **"Background"**, "a polite word for padding, the material you collected that you later discovered was beside the point." Resist. Professors and bosses "are experts in detecting lack of effort."
  - **The table-of-contents paragraph**: "The outline of this paper is as follows." "Don't, please, please, for God's sake, don't." Ninety-nine readers in a hundred skip it; the hundredth can't understand it until she has read the paper, when she no longer needs it. Defended as a "roadmap," but "you're a writer trying to keep your reader, not the American Automobile Association."
  - **Elaborate summaries** and **overtures**.
- **Never repeat without apologizing** for it ("as I said earlier," or merely "again"), or the reader suspects you haven't thought through the arrangement. If you find yourself apologizing a lot, you are repeating too much.
- **"Never, ever... anticipate: 'As we shall see.'"** It is useless and irritating.
- **No traffic directions.** Add headings afterward if you like, best as declarative sentences that advance the argument (like this book's chapter titles). "Your prose, however, should read well and clearly without the headings."

## 13 Control Your Tone

- **The implied author** is the character you take on while writing, the **persona** ("dramatic mask"): the Enthusiastic Student, the Earnest Scientist, the Reasonable and Modest Journeyman, the Genius, the Math Jock, the Professor, the Breezy Journalist. "Look at a piece of writing and ask what implied author it has in mind. The piece that works will have an author the reader can tolerate." You can't abstain from choosing; writing is acting. It persuades best when the implied author resembles you.
- Candor has limits (the dean's memo, the maiden aunt's new hat, the advertising of the VP for sales). "The ethical problem sits right in the middle of the road."
- **Failing personas**: the student's government-form author ("due to," "period of time," "views were opposing"; "No one really talks that way to their mother"); **The Newspaper Reporter**, snappy and built for the one-paragraph jolt but hard to tolerate elsewhere; and the professor's **The Scientist**, pompous and unintelligible out of stage fright.
- **Mills's diagnosis**: unintelligibility comes from "confusions of the academic writer about his own status." "To overcome the academic prose you have first to overcome the academic pose." Ask his three questions: "(1) How difficult and complex after all is my subject? (2) When I write, what status am I claiming for myself? (3) For whom am I trying to write?" "What spoils academic writing is lack of confidence." Ask of any prose, "What is this writer afraid of?" Usually it is of not seeming smart enough. "Write what you have to say, openhandedly."
- **"You must decide to be understood and worry some other time about being admired."** Explain in a reasonable tone to people who don't now understand, not to those who already do. "If you can't explain it to your boyfriend or your uncle, you probably don't understand it yourself." "Be nice, but not servile or pompous."
- **"The worst mistake is to be unpleasant."** Invective ("This is pure nonsense," "there is absolutely no evidence for this view," "the hypothesis is fanciful") arouses the suspicion that the argument needed passion "to overcome its weakness." Argue as McCloskey learned to: less relentlessly, "more diffident, as arguments should be if you are interested in the actual truth and want to establish it together with your reader," making common cause with the reader and "truthfully gentle with the 'opponents.'"
- **Adverbs carry tone.** Delete each *very*: "Most things aren't very." *Absolutely* and *purely* give "a falsely emphatic tone." Say an emotion once: in "endless attempts to extract higher wages by piling constraints on the wage bargain," drop "endless." *Literally* means "actually, in truth," not "Wow, I really feel this strongly!!"
- **Keep your opinions to yourself** unless they are relevant. Strunk and White: "to air one's views gratuitously . . . is to imply that the demand for them is brisk."
- **Wit compensates for tendentiousness** (Mencken; Housman's "The one merit that Professor X's Latin criticism has is that there is so little of it"). Most academic, business, and bureaucratic prose could use more humor: self-deprecating jokes about the sample size, dry wit about opponents. "A writer must entertain if she is to be read." Only "third-rate scholars, ineffective executives, and C− students" insist on their dignity.
