# The word: rules 25–31

"The snappiest rules of good writing are about words." Use these rules in revision, word by word.

## 25 Use Verbs, Active Ones

- "Write with nouns and, especially, verbs, not with adjectives and adverbs. In revision the adjectives and adverbs should be the first to go."
- Sydney Smith: "In composing, as a general rule, run your pen through every other word you have written; you have no idea what vigour it will give to your style." Applied to itself, it becomes "Run your pen through every other word; you have no idea what vigour it will give."
- **Active voice**: not "Active verbs should be used," which is "cowardice, hiding the user in the passive voice," but "You should use active verbs." **The imperative** substitutes well for the passive in a complex argument: "Then divide both sides by x," not "Both sides are then divided by x."
- **"Verbs make English."** "Find the action in a sentence and express it in a verb," "as vivid as truth will bear."
- **Nominalization** expresses the action as a noun. It "afflicts most academic prose":
  - "There is a data reanalysis need" (Joseph Williams's example) becomes "We must reanalyze our data."
  - "On the left, there is widespread disdain for profit-driven healthcare" becomes "The left disdains profit-driven healthcare."
  - "Self-interested exchange is a knowledge-generating process" becomes "Self-interested exchange generates knowledge."
- **Circle every *is*.** "If the page looks like a bad case of acne, try to replace each *is* with a real action verb." "There is" and "It is" often cause nominalization.
- "A real verb requires a real subject. You can't hide." The "data reanalysis need" merely exists, "blessedly free from personal responsibility," which is why bureaucrats love nominalization.

## 26 Avoid Words That Bad Writers Love

Some words "will tag you as incompetent simply because good writers have decided so." *Hopefully* for "I hope" gets you "set down as a fool." Drop *via*, *the process of*, *intra*, *and/or*, *hypothesize*, *respectively*, and "(a strange one, this) *this*," and the gain in clarity is big. Drop *at least minimal*, *process of*, *thus*, *overall*, *basic*, and *factor*, and "the world would be saved." Woolf would write neither *and/or* nor *he/she*: "she wanted prose, not a diagram."

### Bad Words

**Vague nouns**

- **concept**: vague, Latinate, "front-parlor." Consider *idea*, *notion*, *thought*.
- **data**: over- and misused. It is plural and means "givens"; it is not a do-all synonym for *facts*, *statistics*, *information*, *observations*.
- **function**, in the sense of "role": Latinate.
- **situation**: vague. *Position* or *condition*, depending on the meaning.
- **individuals**, **agents**: for plain "people."
- **structure**: vague; "the word usually doesn't mean anything at all."
- **process**: "usually empty, and can be struck out (sometimes with its *the*)": "the transition process" becomes "the transition." It is right in the rare case where an actual sequence of steps is meant (the president "launches a *process*" of consultation before any nuclear launch).
- **the existence of X**: strike it out and just name the X.
- **time frame**: means "time."
- **the turn of the nineteenth century**: is it 1800 or 1900? "Don't puzzle the reader."

**Pretentious and feeble verbs**

- **critique**: elegant variation for "criticize," "read critically," "comment on."
- **implement**: Washingtonese, "a rich and foolish dialect."
- **comprise**: fancy talk for "include" or "consist of."
- **analyze**: misused for *discuss* or *examine*. In Greek it meant "cut to pieces"; keep that in mind and the sentence gets vivid.
- **hypothesize**: for "suppose" or "expect." It "tags you as a barbarian," as do *finalize*, *and/or*, *time frame*.
- **finalize**: boardroom talk.
- **state**, in the mere sense of "say": *state* means "assert with conviction." Choose the real action: *say*, *assert*, *argue*, *reply*, *believe*. "'The book stated' is a childish way of saying 'Coffin et al. claim.'"
- **try and do something**: say "try to do something." In the United States it marks incompetence.
- **the reason was due to**: try again.

**Pointless adjectives**

- **former . . . latter**, **the above**, **the preceding**: they make the reader look back, and she will lose her place. "Never ask the reader to solve a puzzle."
- **aforementioned**: "what are you writing, a will?"
- **intra/inter**: use *within* and *between*. *International* and *intramural* are domesticated, but "the inter- and intra-firm communication was weak" is "silly fancy talk."
- **interesting**: weak from sarcastic use and from writers with nothing else to say about the subject. "It arouses the reader's sadism."
- **kind of / sort of / type of**: "vague, vague, vague." Sparingly.

**Useless adverbs**

- **fortunately**, **interestingly**, etc.: "cheap ways of introducing irrelevant opinion."
- **respectively**: "Consumption and investment were 90% and 10% of income, respectively" draws attention to a parallelism the writer might have broken. Distribute the numbers: "Consumption was 85% of income, investment 10%, and government spending 5%."
- **very**: "Most things aren't very."
- **for convenience**: "All writing should be for convenience. What would be the point of writing for inconvenience?"
- In general, look for words ending in *-ly*. They usually carry opinion, "which only your mother actually cares about."

**Clumsy conjunctions**

- **due to**: used by bad writers to avoid *by* or *because*. Likewise "due to the fact that" and "because of the fact that."
- **in terms of**: like *due to*, *thus*, and *hence*. "Use traffic signals sparingly."
- **plus** for *and*: use *and*.

Her teaching handouts add *the fact that*, *like in* (write *as in*), *as well as* (usually), *in my opinion*, *I believe*, and *I think*. Put *I* in only when your opinion is the point: "you are being asked for *facts* and *arguments*, put into a good *style*, not opinions."

### Ersatz Economics

"The choice of words determines how you think." Every field has a lay vocabulary that carries a lay theory, and using it means thinking the lay theory. McCloskey's example is **Ersatz Economics**, the person in the street's: prices "skyrocketing"; "sellers outnumber buyers"; "exorbitant," "gouging," "fair," and "just" prices; "vicious cycle"; "cutthroat" competition and "dumping"; "bargaining power" used to "exploit"; consumers who can "barely afford" what they "need," such as a "basic necessity"; "obscene" profit margins kept by "passing along" costs in a "spiral"; the "living wage" protected from "cheap foreign labor"; "rebuilding" a "collapsing" industrial "base"; things "going up." "No such locutions should pass the lips of a professional economist. Likewise in other fields." Clear your mind of such cant, "just as to understand astronomy you must stop talking about the sun 'rising.'"

## 27 Be Concrete

- **A singular is more concrete than a plural.** It "calls up an image of the singular thing, standing bravely forward. Plurals call up a more or less baggy collection."
- "Prefer 'Au Bon Pain baguette' to 'bread,' 'bread' to 'widgets,' and 'widgets' to 'X.' That is, flee the abstract." The reader will understand that the baguette stands for any commodity.
- **The code principle**: "A reader finds it harder to translate abstractions down into concrete examples than to translate examples up into abstract principles." Abstract prose reads like a secret code.
- Examples:
  - "Had capital and labor in 1860 embodied the same technology used in 1780, the increase in capital would barely have offset the fixity of land" becomes "Had the machines and men of 1860 embodied the same knowledge of how to spin cotton or move cargo as in 1780, the larger numbers of spindles and ships would have barely offset the fixity of land."
  - "Natural resource–oriented exports" from Australia are "sheep and wheat."
  - "The commencement of the Spanish Price Revolution antedated the inflow of treasure" becomes "Spanish prices began to rise before the treasure came."

## 28 Be Plain

- **Five-dollar words** support the pose of The Scientist or The Scholar. "Science and scholarship depend on the quality of argument, not on the level of diction."
- **Prefer the Anglo-Saxon word.** The old Anglo-Saxon words (*need*, *someone*, *feels*, *buys*, *bread*, *bake*) "have a concreteness through homely use" that the later Latin and Greek coinages (*integrative*, *consequences*, *structural*, *differentiation*) lack. "The integrative consequences of growing structural differentiation" means "the need for others that someone feels when he buys rather than bakes his bread." "Geographical and cultural factors function to spatially confine growth to specific regions for long periods of time" means "It's a good bet that once a place gets poor it will stay poor."
- A master can use five-dollar words for irony (Veblen: sports "afford a politely blameless outlet for energies that might otherwise not readily be diverted from some useful end"). "But you've got to be Veblen to get away with such stuff."
- **"A lot of jargon hides a five-cent thought in a five-dollar word."** "Current period responses" means "what people do now"; "complex lagged effects," "the many things they do later"; "interim variation," "change"; "monitored back," "told." The "time inconsistency problem" is changing one's mind; the "principal/agent problem" is "what hirelings do or fail to do." "It's the hiding and the fancy talk that's wrong" (as in "enhanced interrogation technique" for torture).
- **The teutonism** (*der Grossjargongeneratingfunktion*) is a pile of nouns and adjectives that "does not suit the genius of modern English." **Untie the knot**, using *of*, then strike what is understood or empty. "Private wealth-seeking activity" becomes "the activity of seeking wealth privately," then, with "private" understood and "activity of" pointless, "the seeking of wealth." "The long-run balance of payments adjustment" becomes "the adjustment of the balance of payments in the long run": inelegant, but no less elegant than the original, and clearer. Knots to untie: "elastic credit supply expectations rise"; "antiquantity theory evidence"; "initial relative capital goods price shock"; "community decision making process"; "Cobb-Douglas production function estimation approach." "When you find yourself piling up nouns and adjectives, trim."
- **The possessive** generates teutonisms. Use it with proper nouns ("Samuelson's genius"). "The standard political scientist's model": what's standard, the model or the political scientist?
- "Reexamine any phrase with more than one adjective." "Watch especially for nouns used as adjectives." Turning verbs into nouns and nouns into adjectives is the genius of English. What is objectionable is "piling up these nounverbadjectives teutonically."

## 29 Avoid Cheap Typographical Tricks

- Scare quotes, italics for emphasis, exclamation points, and coy abbreviations add an "air" of "Breathlessness or Solemnity or Coyness." They are sound effects, and the reader "doubts everything that is said." Better is **LISFI**, "letting it speak for itself."
- **Italics for emphasis** mean "the sentence is not set up to give emphasis naturally. Fix it" (usually by moving the emphatic word to the end, rule 23).
- **Quotation marks around words you aren't quoting** apologize for the "wrong" word or sneer at it. "It's impolite to cringe or to sneer."
- **Acronyms** ("Modigliani and Miller (henceforth M&M)," "purchasing power parity (PPP)") "pimple the page and add a burden of excess notation." An occasional GDP is fine. Write out "gross domestic capital formation" once or twice "to fix ideas," then "capital formation" or "it." "The point is to be clear, not to 'save space'": acronyms save half a dozen lines at most, less than the roadmap paragraph. The Dilbert manager's rationale: "The goal is to make ourselves look smart while making the readers feel dumb."
- **Justification**: for an academic audience use a ragged right margin; in business and government writing, full-justify, because that is their practice. "Pick up the fork that your tribe requires, short of actual barbarities."

## 30 Avoid This, That, These, Those

- **This-ism**: "These bad writers think this reader needs those repeated reminders that it is this idea, not that other one, which is being discussed." *This* points the reader back "for no good reason," and "looking back is looking away, interrupting the forward flow."
- "Circle every *this* and *these* and *those* in your draft. You will be shocked by their number." Good writers slip into it too: Wayne Booth's "improving those beliefs" wants plain "the beliefs."
- **The formula in revision**: ask of every *this*, *these*, *those* whether it would be better as plain *the* (the most common answer), *it*, or *such* (*a*). Or repeat the word it stands for: "Graceful repetition... brings clarity and unity to English prose."
- Exercise passage (a museum magazine on ancient Chinese bronzes): "Exquisitely ornamented, these vessels were made to carry sacrificial offerings... these spiritually significant objects were seen as manifestations of a heavenly mandate... This exhibition—the first to explore how these exquisite objects were collected...—presents a rare opportunity to experience a large number of these works." It has this-ism, plus elegant variation in "vessels," "objects," "works."

## 31 Above All, Look at Your Words

- **Idiom and prepositions.** There are no simple guides; good reading trains the instinct. Check prepositions in the third and fourth revisions: "the discussion *about* me doing" beats "the discussion *of* me doing." Experiment: "*by* an increase *of* supply" or "*because of* an increase *in* supply"? Pare prepositions that aren't essential. Too many verb-plus-preposition pairs ("write down") can sometimes give way to one verb holding the idea ("inscribe"), usually Latinate and higher in register.
- **Word lore**: learn a little Latin and Greek and some history of the language. Ayers's *English Words from Latin and Greek Elements* is one guide.
- **Word pairs**: you overcome, not cure, ignorance. But pairs drift into cliché. **"Flee the cliché when a more original word is precise and vivid."** For "the pursuit of profit," try "seeking," "finding," "receiving," "uncovering," or "bumping into" profit. Hayek's thinking about the redundancy of "given data" ("to whom were the data really given") led him to the problem of information dispersed among thousands of people. "New words suggest new thoughts. Word-thought is a tool of thinking." A cliché is a set phrase "you are accustomed to hearing": it gives "an impression of not thinking" ("right place at the right time," "jack up the prices").
- **Dead metaphors** "sit up in their coffins to incongruous effect." Ask what the word literally means, and **are the words literally possible?** "The indicators influenced the controls": how does an indicator influence a control? "The severity of the models": punishments, not models, are severe. The writer meant the models' assumptions are hard to believe, so say *unbelievability*.
- **Word-lore rulings**:
  - *However* "works best in a secondary position, and therefore you should never start a sentence with it." Starting with *But* or *And* is fine.
  - "In this period" is usually redundant.
  - Numbered lists are clumsy.
  - "Regarding X" or "in regard to X" is "definite evidence of a bad education in the language."
- Don't keep reminding the reader that you are talking about, say, the textbook or the nineteenth century.
