# Economical Writing

Deirdre McCloskey's thirty-five rules for clear and persuasive prose, written for anyone in a middle-class occupation who must write (economics, business, government, the social sciences, history, and literature), and Stephen Ziliak's **McZ# method**, which uses the rule numbers to find and fix the faults in a draft. The numbers are load-bearing: a fault is named by its rule, "McZ 20" for elegant variation, "McZ 30" for a needless *this*.

## The governing ideas

- **Writing is a trade.** It is a correctable skill, not a character trait. The **amateur attitude** doesn't know the rules or the reasoning behind them, never rewrites, won't read yesterday's page "with a cold eye," and admires everything it has written. The professional takes advantage of criticism, above all self-criticism. "Good writing pays well and bad writing pays badly... You have to be read to be listened to."
- **Writing is thinking.** "You can't split content from style. They constitute yolk and white in a scrambled egg." The **conduit metaphor** (ideas "conveyed" unchanged, as through the pneumatic tube at the drive-in bank) holds for sheer information, like a phone number, not for knowledge, which rests on judgments. You find the gaps and idiocies in an argument by writing it out and looking back critically.
- **Be Clear** is "the one genuine rule, a golden one." Quintilian: write "not merely so that the reader can understand but so that he cannot possibly misunderstand." **Clarity is a social matter**: "The reader, like the voter in a democratic country, runs the show. If the reader thinks what you write is unclear, then it is. Quit arguing." **"Clarity is a matter of speed directed at The Point."** "In most writing the reader is in trouble more than half the time": bad writing stops her with a puzzle in every other sentence. "Lack of clarity is selfish."
- **The rules are factual rather than logical.** "In matters of taste in writing the only standard is the practice of good writers and the reaction of good readers." A violation "sends a signal of incompetence," and "the violations are correlated with each other": the writer who starts sentences with "However," or ignores parallelism, probably over-summarizes too.
- **Many school rules are wrong** (**Ms. Jones's** rules from the seventh grade). Repeat words: "never repeat the same word or phrase within three lines" breeds elegant variation. Write *I* rather than a royal *we*, and better still, **"Talk about the subject."** Split infinitives freely. End a sentence with a preposition when it sounds better ("judge by sound, not by rule"). Start sentences with *And* or *But*. Skip the comma after an introductory phrase. Above all reject **Jonesian Arrangement**, "Say what you're going to say; say it; say that you've said it," which turns writing into "summary, outline, anticipation, announcement, redundancy, and review." Ms. Jones was right about one thing: misplaced modifiers. "Put the word or clause relevant to X close to X."
- **Style is rewriting.** Rules about the whole piece and the paragraph serve first composition; rules about sentences and words serve revision; brevity serves both, "the midnight fever of composition and the morning chill of revision." "Easy writing makes hard reading" (Hemingway). Then seek joy: learn the rules "in order to be joyously creative," and writing becomes **flow**, a challenge "just about manageable."

## The thirty-five rules

The chapter titles are the rules. Detail, examples, and word lists sit in the files named in each group's heading.

**The piece** ([the-piece.md](the-piece.md)):

1. **Writing Is a Trade.** Good writing pays; being clear is an ethical matter as well as a prudent one.
2. **Writing Is Thinking.** Write to find out what you think; content and style are one.
3. **Rules Can Help, but Bad Rules Hurt.** Learn the good rulebooks' rules; drop Ms. Jones's bad ones.
4. **Be Thou Clear, but Seek Joy, Too.** The reader runs the show; read your draft cold.
5. **The Rules Are Factual Rather Than Logical.** Good style is what good writers do.
6. **Classical Rhetoric Guides Even the Economical Writer.** Invention, arrangement, style; pick a subject that stirs your soul.
7. **Fluency Can Be Achieved by Grit.** "Locate chair. Apply rear end to it."
8. **Write Early Rather Than Late.** Draft during the research; keep The File; outline in substantive points.
9. **You Will Need Tools.** A good dictionary, spellchecker, and thesaurus; a final slow rereading.
10. **Keep Your Spirits Up, Forge Ahead.** Ask So What?; say it out loud; end each session with notes on what comes next.
11. **Speak to an Audience of Human Beings.** Choose one implied reader and stick with her.
12. **Avoid Boilerplate.** Cut "This paper...," background, the table-of-contents paragraph, anticipation, and summary.
13. **Control Your Tone.** Choose a tolerable implied author; no invective, no *very*, no gratuitous opinions.

**The paragraph and the sentence** ([paragraphs-and-sentences.md](paragraphs-and-sentences.md)):

14. **A Paragraph Should Have a Point.** Tell the reader something new in every paragraph; end it a notch lower.
15. **Make Tables, Graphs, Displayed Equations, and Labels on Images Readable by Themselves.**
16. **Footnotes and Other "Scholarly" Tics Are Pedantic.** Footnotes point to sources. That is all.
17. **Make Your Writing Cohere.** Link sentences by repeating words, not by signposts like "not only . . . but also."
18. **Use Your Ear.** Vary sentences; elaborate only one of subject, verb, object (Gardner's Rule).
19. **Write in Complete Sentences.**
20. **Avoid Elegant Variation.** One word for one thing; express parallel ideas in parallel form.
21. **Watch How Each Word Connects with Others.** Check and tighten: redundancy, gapping, modifiers, reference.
22. **Watch Punctuation.** Serial comma, no comma splice, colon versus semicolon, commas by ear.
23. **The Order Around Switch Until It Good Sounds.** The end is the place of emphasis.
24. **Read, Out Loud.** Never write what you would be embarrassed to say to the intended audience.

**The word** ([words.md](words.md)):

25. **Use Verbs, Active Ones.** Find the action and express it in a verb; cure nominalization; circle every *is*.
26. **Avoid Words That Bad Writers Love.** The Bad Words list; Ersatz Economics.
27. **Be Concrete.** Singulars over plurals, examples over abstractions; "flee the abstract."
28. **Be Plain.** No five-dollar words; untie teutonisms.
29. **Avoid Cheap Typographical Tricks.** No scare quotes, italics for emphasis, exclamation points, or acronyms; "letting it speak for itself."
30. **Avoid This, That, These, Those.** Replace this-ism with *the*, *it*, *such*, or the repeated word.
31. **Above All, Look at Your Words.** Idiom, prepositions, word pairs, clichés, dead metaphors, word lore.

**Particular occasions** ([letters-talks-nonnative.md](letters-talks-nonnative.md)):

32. **Use Standard Forms in Letters.** Salutations, closings, capitals, rereading before sending.
33. **Treat Speaking in Public as a Performance.** Never give the outline; talk about the subject; PowerPoint is evil.
34. **Advice for Nonnative English Speakers.**

**And the summing up:**

35. **If You Didn't Stop Reading, Join the Flow.** Become "your own harshest editor and grader," asking "Am I making sense? Am I speaking truth?" It carries Twain's rules and Orwell's rules, cited as "McZ 35, Twain #1" or "McZ 35, Orwell #3":

| | Twain: the writer must | Orwell |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | say what he is proposing to say, not merely come near it | Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print. |
| 2 | use the right word, not its second cousin | Never use a long word where a short one will do. |
| 3 | eschew surplusage | If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out. |
| 4 | not omit necessary details | Never use the passive where you can use the active. |
| 5 | avoid slovenliness of form | Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent. |
| 6 | use good grammar | Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous. |
| 7 | employ a simple and straightforward style | |

"McZ 35, Orwell #1 and Twain #1–2 are especially important for the ethical economist."

## Drafting a piece

When the user is starting or stuck, work from rules 6 to 13 in [the-piece.md](the-piece.md). The core moves: write early, during the research, because writing finds the gaps. Gather notes into **The File**, one idea per note. Read the file (invention) for an outline (arrangement) whose points are substantive claims, not "Introduction." Write whatever part is ready today, not necessarily the beginning. When stuck, write a **sub-outline** for the next few paragraphs. Fix one **implied reader** and one **implied author** before revising. Ask **So What?** of every sentence.

## Revising and editing

Use this order on the user's draft or your own. Each pass is done when every sentence in the text has been read under it.

1. **Read cold, at the reader's speed.** Read as a first-time reader, a day or more after drafting if you wrote it. The paper that took a week to write will be read in an hour, so "reread in the rapid way to get the experience the reader will have." Mark every spot where "reading pace is checked by some difficulty of expression": that is **the Graves and Hodge trick**. Knock each such place into shape.
2. **The piece.** Strip **boilerplate** (rule 12). Hold the implied reader at one level of difficulty and the implied author steady (rules 11, 13). Make sure the arrangement tells a story, without "traffic directions." The prose should read well with the headings removed.
3. **The paragraph.** Each one tells the reader something she doesn't know or doesn't yet believe. Its last sentence comes down a notch into "street-talk." Its sentences link by repeated words (rules 14, 17).
4. **The sentence.** Ask **the check-and-tighten questions**: "Do nouns and verbs link successive sentences? Have I used one word to mean one thing? Is the verb in the sentence singular if the subject is? Have I used parallel forms to emphasize parallel ideas? Can I drop any word?" Then put the main idea at the end, elaborate only one of subject, verb, and object, test every sentence for redundancy, and fix the punctuation (rules 18–23).
5. **The word.** Circle every *is* and every *this*, *these*, and *those*. Strike the Bad Words, the *very*s, and most adjectives and adverbs. Untie teutonisms. Turn nominalizations into verbs and abstractions into concrete singulars (rules 25–31).
6. **Read out loud** (rule 24) for rhythm, rhymes, contractions, and anything you would blush to say to the audience.
7. **Last slow rereading** for typos the spellchecker passes (*their*/*there*/*they're*, *weather*/*whether*).

Rewrite to the rules; McCloskey's own before-and-after pairs in the reference files show the size of change she expects. When the result is not good enough, run another cycle: "No, your paper is not finished—not that kind of finished."

## The McZ# method

Ziliak's procedure for becoming "your own best editor," for a writer alone, a student, an editor, a peer, or "any other second pair of eyes." Use it whenever the user wants a draft marked up, critiqued, or graded.

1. Keep the rules in mind by the **action** each title names (the index above).
2. Start short: "Before you write ten or twelve pages, paragraph one needs to pass the McZ# test."
3. Read "word by word and line by line." Circle each word, passage, or punctuation mark that violates a rule, and note the number beside it: "McZ 13" where tone slips, "McZ 30" at every *this*, *that*, *these*, *those* "or similar vague placeholder," "McZ 35, Orwell #1" at a stock metaphor.
4. **Find at least four McZ#s per page**, so a five-page paper has around twenty. "If all you manage to circle is *this* or *that*, marking 'McZ 30,' you are cheating the system: try again."
5. **Give the fix, cross-referenced**: for McZ 30, see McZ 27 (concrete words: "*bread* and *beer* and *democracy*, not *consumables* and *liquids* and *governmental structures*"). For a paragraph without a point (McZ 14), see McZ 35, Twain #1 and Orwell #1. A vague comment like "This is a rough transition" or "Punctuation is wrong" is "almost useless."
6. Revise, making every change marked. Reread the new version "with a cold eye," keeping the McZ#s in mind. Repeat the cycle until a fresh pass turns up nothing.

Two tools for the "I feel stuck" sentence, Ziliak's:

- **The image-to-sound ratio.** Count the concrete images in a sentence (Charlie Brown, clouds, Ireland) and divide by the number of sounds (syllables) uttered. Far below 40 percent, "you're probably speaking in bureaucratic or other boilerplate terms, like Charlie Brown's teacher" (McZ 12, 26, 35, Orwell #1). Near 100 percent is poetry. To raise it, "replace deadweight loss with concrete imagery": *however*, *nonetheless*, and *interesting* go first.
- **Haiku economics.** "Economical writing is the art of allocating scarce rhetorical means to a number of noneconomic ends." Recast the point as a haiku, seventeen sounds on three lines of 5-7-5, "a three-act haiku play" with an introduction, a middle, and a conclusion. It wins clarity and coherence that hundreds of sounds of boilerplate don't. Ziliak's model is Bashō: "The young farm-child / interrupts rice husking to / gaze up at the moon." He sets it against three sentences by an economist that carry at least eleven McZ# violations.
