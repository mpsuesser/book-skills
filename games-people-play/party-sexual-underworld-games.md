# Party Games, Sexual Games and Underworld Games

White is the one who is "it"; Black is the other party. In the Advantages, IP is internal psychological, EP external psychological, IS internal social, ES external social, Bio biological and Ex existential.

# Party Games

"Parties are for pastimes, and pastimes are for parties", including the period before a group meeting officially begins. As acquaintance ripens, games emerge: "The Schlemiel and his victim recognize each other, as do Big Daddy and Little Old Me."

## Ain't It Awful (AIA)

Played in four forms: three pastimes, with "no denouement or payoff, but much unworthy feeling", and a game.

1. **"Nowadays"**, the self-righteous, punitive Parental pastime: "it's no wonder you can't trust anyone nowadays. I was looking through the desk of one of my roomers. . . ." It explains delinquency, divorce, crime and prices by what people do nowadays. Its slogan, "It's no wonder", separates it from idle gossip: "Nowadays" has direction and closure and offers an "explanation", while gossip rambles and trails off. One such player left a group when her opening move met silence instead of excited corroboration.
2. **"Broken Skin"**, the more benevolent Adult variation, slogan "What a pity!" It is an informal clinical colloquium on the flow of blood: blows to the face, abdominal operations, difficult childbirths, the more horrifying the better, with rivalry and surgical sophistication. A consistently hopeful outlook may bring a secret meeting of the Credentials Committee.
3. **"Water Cooler"** or **"Coffee Break"**, the Child pastime, slogan "Look what they're doing to us now." An organizational variant, played after dark as "Bar Stool". It is three-handed, the ace held by the shadowy "They".
4. **The game**, at its most dramatic in polysurgery addicts, doctor-shoppers who seek surgery against sound medical opposition. IP: having the body mutilated. EP: avoiding all intimacies and responsibilities except complete surrender to the surgeon. Bio: nursing care. IS: the staff and other patients. ES: sympathy and awe after discharge. Fraudulent liability and malpractice claimants play it professionally and demand indemnification. AIA "becomes a game, then, when the player overtly expresses distress, but is covertly gratified at the prospect of the satisfactions he can wring from his misfortune."

People who suffer misfortunes fall into **three classes**:

1. The suffering is inadvertent and unwanted. Some exploitation of the sympathy offered is natural "and may be treated with common courtesy".
2. The suffering is inadvertent but gratefully received for its opportunities. The game is an afterthought, a "secondary gain" in Freud's sense.
3. The suffering is sought, as by polysurgery addicts. The game is the primary consideration.

## Blemish

"The source of a large percentage of petty dissension in everyday life". It is played from the depressive Child position "I am no good", protectively transformed into the Parental position "They are no good", which the player must then prove. Hence Blemish players are uncomfortable with a new person until they have found his blemish. In its hardest form it becomes a totalitarian political game of "authoritarian" personalities, close kin to "Nowadays". "How'm I Doing?" gives positive reassurance; "Blemish" gives negative reassurance.

- **Premises** range from the trivial ("Last year's hat") and the cynical ("Hasn't got $7,000 in the bank") through the sinister ("Not 100% Aryan"), the esoteric ("Hasn't read Rilke") and the intimate ("Can't hold his erection") to the sophisticated ("What's he trying to prove?").
- **Analysis.** Dynamics: usually sexual insecurity. Aim: reassurance. Transactionally: prying, morbid curiosity or watchfulness, "sometimes with Parental or Adult concern charitably masking the Child's relish." IP: wards off depression. EP: avoids the intimacy that might expose White's own blemishes, and justifies turning away the unfashionable, the unbacked or the insecure. IS: the prying, with biological gain. ES: "Ain't It Awful", Neighborly Type.
- The choice of premise is independent of intellect. A competent foreign-service officer told an audience another country was inferior partly because its men's jacket sleeves were too long; only in the Parental "Blemish" did he mention such things.

## Schlemiel

"Schlemiel" is the Yiddish word (allied to German and Dutch words for cunning), not Chamisso's shadowless hero; the victim is the **Schlemazl**.

- **Moves.**
  - 1W: White spills a highball on the hostess's evening gown.
  - 1B: Black, the host, feels rage, but senses that if he shows it White wins. He pulls himself together, which gives him the illusion that he wins.
  - 2W: "I'm sorry."
  - 2B: Black mutters or cries forgiveness, strengthening the illusion.
  - 3W: More damage follows: the cigarette burn in the tablecloth, the chair leg through the lace curtain, the gravy on the rug. White's Child is exhilarated at being forgiven every time, and Black has made "a gratifying display of suffering self-control"; both profit.
- White wins either way: Black's anger justifies White's resentment, and his restraint lets White go on. The real payoff is not the pleasure of destructiveness, "merely an added bonus", but obtaining forgiveness.
- **Antithesis.** Withhold the demanded absolution. After "I'm sorry", instead of "It's okay": "Tonight you can embarrass my wife, ruin the furniture and wreck the rug, but please don't say 'I'm sorry.'" Black switches from forgiving Parent to objective Adult who takes full responsibility for having invited White. White's reaction, perhaps explosive, reveals the game's intensity, and the anti-Schlemiel player risks immediate reprisal or an enemy. Children play an abortive form for the pleasure of mess; social polish teaches them to collect the forgiveness.
- **Protocol.** Thesis: I can be destructive and still get forgiveness. Aim: absolution. Roles: *Aggressor*, Victim (Schlemiel and Schlemazl). Dynamics: anal aggression. Examples: (1) messily destructive children; (2) clumsy guest. Social (Adult-Adult): "Since I'm polite, you have to be polite, too." / "That's fine. I forgive you." Psychological (Child-Parent): "You have to forgive things which appear accidental." / "You are right. I have to show you what good manners are." Moves: (1) Provocation-resentment. (2) Apology-forgiveness.
- **Advantages.** IP: pleasure of messing. EP: avoids punishment. IS and ES: "Schlemiel". Bio: provocative and gentle stroking. Ex: I am blameless.

## Why Don't You—Yes But (YDYB)

"The original stimulus for the concept of games", the first dissected and one of the best understood. It is the game most commonly played at parties and in groups of every kind, psychotherapy groups included.

> White: "My husband always insists on doing our own repairs, and he never builds anything right." Black: "Why doesn't he take a course in carpentry?" White: "Yes, but he doesn't have time." Blue: "Why don't you buy him some good tools?" White: "Yes, but he doesn't know how to use them." Red: "Why don't you have your building done by a carpenter?" White: "Yes, but that would cost too much." Brown: "Why don't you just accept what he does the way he does it?" White: "Yes, but the whole thing might fall down."

Silence follows, until Green breaks it ("That's men for you, always trying to show how efficient they are") and starts "PTA", Delinquent Husband Type. A good player stands off a dozen or more solutions until the others give up.

- **Mechanics.** It is not played for its ostensible Adult purpose of finding solutions, but "to reassure and gratify the Child". White presents herself as a Child inadequate to the situation, and the others become "sage Parents anxious to dispense their wisdom". Both levels are complementary: Adult-Adult socially; psychologically "Why don't you. . . ." runs Parent to Child and "Yes, but. . . ." Child to Parent. The shifts can be seen in posture, muscular tone, voice and vocabulary.
- **Diagnostic question:** "Did anyone suggest anything you hadn't thought of yourself?" Answer: "No, they didn't. As a matter of fact, I've actually tried almost everything they suggested." White is usually as intelligent as anyone present. Playing fair, she gratefully accepts a genuinely original idea ("her 'inadequate' Child will give way if anyone present has an idea ingenious enough to stimulate her Adult"); habitual players seldom play fair. She rejects even what she has tried: "The purpose of the game is not to get suggestions, but to reject them." Too ready an acceptance of suggestions suggests an underlying game of "Stupid".
- **Payoff.** Each rejection brings a little pleasure, but the payoff is the silence or masked silence when the others are exhausted, proving "it is they who are inadequate." White resents anyone who cuts the silence short.
- **Dynamics.** Favoring players play either side with equal facility. Clinically they ask for hypnosis or a hypnotic injection to speed treatment: never surrendering in the game, they seek complete surrender from the therapist, so YDYB is "a social solution to a conflict about surrender." It is common with fear of blushing: "If I'm talking to somebody I have to keep thinking of things to say. If I don't, I'll blush. . . . I can't stand a lull." The Adult kept busy holds the Child down; the game structures unstructured time. Motto: "Don't get panicky, the Parent never succeeds."
- **Internal and external forms reverse the roles.** In groups her Child plays the inadequate help-seeker; at home with her husband her Parent comes out as the wise, efficient suggestion-giver. During courtship she plays helpless, and the bossy Parent emerges after the honeymoon.
- **Antithesis.** The responders are playing "I'm Only Trying to Help You", of which YDYB is the inverse: ITHY has one therapist and many clients, YDYB one client and many "therapists". So the antithesis is not to play ITHY. To an opening of the form "What do you do if . . ." (WYDI): "That *is* a difficult problem. What *are* you going to do about it?" To "X didn't work out properly": "That *is* too bad." Both are polite enough to leave White at a loss, or to cross the transaction so her frustration shows and can be explored. In groups, susceptible members should decline ITHY when invited. Socially, join a friendly, harmless round. If it is an attempt to exploit professional knowledge, an antithetical move exposes White's Child and arouses resentment; the best policy is "to flee from the opening move and look for a stimulating game of first-degree 'Rapo.'"
- **Relatives.** **"Why Did You—No But" (YDNB)**, the obverse, in which the Parent wins and the defensive Child retires in confusion; it is close to "Furthermore". The reverse at first resembles **"Peasant"** (suggestions eagerly accepted) and ends as intellectual "Rapo", like the switch from positive to negative transference. The second-degree hard form is **"Do Me Something"**: she sullenly refuses to do the housework whatever her husband says each evening. The game aspect raises why he chose such a spouse and how he maintains the situation.
- **Protocol.** Thesis: See if you can present a solution I can't find fault with. Aim: reassurance. Roles: *Helpless person*, Advisers. Dynamics: surrender conflict (oral). Examples: (1) "Yes, but I can't do my homework now because. . . ." (2) helpless wife. Social (Adult-Adult): "What do you do if. . . ." / "Why don't you. . . ." / "Yes, but. . . ." Psychological (Parent-Child): "I can make you grateful for my help." / "Go ahead and try." Moves: (1) Problem-Solution. (2) Objection-Solution. (3) Objection-Disconcertion.
- **Advantages.** IP: reassurance. EP: avoids surrender. IS: YDYB, Parental role. ES: YDYB, Child role. Bio: rational discussion. Ex: Everybody wants to dominate me.

# Sexual Games

Played to exploit or fight off sexual impulses. They are "perversions of the sexual instincts in which the satisfaction is displaced from the sexual act to the crucial transactions which constitute the payoff of the game." The agent is usually a woman, because the hard male forms verge on criminality and belong to the underworld. Unmarried people play these as readily as spouses.

## Let's You and Him Fight (LYAHF)

"Essentially feminine", and the basis of much of the world's literature. It comes in three forms:

- **As a maneuver it is romantic.** She challenges two men to fight with the promise of surrender to the winner, and keeps the bargain: an honest transaction.
- **As a ritual it is tragic.** Custom demands the fight even against her wishes, and if the wrong man wins she must take him. If she is unwilling, it gives her scope for games such as "Let's Pull A Fast One on Joey".
- **As a game it is comic.** While the two men fight, she decamps with a third, from the position that "honest competition is for suckers."

It is the classical switch of a rebuffed "Rapo" player (Potiphar's wife). An outsider-driven form forces a girl to cry rape so that others may play LYAHF.

## Perversion

Heterosexual perversions (fetishism, sadism, masochism) are "symptomatic of a confused Child and are treated accordingly", but their transactional aspects yield to game analysis and social control, which neutralizes indulgence even if the impulses remain. Mildly sadistic or masochistic people take "a primitive kind of Mental Health position" (they are strongly sexed, and abstinence will do harm) and play "Wooden Leg": "What do you expect from someone as strongly sexed as I am?"

- **Antithesis.** Extend ordinary courtesy to oneself and one's partner: no verbal or physical flagellation, only conventional coitus. In a true pervert this lays bare the second element, that coitus holds little interest and the real satisfaction is the humiliating foreplay: "After all this work, I have to have intercourse, yet!" Pleading and evasion are then nullified, and specific psychotherapy becomes possible. This applies to ordinary "sexual psychopaths", not to malignant schizophrenic or criminal perversions, nor to those who confine themselves to fantasy.
- Berne treats "Homosexuality" likewise as elaborated into a game and a subculture. He holds that many of its disabilities "arise from making it into a game": the provocative behavior behind "Cops and Robbers", "Why Does This Always Happen to Us", "It's the Society We Live In" and "All Great Men Were" is amenable to social control. Analysis of his games may let the "professional homosexual" establish "a quiet ménage" instead of playing his own variation of "Ain't It Awful".

## Rapo

Played between a man and a woman; more politely called "Kiss Off" or "Indignation".

- **First-Degree, "Kiss Off"**: mild flirtation at social gatherings. White signals availability and enjoys the pursuit; once the man commits himself the game is over. Politely: "I appreciate your compliments and thank you very much." A skillful player keeps moving around a large party so he must maneuver to follow.
- **Second-Degree, "Indignation"** ("Buzz off, Buster"): her primary gratification is rejecting him. She leads him into a far more serious commitment and enjoys his discomfiture. Black is not as helpless as he seems and is usually playing a variation of "Kick Me".
- **Third-Degree**: "a vicious game which ends in murder, suicide or the courtroom." She leads him into compromising physical contact, then claims criminal assault or irreparable damage, immediately or after a long affair. Mercenary or morbid allies (press, police, counselors, relatives) may join her, and may turn on her, so that she becomes a tool in their games.
- Joseph refused Potiphar's wife, who made "the classical switch into 'Let's You and Him Fight,' an excellent example of the way a hard player reacts to antithesis, and of the dangers that beset people who refuse to play games." Combined, the two make the **"Badger Game"**. The childhood prototype is the same as for "Frigid Woman" (the girl who gets the boy dirty, then sneers).
- **Antithesis.** The man's safety depends on "his capacity to distinguish genuine expressions of feeling from moves in the game"; with that social control he can enjoy "Kiss Off". Against Potiphar's Wife there is no safe antithesis "other than checking out before closing time with no forwarding address." In 1938 Berne met an aging Joseph in Aleppo who had left Constantinople thirty-two years earlier, after a lady of the Sultan's cornered him in the Yildiz harem; he abandoned his shop but took his gold francs.
- **Male versions**, in commercial settings: "Casting Couch" (and then she didn't get the part), "Cuddle Up" (and then she got fired).
- **Protocol (Third Degree).** Aim: malicious revenge. Roles: *Seductress*, Wolf. Dynamics: penis envy, oral violence; "Kiss Off" is phallic, "Indignation" has strong anal elements. Examples: (1) I'll tell on you, you dirty little boy. (2) Wronged woman. Social (Adult-Adult): "I'm sorry if I went further than you intended me to." / "You have violated me and must pay the full penalty." Psychological (Child-Child): "See how irresistible I am." / "Now I've got you, you son of a bitch." Moves: (1) seduction / counter-seduction; (2) surrender / victory; (3) confrontation / collapse.
- **Advantages.** IP: expression of hatred and projection of guilt. EP: avoidance of emotional sexual intimacy. IS: NIGYSOB. ES: "Ain't It Awful", "Courtroom", "Let's You and Him Fight". Bio: sexual and belligerent exchanges. Ex: I am blameless.

## The Stocking Game

Of the "Rapo" family; hysterical exhibitionism. A woman new to a group soon raises her leg provocatively: "Oh my, I have a run in my stocking." The men are aroused and the women angered, and any confrontation meets protestations of innocence or counter-accusations. The significant feature is her **lack of adaptation**: she seldom waits to learn who she is dealing with or how to time the move, so it stands out as inappropriate. Her aim is to prove that other people have lascivious minds; her Adult is conned by her Child and her Parent (usually a lascivious mother) into ignoring her own provocativeness and others' good sense, so the game is self-destructive. There are oral variants (thrusting the breasts forward and remarking on their size or on a lump), anal variants (squirming), and a symbolic form in bereaved women who "exhibit" their widowhood insincerely.

- **Antithesis.** These players tolerate antithesis poorly and may not return to a group that ignores or counters the game. "Antithesis must be carefully distinguished in this game from reprisal, since the latter signifies that White has won." Women counter it more skillfully than men, who have little incentive to, so leave it to the other women present.

## Uproar

The classical game is between a domineering father and a teen-age daughter, with a sexually inhibited mother. He finds fault, she answers impudently (or the reverse), voices rise, and it ends in one of three ways: he retires and slams his door, she slams hers, or both slam theirs. "In any case, the end of a game of 'Uproar' is marked by a slamming door." It is a distressing but effective solution to their sexual problem: they can live together only if angry, and the slammed doors emphasize the separate bedrooms. In degenerate households the father inspects the daughter after every date, and a violent altercation may expel her at night, until nature takes its course and his suspicions are "justified" to the "helpless" mother.

- In general "Uproar" may be played by any two people avoiding sexual intimacy. It is a common terminal phase of "Frigid Woman". Siblings' physical combat is a semi-ritualistic "Uproar", in upper-class England channeled into the playing fields.
- **Antithesis.** The father enjoys it more than he thinks. Usually the daughter makes the antithetical move, through an early, often premature or forced marriage. The mother can, if psychologically able, give up her frigidity. An outside interest for the father may end it, with other complications. For married couples, use the antitheses of "Frigid Woman" (decline "Uproar"). Under the right circumstances "Uproar" leads naturally into "Courtroom".

# Underworld Games

What probation workers, criminologists and therapists in prisons should know.

## Cops and Robbers (C&R)

Many criminals get as much satisfaction from outwitting the police as from the take, often more: "at the Adult level, are games played for the material rewards, the take; but at the Child level it is the thrill of the chase: the getaway and the cool-off." The childhood prototype is **hide-and-seek**, whose essential element is "the chagrin at being found". If father gives up, the boy is disappointed, not victorious, and a good father looks chagrined when caught in turn: "being found is the necessary payoff." Socially it is a battle of wits; psychologically it is "set up like compulsive gambling, in which White's Adult has to lose in order for his Child to win." The child with an insoluble hiding place spoils the game by turning it into an Adult procedure.

- **Players versus straight professionals.** Some habitual criminals are in it for profit, some for the game, and many handle it either way. The "compulsive winner", whose Child really does not want to be caught, rarely is: "the fix is always in." The "compulsive loser" plays C&R, seldom does well and ends up "squawking rather than riding high." The C&R burglar leaves a calling card of gratuitous vandalism; the C&R bank robber looks for an excuse to vent anger, while the straight one avoids violence; the straight professional never operates until the fix is in. Gangs take drastic measures against a member whose need to be caught begins to show. Gamblers split the same way: gamesters, "in whom the strength of the Adult's desire to win is exceeded only by the strength of the Child's need to lose", and house operators, who are not playing. Distinguishing "players" from "straight professionals" transactionally, below the social level, removes the ambiguity that has made criminological studies "ambiguous and unproductive". Most clinical knowledge concerns players, because they get caught.
- The roles switch, as in "Alcoholic": Parental Cop by day, Child Robber after dark; the reformed player becomes a Rescuer. Each player has his own *modus operandi* for getting caught. Kleptomaniacs play trivial C&R, and the "perfect murder" fantasy is the hardest form played in fantasy. Variants: "Auditors and Robbers" (embezzlers) and "Customs and Robbers" (smugglers). For the arrested professional, "Courtroom" is a procedure; for compulsive-winner lawyers it is a constructive game.
- **Antithesis.** This is a matter for criminologists, and the police and judiciary are not antithetical but play their roles. Until C&R is accepted "as the very heart of the matter in a significant percentage of cases," criminology will deal in trivialities. In one hard case a young man shot his fiancée and had to tell the police four times before they believed him: "It just seemed to me that all my life I was bound to end up in the chair." In game terms, a nine-year-old boy decided he would end in the chair, spent his life heading there, and used his girl as a target to set himself up.
- **Protocol.** Thesis: See if you can catch me. Aim: reassurance. Roles: *Robber*, Cop (Judge). Dynamics: phallic intrusion. Examples: (1) hide-and-seek, tag; (2) crime. Social (Parent-Child): "See if you can catch me." / "That's my job." Psychological (Parent-Child): "You must catch me." / "Aha, there you are." Moves: (1) W Defiance / B Indignation. (2) W Concealment / B Frustration. (3) W Provocation / B Victory.
- **Advantages.** IP: material indemnification for old wrong. EP: counterphobic. IS: See if you can catch me. ES: I almost got away with it (pastime: They almost got away with it). Bio: notoriety. Ex: I've always been a loser.

## How Do You Get Out of Here ("Want Out")

Prisoners survive best whose time is structured by an activity (reading or writing books), a pastime (escape, as with Casanova and Baron Trenck) or a game; political police break prisoners by inactivity and social deprivation. "Want Out" must be distinguished from the **operation "Good Behavior"**, in which an inmate who really wants freedom complies so as to be released as early as possible, nowadays often through a good game of "Psychiatry", Group Therapy Type. In "Good Behavior" Parent, Adult and Child work together for discharge. In "Want Out", Parent and Adult go through the motions until the critical moment, when the Child, frightened of the uncertain world outside, takes over and spoils it (in the late 1930s, recently arrived immigrants from Germany who became psychotic would improve and beg for release, then relapse as the day approached).

- **Antithesis.** Alert administrators recognize both; beginning group therapists are taken in. These are the most frequent manipulations in psychiatrically oriented prisons, so ferret them out early. "Good Behavior" is an honest operation and may be discussed openly; "Want Out" requires active therapy.
- **Relatives.** **"You've Got to Listen"** is an operation: the inmate or client demands the right to complain, often irrelevantly, to be assured of a hearing. Cut him off and there is trouble; grant his demands and he increases them. Listen "patiently and with signs of interest" and he is satisfied and cooperative. Administrators must distinguish it from serious demands for action. **"Bum Rap"** is part of the procedure for a straight criminal trying to get out, but a game for one who does not use it effectively, "since if he gets out he will no longer have much excuse to holler."

## Let's Pull a Fast One on Joey (FOOJY)

The prototype is "The Big Store", the big-time confidence game; many small grifts and the badger game are FOOJY. "No man can be beaten at FOOJY unless he has larceny in his veins": Black tells White that dumb-honest-old-Joey is waiting to be taken, and an honest White would back off or warn Joey. Just as Joey is about to pay off (or be cuckolded), something goes wrong, and White must play Joey's rules, "and they hurt." The mark is expected to know the rules. Honest squawking is a calculated risk, but false accusation is cheating. A con man who works a drunk mark, or a mark with a sense of humor, gets no sympathy: "Experienced con men are scared of marks who laugh after they have been taken." A practical joke is a pastime in which Joey suffers; FOOJY is a game in which the joke is arranged to backfire on White. It is three- or four-handed (the police play the fourth hand) and related to "Let's You and Him Fight".
