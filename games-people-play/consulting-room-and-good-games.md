# Consulting Room Games and Good Games

White is the one who is "it"; Black is the other party. In the Advantages, IP is internal psychological, EP external psychological, IS internal social, ES external social, Bio biological and Ex existential.

# Consulting Room Games

The games played tenaciously in the therapeutic situation, the most important for a professional to recognize. They fall into three types by the agent's role:

1. Played by therapists and case workers: "I'm Only Trying to Help You", "Psychiatry".
2. Played by professionally trained people who are patients in groups: "Greenhouse".
3. Played by lay patients and clients: "Indigence", "Peasant", "Stupid", "Wooden Leg".

## Greenhouse

A variation of "Psychiatry", played hardest by young social scientists. Among colleagues, jocular "Psychoanalysis" ("Your hostility is showing", "How mechanical can a defense mechanism get?", or Berne's own "I see National Parapraxis Week is here again") is a harmless pastime and a normal phase of learning. As group patients they may pursue the mutual critique seriously, and when the therapist heads it off it turns into "Greenhouse". Recent graduates hold an exaggerated respect for "Genuine Feelings". A feeling's arrival is announced, then presented "as though it were a rare flower which should be regarded with awe", while the others act as connoisseurs at a botanical garden judging whether it is good enough for the National Feeling Show. A questioning intervention is resented as a "clumsy-fingered clod mauling the fragile petals"; the therapist knows that to understand a flower it may be necessary to dissect it.

- **Antithesis.** "The irony of the above description." Allowed to run, the game may go on unchanged for years, leaving a "therapeutic experience" of having "expressed hostility" and learned to "face feelings", while little of dynamic significance happens. The irony is aimed at their teachers and the milieu that encourages such over-fastidiousness. A properly timed skeptical remark may "divorce them from foppish Parental influences" toward a less self-conscious robustness: instead of hothouse cultivation, feelings grow naturally, "to be plucked when they are ripe."
- **Advantage.** Chiefly EP: it avoids intimacy by setting special conditions for expressing feelings and special restrictions on the responses of those present.

## I'm Only Trying to Help You (ITHY)

Possible in any profession, most florid among social workers of a certain training. Its structure became clear at a poker table, where a psychologist with an unbeatable hand raised a puzzled businessman: "Don't be upset, I'm only trying to help you!" The loser, beaten, remarked ruefully, "You sure were helpful!"

- **The sequence.** The worker gives advice. The client reports it did not work. The worker shrugs it off with resignation and tries again, perhaps with a twinge of frustration. He sees no need to question his motives, since colleagues do the same and supervisors support the "correct" procedure. Against a hard player (a hostile obsessional) he feels increasingly inadequate. At worst an angry paranoid rushes in crying "Look what you made me do!", and the helper thinks "But I was only trying to help you!" "This bewilderment is the payoff."
- **Legitimate help versus ITHY.** "I think we can do something about it", "I know what to do", "I was assigned to help you" and "My fee for helping you will be. . . ." are, in good faith, Adult offers of professional qualifications. ITHY rests on the position that people are ungrateful and disappointing. Success alarms the helper's Parent and invites sabotage, because it would threaten the position, so he needs reassurance that his help will not be accepted. The client answers with "Look How Hard I'm Trying" or "There's Nothing You Can Do to Help Me". Flexible players compromise: help may be accepted if it takes a long time, so therapists apologize for quick results. At the opposite pole are good lawyers, who help without personal involvement or sentimentality: "craftsmanship takes the place of covert strenuousness."
- **Everyday forms.** Relatives play "I Can Get It For You Wholesale". Parents play it, and the offspring's complement is usually "Look What You Made Me Do". Socially it may be a "Schlemiel" in which the damage is done while being helpful, with the victim playing "Why Does This Always Happen To Me?"
- **The professional's antitheses**, chosen by the state of the relationship and the attitude of the patient's Child:
  1. The classical psychoanalytic antithesis, the most thoroughgoing and hardest to tolerate: ignore the invitation completely. The patient tries harder and falls into despair (anger or depression), "the characteristic sign that a game has been frustrated", which may lead to a useful confrontation.
  2. A gentler (but not prim) confrontation on the first invitation: the therapist is the patient's therapist, not his manager.
  3. Gentler still: put the patient in a therapy group and let the other patients handle it.
  4. With an acutely disturbed patient, play along at first. A psychiatrist prescribes medication and a hygienic regimen (baths, exercise, rest periods, regular meals). The patient either (1) carries it out and feels better, (2) carries it out scrupulously and complains that it does not help, or (3) mentions casually that he forgot or abandoned it. In cases 2 and 3, decide whether he is ready for game analysis or needs other preparation.
- **The patient's antithesis.** "Don't tell me what to do to help myself, I'll tell you what to do to help me." If the therapist is a Schlemiel: "Don't help me, help him." Serious ITHY players generally lack a sense of humor, and a patient's antithetical move may earn lifelong enmity. In everyday life such moves "should not be initiated unless one is prepared to carry them through ruthlessly and take the consequences": spurning a relative who "Can Get It For You Wholesale" may cause serious domestic complications.
- **Protocol.** Thesis: Nobody ever does what I tell them. Aim: alleviation of guilt. Roles: *Helper*, Client. Dynamics: masochism. Examples: (1) children learning, parent intervenes; (2) social worker and client. Social (Parent-Child): "What do I do now?" / "Here's what you do." Psychological (Parent-Child): "See how adequate I am." / "I'll make you feel inadequate." Moves: (1) Instructions requested-Instructions given. (2) Procedure bungled-Reproof. (3) Demonstration that procedures are faulty-Implicit apology.
- **Advantages.** IP: martyrdom. EP: avoids facing inadequacies. IS: "PTA", Projective Type; ingratitude. ES: "Psychiatry", Projective Type. Bio: slapping from client, stroking from supervisors. Ex: All people are ungrateful.

## Indigence

Thesis, in Henry Miller's words: "looking for a job without the slightest intention of taking one", without even bothering "to look through the columns of the want ads." It is the client's complement to ITHY, played as professionally by the client who earns his living by it as by the social worker who earns hers.

- **The case of Miss Black.** Her agency's clients were always "making progress" but rarely "rehabilitated". She asked her own clients how many job opportunities they had actually investigated. One answered an ad a day, but only for sales jobs, though his stutter held him back from his "chosen career". She was reprimanded for "undue pressure". She then discussed ITHY and "Indigence" with selected able-bodied clients and said that unless they found jobs she would cut off their funds and refer them elsewhere. Several found work, some for the first time in years, then complained to her supervisor, who reprimanded her again: they were not "really rehabilitated". No one would say what that meant.
- **The tacit contract.** W: "I'll try to help you (providing you don't get better)." C: "I'll look for employment (providing I don't have to find any)." Breaking it penalizes both: the agency loses a client, the client loses benefits, and complaints reach higher authorities. Kept, it gives the client his benefits and the agency its chance to "reach out" plus "clinical material" for "client-centered" staff conferences. Miss Black "reached in" instead, and disturbed everyone, though she was only complying with the stated intent of the regulations.
- Indigence as a game is played by only a limited percentage of welfare clients, and is supported only by workers trained in ITHY. Allied games: **"Veteran"** (professional veterans and the agencies that serve them) and **"Clinic"** (some hospital out-patients, who get no money but serve teaching and research, a legitimate Adult satisfaction).
- **Antithesis.** Withhold the benefits. The risk comes less from the player than from the game being culturally syntonic: colleagues, the aroused public, agencies and unions. The resulting outcry of "Yes, Yes, How About That?" may be regarded as a healthy operation or pastime; the license to ask that question underpins democratic freedoms.

## Peasant

The prototype: an arthritic Bulgarian villager sells her only cow to see the professor in Sofia. He presents her case to students and prescribes treatment, and she exclaims, "Gee, you're wonderful, Professor!" She never fills the prescription (no apothecary, and she would never part with so valuable a paper), lives on crippled and happy, and thanks him nightly in her prayers. Years later, passing through, he is gratified by her homage and fails to notice that she limps as badly as ever.

- **Socially**, the motto is "Gee you're wonderful, Mr. Murgatroyd!" (GYWM). In the innocent form Murgatroyd *is* wonderful, and naive young women romanticize his imperfections or sophisticated ones exploit them; the game lies in the romanticizing or exploiting, the innocence in genuine respect for his accomplishments. In the dissembled form, the woman (perhaps a high-class prostitute) plays "Little Old Me" and uses GYWM as sheer flattery for the perquisites, bewildered by him or laughing at him underneath.
- **Clinically**, the motto is "Gee you're wonderful, Professor!" (GYWP). In the innocent form the patient stays well as long as she can believe in GYWP, which obliges the therapist to behave well in public and private. In the dissembled form she hopes he will think "You're uncommonly perceptive" (YUP), then makes him look foolish and moves on. "The simplest way for the patient to win GYWP is not to get better." One woman left her psychiatrist unimproved with many salaams, played GYWP with her clergyman, seduced him into second-degree "Rapo", and "inadvertently" told a neighbor who was a church elder. In a therapy group she could no longer maneuver, examined her behavior, and gave up both games.
- **Antithesis.** First decide whether it is innocent. If so, let it continue for the patient's benefit until her Adult is established enough to risk countermeasures. If not, act at the first appropriate opportunity once she is prepared to understand: steadfastly refuse to give advice, and make clear this is not "Poker-Faced Psychiatry" but a well-thought-out policy. The refusals may enrage her or precipitate acute anxiety; if she is too upset, restore the therapeutic situation by appropriate procedures. In the dissembled form, the first goal is "to split off the Adult from the hypocritical Child so that the game can be analyzed." Socially, avoid intimate entanglements with innocent GYWM players, as an intelligent actor's agent advises his clients; dissembled players can sometimes be "de-GYWMed" into delightful company.

## Psychiatry

Psychiatry as a procedure (shock therapy, hypnosis, drugs, psychoanalysis, orthopsychiatry, group therapy) differs from "Psychiatry" as a game, which any of them can serve. The game rests on the position "I am a healer", backed by a diploma: "It says here I am a healer." It is a constructive, benevolent position, and trained players can do much good.

- **Antithesis.** Moderate therapeutic ardor. Ambroise Paré: "I treat them, but God cures them" (with *primum non nocere* and *vis medicatrix naturae*). Replace the position with "I will apply what therapeutic procedures I have learned in the hope that they will be of some benefit." This forestalls "Since I am a healer, if you don't get better it's your fault" (ITHY) and "Since you're a healer, I'll get better for you" ("Peasant"). A good clinic is one that makes its therapists aware of this.
- **The patient's game.** It is commonest in patients previously treated by weaker therapists; some pick weak analysts in succession, sharpening their game. Duplex transaction: Adult: "I am coming to be cured." Child: "You will never cure me, but you will teach me to be a better neurotic (play a better game of 'Psychiatry')." In **"Mental Health"**: "Everything will get better if I apply the principles of mental health which I have read and heard about." One patient played "Psychiatry", "Mental Health" and then "Transactional Analysis". When this was discussed frankly she agreed to drop "Mental Health" but asked to keep "Psychiatry" because it comforted her. Allowed to, she recited dreams for months, then grew genuinely curious about what was wrong with her, with good results.
- **Variants.**
  - **"Archaeology"**: if she can only find out "who had the button", all will be well; endless rumination over childhood.
  - **"Critique"**: she describes her feelings and the therapist tells her what is wrong with them.
  - **"Self-Expression"**: the dogma "Feelings are Good", with vulgar expletives applauded. A woman from a Self-Expression group told her oft-repeated incest story to a sophisticated group, met indifference instead of awe, and was enraged. The group "was more interested in her transactional anger than in her historical incest". She accused them of not being Freudian, though Freud avoided making a game of psychoanalysis by saying he himself was not a Freudian.
  - **"Tell Me This"**: like "Twenty Questions". Members question White about a dream until one finds a question he cannot answer, then sit back: "Aha! If you could answer *that* one, you would certainly get better, so I have done *my* part." It is a distant relative of YDYB, and some groups run on it for years. White can counter by answering every question, throwing back "I've answered all your questions and you haven't cured me, so what does that make you?" In schoolrooms, pupils guess which answer will make the teacher happy instead of processing the data. Teachers of ancient Greek and Hebrew play a pedantic variant.

## Stupid

The mild thesis: "I laugh with you at my own clumsiness and stupidity." The sullen form of the seriously disturbed: "I am stupid, that's the way I am, so do me something." Both are played from a depressive position. Distinguish it from "Schlemiel", whose clumsiness is an aggressive bid for forgiveness (forgiveness makes the "Stupid" player uneasy, because it threatens his position), and from **"Clown"**, a pastime reinforcing "I am cute and harmless". The critical transaction is to make Black call him stupid or treat him as stupid, and to have his behavior taken seriously as evidence. The external gain is large: the less he learns, the better he plays, so he need not study or learn anything leading to advancement. Under stress he may turn out not stupid at all, like the "stupid" younger son of the fairy tale.

- **Antithesis (mild form).** Refrain from laughing at the clumsiness or railing at the stupidity, and "make a friend for life". Cyclothymic players seem to want everyone laughing with them when euphoric and resent the abstainer, but when depressed they turn on those who laughed, and the abstainer may be the only one welcome. "It is no use telling White that he is not really stupid": he may know his limits, which is how the game began. Show whatever respect his real aptitudes deserve (often psychological insight), which differs from clumsy "reassurance", itself usually a move in ITHY. "The antithesis of 'Stupid' is not to substitute another game, but simply to refrain from playing 'Stupid.'"
- **Sullen form.** He provokes helplessness or exasperation and wins either way: doing nothing shows Black helpless, doing something shows him exasperated. These players also favor YDYB. There is no easy solution yet.

## Wooden Leg

Thesis: "What do you expect of a man with a wooden leg?" Its most dramatic form is "The Plea of Insanity": "What do you expect of someone as emotionally disturbed as I am—that I would refrain from killing people?" The jury is asked to reply, "Certainly not, we would hardly impose that restriction on you!" This legal game, acceptable to American culture, differs from the respected principle that a psychosis may be so profound that no reasonable person would hold the sufferer responsible. In Japan drunkenness, and in Russia war-time military service, serve as the accepted excuses. Against the plea stand the man with a wooden leg who demonstrated very competent jitterbug dancing at Army amputation centers, blind men who practice law and hold office, deaf psychiatrists and handless typists.

- **When to intervene.** Someone content with a real, exaggerated or imaginary disability may be let alone. "But the moment he presents himself for psychiatric treatment, the question arises if he is using his life to his own best advantage, and if he can rise above his disability." Expect educated public opinion against the therapist, and relatives who complained loudest about the infirmity, together with the ITHY players, turning on him when the patient progresses.
- **The pleas.** The stutterer who will consider only sales work. The "Ideological Plea" ("What do you expect of a man who lives in a society like ours?") and the "Psychosomatic Plea": one patient found therapists who accepted one plea but not the other, and so "proved that psychiatry couldn't help people." Colds, head injuries, situational stress, modern living, American culture, the economic system; "I drink because I'm Irish"; "This wouldn't happen if I lived in Russia or Tahiti" (mental patients there resemble American ones). A literate player has no difficulty finding authorities to support him. Weigh "If It Weren't For Them" and "They Let Me Down" carefully, in clinics and in social research. More sophisticated: a broken home, being neurotic, being in analysis, "a disease known as alcoholism". Topping them all: "If I stop doing this I won't be able to analyze it, and then I'll never get better." The game is especially pernicious when the patient finds a therapist who plays the same plea.
- **Obverse: "Rickshaw"**: "If they only had (rickshaws) (duckbill platypuses) (girls who spoke ancient Egyptian) around this town, I never would have got into this mess."
- **Antithesis.** It is not difficult "if the therapist can distinguish clearly between his own Parent and Adult, and if the therapeutic aim is explicitly understood by both parties." The "good" Parent accepts the plea; the "harsh" Parent rejects it and enters a contest of wills. The player knows how to milk both. The Adult declines both: to "What do you expect of a neurotic?" the reply is "I don't expect anything. The question is, what do you expect of yourself?" The only demand is a serious answer; the only concession is reasonable time to give one, six weeks to six months, depending on the relationship and the patient's preparation.

# Good Games

Since all games are ulterior and so involve some exploitation, and clinicians mostly see the bad ones, good games are hard to find. A good game's "social contribution outweighs the complexity of its motivations, particularly if the player has come to terms with those motivations without futility or cynicism". Berne calls his examples "admittedly deficient in both number and quality."

## Busman's Holiday

Strictly a pastime, and constructive: the American mail carrier who helps a Tokyo postman on his rounds, the ear-nose-and-throat specialist who spends his holiday in a Haitian hospital, as refreshed as if he had gone lion hunting. The Peace Corps gave it official sanction. It becomes a game when the work is a show for some ulterior purpose, and even then it remains "one of the more commendable covers for other activities".

## Cavalier

Played by men not under sexual pressure: younger men with a satisfactory marriage or liaison, more often older men gracefully resigned to monogamy or celibacy. White remarks on a woman's good qualities, never exceeding what suits her station, the situation and good taste, and within those limits gives full play to creativity and originality. "The object is not to seduce but to exhibit his virtuosity in the art of effective compliment." Where both know the game, it may stretch delightfully to extravagance. A man of the world stops when he ceases to amuse (for her sake) or when his offerings deteriorate (for his pride of craftsmanship). Poets play it for the external social advantage of critics and public. The European romantics and British poets (Herrick, Lovelace) excel; the American "Fruit Stand" school ("your eyes are like avocados, your lips like cucumbers") cannot compare.

- **Her part.** The proper complement is "I Admire Your Productions, Mr. M.", since he offers his poetry for appreciation, not himself; plain GYWM misses the point. A sulky woman answers with Second-Degree "Rapo" ("Buzz Off, Buster"); a stupid one plays First-Degree "Rapo", feeding her vanity and ignoring his craft. The game is spoiled if she treats it as an attempt at seduction. Distinguish it from a straightforward courtship, whose transactions have no ulterior motive. The female counterpart is **"Blarney"**, played by gallant Irish ladies in their sunset years.
- **Partial analysis.** Aim: mutual admiration. Roles: *Poet*, Appreciative subject. Social (Adult-Adult): "See how good I can make you feel." / "My, but you make me feel good." Psychological (Child-Child): "See what phrases I can create." / "My, but you're creative." IP: creativity and reassurance of attractiveness. EP: avoids rejection for unnecessary sexual advances. IS: "Cavalier". ES: these may be resigned. Bio: mutual stroking. Ex: I can live gracefully.

## Happy to Help

White is consistently helpful, with an ulterior motive: penance for past wickedness, cover for present wickedness, making friends to exploit later, or prestige. Whoever questions his motives must still credit his actions, since there are worse ways to do all of these. Philanthropists who compete ("I gave more money . . . than you did") still compete constructively. Players have friends who minimize their motives and enemies who minimize their actions, so "objective" discussion is practically nonexistent: "People who claim to be neutral soon show which side they are neutral on." It is the basis of much public relations, perhaps the most pleasant of commercial games. Even its reprehensible family form, parents competing for a child's affection, beats the alternatives ("Mommy is sicker than daddy", "Why do you love him more than you love me?").

## Homely Sage

Properly a script with gamelike aspects. A well-educated man learns all he can beyond his own business, retires to a small town, and becomes known as someone people can bring any problem to, "from a knock in the engine to a senile relative". He helps himself if competent and otherwise refers them to qualified experts, "making no pretenses, but always willing to listen". In its best form it is played by people who have first examined their motives with a psychiatrist.

## They'll Be Glad They Knew Me

A worthier variant of "I'll Show Them". In destructive "I'll Show Them", White maneuvers into a superior position for the power to exercise his spite. In constructive "I'll Show Them", he works for prestige so that his enemies will be "eaten with envy and with regret for not having treated him better." In "They'll Be Glad They Knew Me" he works for his former associates' interests, showing them their friendliness and respect were justified. His means as well as his ends must be honorable for a secure win. Both are merely secondary advantages of success until "White is more interested in the effects on his enemies or friends than he is in the success itself."
