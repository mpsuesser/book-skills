# Life Games and Marital Games

White is the one who is "it"; Black is the other party. Each protocol gives Thesis, Aim, Roles ("it" first), Dynamics, Examples (childhood, adult), Paradigm (social / psychological), Moves and the six Advantages. In the Advantages, IP is internal psychological, EP external psychological, IS internal social, ES external social, Bio biological and Ex existential.

# Life Games

These games offer the most opportunity for lifelong careers and are the most likely to involve innocent bystanders. They merge with marital games on one side and underworld games on the other.

## Alcoholic

In game analysis "there is no such thing as alcoholism or 'an alcoholic,' but there is a role called the Alcoholic in a certain type of game." In full flower it is five-handed:

- **Alcoholic**: White.
- **Persecutor**: usually the spouse.
- **Rescuer**: usually someone of the same sex, often the good family doctor. After six months on the wagon they congratulate each other; the next day White is found in the gutter.
- **Patsy (Dummy)**: gives without persecuting or rescuing, like the delicatessen man extending credit, or more often White's mother, who gives money and sympathizes about the wife who doesn't understand him. The two pretend to believe in some project to explain his need for money.
- **Agitator** (optional): the "good guy" who offers supplies unasked: "Come have a drink with me."
- **Connection**: the bartender or liquor clerk, the direct source of supply, who understands alcoholic talk. Like any professional, he knows when to stop and refuses to serve.

Early on, the wife plays all three supporting roles: Patsy at midnight (undressing him, making coffee), Persecutor in the morning, Rescuer in the evening. Later, the Persecutor and Rescuer are tolerated only if they also supply (a free meal at the Mission House, a handout after a scolding).

**Payoff.** Drinking is "merely an incidental pleasure". The culmination is the hangover, the psychological torment of the morning after. The Child arranges to be severely scolded "not only by the internal Parent but by any parental figures in the environment who are interested enough to oblige." One patient berated himself to a silent psychiatrist, then told his group with smug satisfaction that the psychiatrist had called him all those names. Treat the morning after, "the self-indulgence in self-castigation", not the drinking. Heavy drinkers without hangovers are not in this category. Pastimes: "Martini" (how many drinks and how mixed) and "Morning After" (let me tell you about *my* hangover).

**Variants.** "Dry Alcoholic": the same moves, cast and job-losing procedure, without a bottle. "Addict": more sinister and faster, leaning on the Persecutor and the Connection. A.A. keeps playing but induces the Alcoholic to take the Rescuer role; cases have been reported of an A.A. chapter that ran out of Alcoholics to work on and resumed drinking, there being no other way to continue the game. The disease idea taught alcoholics "Wooden Leg": "I am a sinner" became "What do you expect from a sick man?" Even so, A.A. "is still for most people the best initiation into the therapy of over-indulgence." The Rescuer is tempted to play "I'm Only Trying to Help You", the Persecutor "Look What You've Done to Me", the Patsy "Good Joe".

**Antithesis.** The game is played hard. One woman in a group asked what members "really" thought of her ("That's not what I want. I want to know what you really think"). When they refused to persecute, she got drunk that night and had her husband send her to a sanitarium. The correct procedure, "after careful preliminary groundwork", is to "take an Adult contractual position and refuse to play any of the roles": not Persecutor, not Rescuer, and not Patsy either (do not waive financial or punctuality obligations). If the patient cannot tolerate abstaining from the game, refer him to a Rescuer. The cure lies in stopping the game, not shifting roles. Since he is "classically afraid of intimacy", the substitute may have to be another game. The criterion of a true "game cure" is that he can drink socially without putting himself in jeopardy; "The usual 'total abstinence' cure will not satisfy the game analyst." The organization closest to the ideal encourages teen-age children of alcoholics to break away from the game rather than shift roles. Expect public indignation and alarmed Rescuers.

**Relatives.** "Have One": at a picnic White urges the Blacks (Patsies), "Have one!", which licenses him to have four or five. If they refuse, the game is unmasked and he finds other companions. His non-drinking wife, as Persecutor, consents because she too wants the game to go on. Patsies are often lonely people who gain by it ("Good Joe"). A constructive variant of "Good Joe" goes around asking how best to help people; its inverse, "Tough Guy", asks how best to hurt them, to bask in real tough guys' glory.

- **Protocol.** Thesis: How bad I've been; see if you can stop me. Aim: self-castigation. Roles: *Alcoholic*, Persecutor, Rescuer, Patsy, Connection. Dynamics: oral deprivation. Examples: (1) "See if you can catch me" (lying, hiding things, seeking derogatory comments, finding benevolent neighbors); (2) the alcoholic and his circle. Social (Adult-Adult): "Tell me what you really think of me or help me stop drinking." / "I'll be frank with you." Psychological (Parent-Child): "See if you can stop me." / "You must stop drinking because. . . ." Moves: (1) Provocation-accusation or forgiveness. (2) Indulgence-anger or disappointment.
- **Advantages.** IP: drinking as procedure (rebellion, reassurance, craving); "Alcoholic" as game (self-castigation, probably). EP: avoidance of sexual and other intimacy. IS: See if you can stop me. ES: "Morning After", "Martini". Bio: alternating loving and angry exchanges. Ex: Everybody wants to deprive me.

## Debtor

"More than a game": in America it tends to become a script. The New Guinea bride price becomes a house price, and the celebration comes when the debt is undertaken, not discharged. Once paid off, a man becomes a "senior citizen" in need of a new "purpose", like the sow bug that has a purpose only while struggling off its back. Most people play a mild "If It Weren't for the Debts"; only a few make a career of hard "Debtor".

**"Try and Collect" (TAC)** shows how a game is set up so that the player "wins" whichever way it goes. If the creditor gives up after soft efforts, White enjoys the goods free. If he tries harder, White enjoys the chase as well. If he is determined and turns coercive (the employer contacted, the garish COLLECTION AGENCY truck, the "third letter": "If you do not appear at our office within 48 hours. . . ."), White switches to a variant of NIGYSOB. He feels justified in anger, proves the creditor greedy, ruthless and untrustworthy, strengthens his position ("All creditors are grasping") and abuses the creditor to friends while remaining a "Good Joe": "why pay anybody?" **"Try and Get Away With It" (TAGAWI)** is the creditor's form, played by some small landlords. TAC and TAGAWI players recognize each other, are secretly pleased, and both end well placed for "Why Does This Always Happen To Me?" The descriptions sound facetious only because they expose "trivial motivations behind matters people are taught to take seriously."

**Antithesis.** Against TAC, request immediate payment in cash (good players get around even that). Against TAGAWI, promptness and honesty. Amateurs rarely beat these professionals, but an amateur victim who laughs at the outcome disconcerts them more than anything; in financial circles laughing is "strictly Out". Laughing at a debtor on the street bewilders him as anti-"Schlemiel" bewilders a Schlemiel.

## Kick Me

Played by men "whose social manner is equivalent to wearing a sign that reads 'Please Don't Kick Me.'" The temptation is almost irresistible. When it is yielded to, White cries, "But the sign says '*don't* kick me,'" and adds, "Why does this always happen to me?" (WAHM). In "Psychiatry" form: "Whenever I'm under stress, I get all shook up." WAHM carries inverse pride, "My misfortunes are better than yours" (often found in paranoids). If kindness, convention or rules restrain people from kicking, he grows more provocative until they oblige: the cast-out, the jilted, the job losers.

- **"Threadbare"** is the women's version. Often genteel, they take pains to be shabby and keep earnings near subsistence, and enterprising young men help them lose any windfall. Called "Mother's Friend", always ready with judicious Parental advice and living vicariously, she plays a silent WAHM in her demeanor of brave struggle.
- Among the well-adapted who reap unexpected success, WAHM can become constructive: "What did I really do to deserve this?"

## Now I've Got You, You Son of a Bitch (NIGYSOB)

The poker player with four aces cares more that Black is at his mercy than about good poker or money. White agreed a price with a plumber, no extras. The bill came about four dollars over on a four-hundred-dollar job, for an unexpected valve. White phoned in fury, wrote a long letter attacking the plumber's integrity and ethics, and refused to pay until the plumber gave in. On the surface it was an Adult-Adult business dispute. Psychologically it was Parent to Adult: White exploited a trivial but socially defensible objection to vent the pent-up furies of many years, "just as his mother might have done". He had been secretly delighted at the provocation. Since childhood he had sought such injustices, and he forgot the provocations while remembering every battle. The plumber was playing a variant of WAHM: the two had recognized each other's potentials during negotiation.

- **Distinguish** NIGYSOB (two-handed) from **"Ain't It Awful?"**, in which the agent seeks injustices to complain about to a third party: Aggressor, Victim, Confidant, under the slogan "Misery Loves Company". WAHM is also three-handed but seeks pre-eminence in misfortune and resents competition. Professionally, NIGYSOB becomes the "badger game".
- **Antithesis.** "The best antithesis is correct behavior." State the contractual structure explicitly and in detail at the first opportunity, and adhere to the rules strictly: in practice, settle payment for missed appointments at once and take extra care with bookkeeping. If an unforeseen contretemps arises, "yield gracefully without dispute" until ready to deal with the game. Business with such players is always a calculated risk. Treat the player's wife with polite correctness and avoid even the mildest flirtation, gallantry or slight, especially if he seems to encourage it.
- **Protocol.** Thesis: Now I've got you, you son of a bitch. Aim: justification. Roles: *Victim*, Aggressor. Dynamics: jealous rage. Examples: (1) I caught you this time. (2) Jealous husband. Social (Adult-Adult): "See, you have done wrong." / "Now that you draw it to my attention, I guess I have." Psychological (Parent-Child): "I've been watching you, hoping you'd make a slip." / "You caught me this time." / "Yes, and I'm going to let you feel the full force of my fury." Moves: (1) Provocation-Accusation. (2) Defense-Accusation. (3) Defense-Punishment.
- **Advantages.** IP: justification for rage. EP: avoids confrontation of own deficiencies. IS: NIGYSOB. ES: they're always out to get you. Bio: belligerent exchanges, usually ipsisexual. Ex: people can't be trusted.

## See What You Made Me Do (SWYMD)

A "three-star marriage buster", also played between parents and children and at work.

- **First Degree.** White, unsociable, is engrossed in some insulating activity. An intruder comes for stroking or asks "Where can I find the long-nosed pliers?", his chisel slips, and he cries "See what you made me do." His own irritation caused the slip, and he welcomes it as a lever for ejecting the visitor. Over the years the family learns to leave him alone. Children learn it easily, so it passes down the generations.
- **Second Degree.** As a way of life, White marries a woman who plays "I'm Only Trying to Help You" and defers decisions to her "in the guise of considerateness or gallantry" (where to dine, which movie). If it goes badly: "You Got Me Into This" (UGMIT). Handing her the decisions on child-rearing sets up blaming mother if the children turn out badly, on the way to "I Told You So" or "See What You've Done Now". In occupational SWYMD a long-suffering look of resentment replaces words. The boss asks juniors for suggestions "democratically" and so gains an unassailable position for terrorizing them. Used against seniors it is self-destructive (fired, transferred): a component of WAHM for the resentful or "There I Go Again" for depressives, both of the "Kick Me" family.
- **Third Degree.** Played by paranoids against people incautious enough to give them advice: dangerous, rarely fatal.
- **The SWYMD-UGMIT marriage contract.** Mrs. White kept the books because Mr. White was "poor at figures". Every few months an overdraft revealed an undisclosed purchase; he played UGMIT and she tearfully promised it would not happen again. Then a creditor's agent appeared with a long-overdue bill, and she played SWYMD: since he had forbidden overdrafts, she had to hide the duns. This went on ten years, "on the basis that each occurrence would be the last". The remedy, which Mr. White devised himself: all accounts in his name; she kept the books and wrote the checks, but he saw the bills first and controlled payments. Deprived of the game, they were at first at a loss, then found "more open and constructive types of gratification".
- **Antithesis.** First Degree: leave the player alone (he may feel forlorn, seldom angry). Second Degree: throw the decision back on White (he may sulk at being forced to take the initiative). Third Degree: put it into competent professional hands.
- **Partial analysis.** Aim: vindication. Dynamics: the mild form relates to premature ejaculation, the hard form to rage based on "castration" anxiety. EP (prominent): avoidance of responsibility. It is often precipitated by the threat of impending intimacy, since "justifiable" anger excuses avoiding sex. Ex: I am blameless.

# Marital Games

Almost any game can scaffold married life. These evolve their fullest forms there.

## Corner

"A disingenuous refusal to play the game of another", showing more clearly than most games their manipulative function as barriers to intimacy.

1. Mrs. White proposes a movie; Mr. White agrees.
2. (a) She "unconsciously" mentions that the house needs painting, though he has just asked her not to raise unusual expenditures before the new month; or (b) he steers the talk to the house. He responds rudely.
3. She says if he is in one of his bad moods she won't go, he had best go alone; he says if that's how she feels, he will.
4. He goes to the movie (or out with the boys); she stays home nursing injured feelings.

Gimmick A: she knows she is not supposed to take his annoyance seriously; he wants appreciation for how hard he works. She refuses, and stays home looking abused but secretly triumphant. Gimmick B: he knows she wants to be "honeyed out of it" and pretends not to; he leaves cheerful but looking wronged. The winner is irreproachable: "all he or she has done is take the other literally." "Since she said it, she is cornered." The gain is external psychological: movies stimulate them both and lovemaking would follow, so whoever wants to avoid intimacy sets up move 2. It is an exasperating variety of "Uproar".

- **Antithesis.** For her it is simple: change her mind, take his arm, smile and go along (a shift from Child to Adult). For him it is harder, since she has the initiative, but he may coax her along, as a placated sulky Child or better as an Adult.
- **Family forms.** The child's version resembles Bateson's "double-bind": whatever the child does is wrong. Schizophrenia "may be a child's antithesis to 'Corner'", and analyzing the family game to show that the schizophrenic behavior was undertaken to counter it brings partial or total remission in a prepared patient. In the **Dilemma Type** the child is urged to help, then faulted for how he helps ("damned if you do and damned if you don't"). In the **Asthma Type**: "Mommy, do you love me?" / "What is love?" The child cannot follow mother into philosophy, breathes hard, asthma sets in, mother apologizes. In the **Russell-Whitehead Type**, in groups: "Silence itself may be a game." / "But not playing games may itself be a game." Its antithesis: logical paradoxes are forbidden, and the underlying anxieties surface.
- **"Lunch Bag"** (allied to "Corner" and "Threadbare"): the well-off husband brings sandwiches from leftovers in a saved paper bag, which gives him complete control of the finances ("what wife would dare buy herself a mink stole in the face of such self-sacrifice?"). It is a constructive game Benjamin Franklin would approve.

## Courtroom

At home in the law ("Wooden Leg" is the insanity plea, "Debtor" the civil suit), and clinically in marital counseling. Some marital groups are a perpetual "Courtroom" in which nothing is resolved, the counselor heavily involved without knowing it. It is three-handed: plaintiff (husband), defendant (wife) and judge (therapist), with the group or audience as jury. "Let me tell you what (wife's name) did yesterday. . . ." / "Here is the way it really was . . . and besides just before that he was . . ." / "Well, I'm glad you people have a chance to hear both sides of the story, I only want to be fair." / "It seems to me that if we consider . . ." The game element: "while the plaintiff is overtly clamoring for victory, fundamentally he believes that he is wrong."

- **Antithesis.** Tell the husband "You're absolutely right!" If he relaxes triumphantly, ask "How do you feel about my saying that?" ("Fine."), then: "Actually, I feel you're in the wrong." An honest man replies "I knew that all along"; a dishonest one reacts in a way that shows a game is in progress. Once enough material is gathered, interdict the game with "one of the most elegant [maneuvers] in the whole art of antithetics": a rule prohibiting the grammatical third person in the group. Members may only say "you" or "I", never "Let me tell you about him." The couple then stop playing, or shift to "Sweetheart" (an improvement) or "Furthermore" (no help).
- **"Furthermore"**: the plaintiff lays one accusation after another, the defendant answers each with "I can explain", and the plaintiff ignores the explanation and launches the next "furthermore": a Parent-Child interchange. Paranoid defendants play it hardest; their literalness frustrates accusers who speak humorously or metaphorically. "Metaphors are the most obvious traps to avoid in a game of 'Furthermore.'"
- **Everyday form**: two siblings and a parent. "Mommy, she took my candy away." / "Yes, but he took my doll, and before that he was hitting me, and anyway we both promised to share our candy."
- **Protocol.** Thesis: They've got to say I'm right. Aim: reassurance. Roles: *Plaintiff*, Defendant, Judge (and/or Jury). Dynamics: sibling rivalry. Examples: (1) children quarreling, parent intervenes; (2) married couple seeking "help". Social (Adult-Adult): "This is what she did to me." / "The real facts are these." Psychological (Child-Parent): "Tell *me* I'm right." / "This one is right." or "You're both right." Moves: (1) Complaint filed-Defense filed. (2) Plaintiff files rebuttal, concession or good-will gesture. (3) Decision of judge or instructions to jury. (4) Final decision filed.
- **Advantages.** IP: projection of guilt. EP: excused from guilt. IS: "Sweetheart", "Furthermore", "Uproar" and others. ES: "Courtroom". Bio: stroking from judge and jury. Ex: depressive position, I'm always wrong.

## Frigid Woman

Almost always marital. The husband's advances are repulsed: all men are beasts, he doesn't love her for herself, all he wants is sex. He desists, tries again, and finally resigns himself. She then grows "increasingly informal and sometimes forgetful": she walks through the bedroom half dressed, forgets her towel in the bath, perhaps flirts at parties. He responds and is repulsed again, and "Uproar" follows (their behavior, other couples, in-laws, finances, failures), ending in a slammed door. He resolves on a sexless *modus vivendi* and resists the negligee parade, until one evening she kisses him. At the critical point she steps back: "See, what did I tell you! All men are beasts, all I wanted was affection, but all you are interested in is sex!" This time "Uproar" goes straight to finances. The husband is "usually just as afraid of sexual intimacy as his wife is" and chose her to spare his disturbed potency, which he can now blame on her. The everyday form, played by unmarried women, merges into "Rapo".

- **Crux and antithesis.** The crucial point is the terminal "Uproar": once it has run, both have a perverse satisfaction and need no sexual excitement from each other. "Hence the most important item in anti-'Frigid Woman' is to decline 'Uproar.'" That leaves her sexually dissatisfied and perhaps more compliant. The other antitheses are dangerous. A mistress is a gamble (she may reform, or use the affair, with a lawyer, for NIGYSOB). The husband's therapy alone may lead to a healthier adjustment or, with a hard player, to divorce. Best: both spouses in a transactional marital group, then individual therapy, which may yield "a psychological remarriage". For the everyday form, the decent antithesis is to find another social companion.
- **Relatives.** "Frigid Man" is the converse, less common. In "Beat Me Daddy", "Uproar" is foreplay, a fetish; in "Frigid Woman" it substitutes for the sex act. The childhood analogue is Dickens's prissy girl in a starched dress who asks a boy for a mud pie, then sneers at his dirty hands.
- **Protocol.** Thesis: Now I've got you, you son of a bitch. Aim: vindication. Roles: *Proper Wife*, Inconsiderate Husband. Dynamics: penis envy. Examples: (1) Thank you for the mud pie, you dirty little boy. (2) Provocative, frigid wife. Social (Parent-Child): "I give you permission to make me a mud pie (kiss me)." / "I'd love to." / "Now see how dirty you are." Psychological (Child-Parent): "See if you can seduce me." / "I'll try, if you stop me." / "See, it was you who started it." Moves: (1) Seduction-Response. (2) Rejection-Resignation. (3) Provocation-Response. (4) Rejection-Uproar.
- **Advantages.** IP: freedom from guilt for sadistic fantasies. EP: avoids feared exhibition and penetration. IS: "Uproar". ES: What do you do with dirty little boys (husbands)? Bio: inhibited sex play and belligerent exchanges. Ex: I am pure.

## Harried

The housewife must fill ten or twelve conflicting roles (mistress, mother, nurse, housemaid...). The years bring "Housewife's Knee", summed up in "I'm tired." If she sets her own pace and finds satisfaction in loving her family, she enjoys her twenty-five years. If she is driven by her inner Parent and called to account by a critical husband chosen for the purpose, she falls back on "If It Weren't For You" and "Blemish", and when those fail she plays "Harried". She takes on everything and asks for more: agrees with every criticism, accepts every demand, and on a dinner-party day volunteers to bake a cake and take the children to the dentist as well. In mid-afternoon she justifiably collapses and lets everyone down, and self-reproach adds to her misery. After two or three rounds the marriage is in jeopardy, and she appears at the psychiatrist's ready to be hospitalized.

- **Antithesis.** "Mrs. White can fill each of her roles in succession during the week, but she must refuse to play two or more of them simultaneously": at a cocktail party, caterer or nursemaid, not both. That suffices for mere Housewife's Knee. In the true game the husband was chosen to criticize her for not matching his mother. She "marries his fantasy of his mother as perpetuated in his Parent", which matches her own fantasy of her mother or grandmother, and the more responsible his job, the more Adult reasons both find to keep the game.
- **In treatment** the psychiatrist is drawn in as third hand, wanted either to overhaul the wife or as her ally against the husband. Alleviating her depression goes smoothly. The decisive phase is when she gives up "Harried" for "Psychiatry", which rouses opposition from both spouses. The real culprit is the wife's Parent (mother or grandmother); the husband is partly a lay figure. The therapist fights that Parent, the husband and a social environment that encourages compliance: the Sunday paper's "How Good A Hostess (Wife) (Mother) . . . Are You?" test is the rules leaflet for "Harried". Unchecked, it ends in "State Hospital" ("The last thing I want is to be sent to a hospital"). The husband, more disturbed than he admits, plays "Look How Hard I'm Trying" and sends messages through temper outbursts he knows will be reported. The game can progress to a third-degree life-death-divorce struggle: the psychiatrist and the patient's harried Adult against all three aspects of the husband allied with her own Parent and Child, "odds of two against five". A therapist who quails offers the patient to the divorce court, saying in effect "I surrender—Let's you and him fight."

## If It Weren't For You (IWFY)

The second game uncovered, after "Why Don't You—Yes But". Its discovery showed "there must be a whole department of social action based on ulterior transactions." A woman marries a domineering man so that he will restrict her and keep her from frightening situations. As a simple operation she would thank him; in IWFY she complains of the restrictions instead. The full protocol is the worked example in SKILL.md.

## Look How Hard I've Tried (LHHIT)

**Clinical form**, three-handed with a psychiatrist. The husband (usually) is "bucking for a divorce, despite loud protestations to the contrary". He comes under protest, talks just enough to show cooperation (a mild "Psychiatry" or "Courtroom"), and grows resentfully pseudocompliant or belligerently argumentative. At home he is at first more "understanding", then worse than ever. After one, five or ten visits he quits and goes hunting or fishing. The wife is forced to file, and he tells attorney, judge and relatives, "Look how hard I've tried!"

- **Antithesis.** See the couple together. If one is clearly playing, take the other into individual treatment and send the player on his way, "on the valid ground that he is less ready for therapy." He can still divorce, but only by abandoning his position that he is really trying. The hoped-for outcome: his game broken, he despairs and seeks treatment elsewhere with genuine motivation.
- **Everyday form**: a child with one parent, played from "I am helpless" or "I am blameless". The child tries and bungles: if Helpless, the parent does it for him; if Blameless, the parent has no grounds to punish. The parents should find out "which of them taught the child this game; and what they are doing to perpetuate it."
- **"Look How Hard I Was Trying"**, the ulcer variant, is harder.
  - First Degree: he announces his ulcer and keeps working. He deserves credit for not playing "Wooden Leg", and the courteous reply is "Yes, we all admire your fortitude and conscientiousness."
  - Second Degree: he keeps the ulcer secret until he collapses on the job. His wife gets the message instantly: now she must love him. Her solicitude comes from guilt, she resents the unfair leverage, and she feels trapped rather than won: "a diamond bracelet is a much more honest instrument of courtship than a perforated stomach." The plan flashes through his mind when he first hears the diagnosis, and a careful review recovers "the secret gloating of his Child at learning that he has such a weapon, masked by his Adult concern".
  - Third Degree: unheralded suicide over a serious illness, with the note in effect saying "Look How Hard I Was Trying." "If something like this happens twice to the same woman, it is time for her to find out what she has been playing."
- **Protocol.** Thesis: They can't push me around. Aim: vindication. Roles: *Standfast*, Persecutor, Authority. Dynamics: anal passivity. Examples: (1) child dressing; (2) spouse bucking for divorce. Social (Adult-Adult): "It's time to (get dressed) (go to a psychiatrist)." / "All right, I'll try it." Psychological (Parent-Child): "I'm going to make you (get dressed) (go to a psychiatrist)." / "See, it doesn't work." Moves: (1) Suggestion-Resistance. (2) Pressure-Compliance. (3) Approval-Failure.
- **Advantages.** IP: freedom from guilt for aggression. EP: evades domestic responsibilities. IS and ES: Look how hard I've tried. Bio: belligerent exchanges. Ex: I am helpless (blameless).

## Sweetheart

Next to "Courtroom", the commonest game in marital groups, and seen on social occasions. White tells a subtly derogatory anecdote about his wife and ends, "Isn't that right, sweetheart?" She agrees for two ostensibly Adult reasons: (a) the anecdote is in the main accurate, and disputing what is presented as a peripheral detail (really the essential point) would seem pedantic; (b) it would seem surly to contradict a man who calls one "sweetheart" in public. Her real reason is her depressive position: she married him to expose her deficiencies for her, as her parents did. The nearer the game comes to exposure, the more bitterly "sweetheart" is pronounced. It is a relative of "Schlemiel": the significant move is her implicit forgiveness of a resentment she is trying hard not to notice.

- **Antithesis.** "You can tell derogatory anecdotes about me, but please don't call me 'sweetheart.'" This carries the perils of anti-"Schlemiel". A more sophisticated, less dangerous reply: "Yes, *honey!*"
- **Variants.** The wife answers with a matching anecdote ("You have a dirty face too, dear"). In "Sweetheart", Silent Type, the endearment goes unspoken but a careful listener hears it.
