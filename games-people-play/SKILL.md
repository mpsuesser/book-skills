# Games People Play (Eric Berne)

Game analysis is a special aspect of transactional analysis, which is a branch of social psychiatry. It passes judgment on the "healthiness" of what it studies: psychiatry "reserves the right to say, 'Just a moment!'" Work from the transaction outward: diagnose the ego states, name the transactions, decide whether the sequence is a game, then name its payoff and its antithesis.

## Strokes and the three hungers

- **Stimulus-hunger**: infants deprived of handling decline and may die; adults in sensory deprivation break down. Colloquially: "If you are not stroked, your spinal cord will shrivel up."
- **Recognition-hunger**: the grown-up compromise, in which subtler, symbolic handling stands in for physical contact, "until the merest nod of recognition may serve the purpose." Appetites differ: a movie actor may need hundreds of strokes a week from anonymous admirers, a scientist one stroke a year from a respected master.
- **Structure-hunger**: the need to avoid boredom, the "eternal problem" of how to structure waking hours ("What do you say to her (him) then?"). Persistent boredom amounts to emotional starvation.
- A **stroke** is any act implying recognition of another's presence, the fundamental unit of social action. An exchange of strokes is a **transaction**, the unit of social intercourse. The principle on which the theory of games rests: "any social intercourse whatever has a biological advantage over no intercourse at all." In Levine's rats, gentle handling and painful electric shocks were equally effective in promoting health.
- **Strokes are kept as accounts.** The American greeting ritual ("Hi!" "Hi!" "Warm enough forya?" "Sure is. Looks like rain, though." ...) is an "eight-stroke ritual": two acquaintances owe each other exactly four strokes per meeting, not oftener than once a day. After a month's vacation about thirty are owed, and a bare "Hi!" offends ("his spinal cord will shrivel slightly"); the debt is paid in a few emphatic transactions. Giving five strokes where one is owed provokes suspicion ("Is he selling insurance or something?"); returning none provokes "What's the matter with him?" Read any slight, gush or snub through this ledger.

## Time structuring

The solitary person structures time by **activity** and **fantasy**. In company of two or more the options, in order of complexity, are (1) **rituals**, (2) **pastimes**, (3) **games**, (4) **intimacy**, with (5) **activity** forming a matrix for any of the others. Programing comes from three sources: **material** (Adult, external reality), **social** (Parent, "good manners") and **individual** (Child, idiosyncrasy).

- **Activity**: a project dealing with the material of external reality, commonly called work.
- **Procedure**: a series of simple complementary Adult transactions directed toward the manipulation of reality (piloting a plane, removing an appendix). It is **efficient** when the agent makes the best possible use of the data and experience available to him, and **contaminated** (less efficient) when Parent or Child interferes; it is **effective** by its actual results. Efficiency is a psychological criterion, effectiveness a material one.
- **Ritual**: a stereotyped series of simple complementary transactions programed by external social forces. Procedures are programed by the Adult; rituals are Parentally patterned. People uncomfortable with rituals evade them by substituting procedures (helping the hostess serve).
- **Pastime**: a series of semiritualistic, simple, complementary transactions arranged around a single field of material, whose primary object is to structure an interval of time (see Pastimes below).
- **Operation**: a simple transaction or set of transactions undertaken for a specific, stated purpose. Frankly asking for reassurance and getting it is an operation.
- **Game**: individually programed sequences that look adventitious but follow definite patterns and unspoken rules, which "become manifest if an illegal move is made, giving rise to a symbolic, verbal or legal cry of 'Foul!'" (definition below).
- **Intimacy**: begins when individual (usually instinctual) programing intensifies and both social patterning and ulterior motives give way; the only completely satisfying answer to the three hungers. "Pastimes and games are substitutes for the real living of real intimacy."

Games are sandwiched between pastimes and intimacy. Pastimes grow boring; intimacy is discriminated against by all three ego states (society frowns on candidness except in privacy, good sense knows it can be abused, the Child fears the unmasking). So most people compromise for games, which fill the more interesting hours of social life. Because so little intimacy is available, "games are both necessary and desirable, and the only problem at issue is whether the games played by an individual offer the best yield for him."

## Ego states (structural analysis)

An **ego state** is "a system of feelings accompanied by a related set of behavior patterns": not roles but psychological realities, revealed by noticeable shifts in posture, viewpoint, voice, vocabulary, gestures and feeling. Capitalized Parent, Adult and Child are ego states; lowercase parent, adult, child are actual people.

- **Parent** (exteropsychic), resembling parental figures. "That is your Parent" means: you are in the same state of mind as one of your parents used to be, responding as he would, with the same posture, gestures, vocabulary, feelings. It appears **directly** as an active ego state ("Do as I do": he becomes one of them) or **indirectly** as an influence ("Don't do as I do, do as I say": he adapts to their requirements). Its value: it parents actual children, and it makes routine responses automatic ("That's the way it's done"), freeing the Adult.
- **Adult** (neopsychic), autonomously directed toward objective appraisal of reality. "That is your Adult" means: you have just made an autonomous, objective appraisal and are stating it in a non-prejudicial manner. It processes data and computes probabilities (crossing a busy highway), and regulates and mediates between Parent and Child. "Everyone has an Adult", children and schizophrenics included.
- **Child** (archaeopsychic), archaic relics fixated in early childhood. "That is your Child" means: the manner and intent of your reaction is the same as when you were a very little boy or girl. The **adapted Child** modifies behavior under Parental influence (compliantly, precociously, by withdrawing or whining; the Parental influence is the cause, the adapted Child the effect). The **natural Child** is spontaneous expression: rebellion or creativity. Alcohol decommissions the Parent first, releasing the adapted Child into the natural Child. The Child holds intuition, creativity and spontaneous drive and enjoyment; it is "in many ways the most valuable part of the personality."
- **The Professor**: the Adult aspect of the Child ego state, whose psychological aptitude is "so striking, and its skill in manipulating people so enviable". It set up the person's games between the ages of two and eight. In game-analysis groups, search for the little Professor in each patient: once he can tell its early adventures with the group's hilarity and "justifiable self-appreciation and smugness", "he is well on his way to relinquishing" the pattern.

Say **childlike**, never "childish". There is no "immature person", only people in whom the Child takes over inappropriately or unproductively, all of whom have a complete Adult needing only to be uncovered or activated. All three ego states are entitled to equal respect; analysis is indicated only when one disturbs the healthy balance.

## Transactions

The **transactional stimulus** is the opening acknowledgment; the **transactional response** answers it. Simple transactional analysis diagnoses which ego state implemented the stimulus and which executed the response.

- **Complementary**: the response is appropriate and expected (scalpel handed Adult to Adult; fevered child asks, nurturing mother brings water, Child to Parent). **First rule of communication**: communication proceeds smoothly as long as transactions are complementary, and can in principle proceed indefinitely, whatever the content (Parent-Parent critical gossip, Adult-Adult problem solving, Child-Child or Parent-Child play). The rule depends entirely on the direction of the vectors.
- **Crossed**: communication is broken off. **Crossed Transaction Type I** (the classical transference reaction), the cause of most social difficulties in marriage, love, friendship and work: an Adult stimulus ("Do you know where my cuff links are?") gets a Child-to-Parent response ("You always blame me for everything") instead of "On the desk." **Type II** (counter-transference): the Adult stimulus gets a Parent-to-Child response ("Why don't you keep track of your own things? You're not a child any more."). The Adult subject is suspended until the vectors are realigned, which takes seconds for cuff links and months for a drinking problem. Realign by complementing the activated ego state (agent becomes Parental to the respondent's Child) or by reactivating the respondent's Adult. If the maid rebels during a talk about dishes, the dish conversation is over; what remains is Child-Parent discourse or a different Adult subject, her continued employment.
- When both parties come at each other Parent-to-Child at once, the result is two speechless people glaring. If neither yields they must part; one yielding produces a game of "Uproar"; better, both shift Adult-Adult, "in which case they both burst out laughing or shake hands."
- **Ulterior**: involves more than two ego states at once; the basis for games. Every ulterior transaction runs on a **social level** (ostensible) and a **psychological level** (ulterior). **Angular** (three ego states), the salesman's specialty: "This one is better, but you can't afford it." / "That's the one I'll take." The salesman's Adult aims the psychological vector at the housewife's Child ("I'll show that arrogant fellow I'm as good as any of his customers"). **Duplex** (four ego states), as in flirtation: "Come and see the barn." / "I've loved barns ever since I was a little girl." Adult talk about barns, Child talk about sex play. "As in most games, the outcome is determined by the Child."
- A **superficial relationship** is one confined to simple complementary transactions.

## Pastimes

Pastimes fill parties and the waiting period before a meeting "begins". Classify them sociologically ("Man Talk": "General Motors", "Who Won"; "Lady Talk": "Kitchen", "Wardrobe", "Grocery"; "Making Out" for adolescents; "Balance Sheet" at middle age; "How To", "How Much", "Ever Been", "Do You Know", "What Became", "Morning After", "Martini"), structurally (one pastime at different levels: "PTA" Child-Child is "How do You Deal with Recalcitrant Parents", Adult-Adult is "PTA" proper, Parent-Parent is "Juvenile Delinquency"; "Tell Them Dear"; "Look Ma No Hands"), or psychologically as **projective** ("There wouldn't be all this delinquency if it weren't for broken homes") or **introjective** ("I just don't seem to have what it takes to be a mother").

- Pastimes are **social-selection processes**: while one runs, the Child in each player watchfully assesses the others and selects the likely candidates for more complex relationships, that is, games. The insurance salesman's Adult overrides this and selects by financial readiness.
- Pastimes are **exclusive**: a newcomer who plays "Sunny Side Up" (her husband is marvelous) at a coffee klatsch playing "Delinquent Husband" will not be kept long. To join a group, join its pastime or switch the whole proceeding; a good hostess states the program ("We were just playing Projective 'PTA.' What do you think?").
- Pastimes confirm **role** (like Jung's persona, but less opportunistic and more deeply rooted in fantasy: tough, righteous, indulgent or helpful Parent in "PTA") and stabilize **position**: "a simple predicative statement which influences all of the individual's transactions; in the long run it determines his destiny and often that of his descendants." "All children are bad!" "All other children are bad!" Positions are fixed between the first or second and the seventh year of life, and the rest of life goes to stabilizing them: avoiding threatening situations, warding off certain elements, or manipulating them provocatively "so that they are transformed from threats into justifications." A position shows itself as an **attitude**.

## Games

A **game** is "an ongoing series of complementary ulterior transactions progressing to a well-defined, predictable outcome." Descriptively, "a recurring set of transactions, often repetitious, superficially plausible, with a concealed motivation; or, more colloquially, a series of moves with a snare, or 'gimmick.'"

- **Two marks separate a game** from procedures, rituals and pastimes: (1) its **ulterior** quality and (2) the **payoff**. Those others are candid; they may involve contest but not conflict, and their endings may be sensational but not dramatic. "Every game, on the other hand, is basically dishonest, and the outcome has a dramatic, as distinct from merely exciting, quality."
- **Operation or maneuver?** Ask for reassurance and get it: operation. Ask for reassurance and turn it "to the disadvantage of the giver": game. After the payoff the apparent operations prove to have been **maneuvers**, moves in the game rather than honest requests.
- **Position is not a game.** "A game is played from a position, but a position or its corresponding attitude is not a game." Games are dynamic; attitudes are static. Keep the term precise: distinguish games from procedures, rituals, pastimes, operations, maneuvers and attitudes.
- **Angular and duplex games.** Con games are angular, consciously planned under Adult control for maximum gain. The games that matter are "the unconscious games played by innocent people engaged in duplex transactions of which they are not fully aware."
- **"Game" does not imply fun.** "The essential characteristic of human play is not that the emotions are spurious, but that they are regulated." Calling suicide, addiction, criminality or schizophrenia "playing games" is not irresponsible or facetious.
- **The payoff is the culmination**, and the preliminary moves set it up while harvesting "the maximum permissible satisfaction at each step as a secondary product." In "Schlemiel" the spilling is pleasure but the payoff is the forgiveness forced by the apology ("The apology is the critical stimulus that leads to the denouement"). In "Alcoholic" the drinking is incidental; the payoff is the hangover's self-castigation, proven by "Dry Alcoholic", which runs the same moves to the same payoff with no bottle. The payoff "comes from the aspect to which most investigators pay least attention." Look there first.
- **The one who starts wins either way.** In "Schlemiel", the host's anger justifies White's resentment and his restraint lets White carry on. In "Try and Collect", the creditor who gives up and the creditor who chases both pay off.
- **Roles switch.** Players "can and will play either side of the game with equal facility": the Alcoholic becomes the Rescuer, the "Why Don't You—Yes But" help-seeker becomes the suggestion-giver at home.
- **Beautiful friendships** form when players complement each other "with great economy and satisfaction": wasteful moves are eliminated, precautionary moves elided, ornamental flourishes added. People pick friends, associates and intimates who play the same games; whoever changes his games tends to be extruded from his circle and finds a welcome in another.

**Genesis.** Child rearing is "an educational process in which the child is taught what games to play and how to play them." Procedures, rituals and pastimes determine a person's opportunities; his games determine what he does with them. Games are imitative, deliberately initiated by young children and set up by the Professor. Tanjy, 7, had a stomach-ache and was excused; Mike, 3, said "I have a stomach-ache too." Father: "You don't want to play that game, do you?" Mike laughed: "No!" A game *in statu nascendi* was broken up by the father's question and the boy's frank acknowledgment; repeated with cooperating parents, it would have become a hypochondriacal career ("I don't feel well" + "You must grant me a privilege, too"). Favored games pass down five or more generations, and people "inbreed" with players of the same family of games. "Breaking this chain which involves five or more generations may have geometrically progressive effects."

**Script.** A person's favored games are elements of his **script**, an unconscious life-plan, which determines his destiny: the payoffs on his marriage and career and the circumstances of his death. Games fill in time while he waits for the last act, which calls for a miracle or a catastrophe. "Waiting for Santa Claus" goes with pleasant games like "Gee You're Wonderful, Mr. Murgatroyd"; "waiting for *rigor mortis* to set in" goes with "Now I've Got You, You Son of a Bitch". In practice the two waits are synonymous.

**Degrees and intensity.** A **First-Degree Game** is socially acceptable in the agent's circle. A **Second-Degree Game** does no permanent, irremediable damage, but the players would rather conceal it from the public. A **Third-Degree Game** "is one which is played for keeps, and which ends in the surgery, the courtroom or the morgue." Three further variables: **flexibility** (how many currencies it can use: words, money, parts of the body), **tenacity** (how readily it is given up) and **intensity** (relaxed **easy** games versus tense, aggressive **hard** games). Together these make a game gentle or violent, and disturbed players progress through the stages. Games are also classified by number of players (two-, three-, five-, many-handed), clinical type, zone, psychodynamics and instinct; the thesaurus groups them by the situations they occur in.

## Analyzing a game

Run these in order. Done when every heading of the protocol is filled and the antithesis is named and, where possible, tested.

1. **Establish that it is a game.** Look for the ulterior quality (a social and a psychological level that diverge) and the payoff (the dramatic culmination the sequence was set up to reach: the forgiveness, the morning-after scolding, the crestfallen silence). Rule out a candid procedure, ritual, pastime or operation, and a static attitude. A cry of "Foul!" when someone breaks the unspoken rules is a sign.
2. **Find who is "it".** The analysis is written from the point of view of the agent, called **White**; the other party is **Black**.
3. **Write the critical transaction at both levels** and diagnose each ego state. The bare transcript may sound Adult; "in the living tissue" the shifts show in posture, muscular tone, voice and vocabulary.
4. **Name the game** from the index below and read its entry. What looks like a known game may prove a new one, and an apparent new one a variation. For a genuinely new game, collect as many samples as possible, isolate the significant features and classify the essentials under the headings.
5. **Fill the protocol.**
   - **Thesis**: the general description, at the social and the psychological level.
   - **Aim**: its general purpose (reassurance, vindication, absolution...).
   - **Roles**: the number of hands, "it" listed first. Ego states are not roles.
   - **Dynamics**: the single psychodynamic concept that best epitomizes it.
   - **Examples**: (1) the childhood prototype, (2) the adult form.
   - **Paradigm**: the critical transaction, social and psychological.
   - **Moves**: the minimum number of stimuli and responses; players embellish them without limit.
   - **Advantages**, the six:
     1. *Internal psychological*: what the game does for internal psychic stability.
     2. *External psychological*: which anxiety-arousing situations or intimacies it avoids.
     3. *Internal social*: the characteristic phrase used with intimates.
     4. *External social*: the key phrase of the derivative game or pastime played in less intimate circles.
     5. *Biological*: the kind of stroking it yields.
     6. *Existential*: the position it is played from.
   - **Relatives**: complementary, allied and antithetical games.
   - Also state the **degree**.
6. **Validate by antithesis.** "The presumption that a certain sequence constitutes a game is tentative until it has been existentially validated", by a refusal to play or by undercutting the payoff. The player who is "it" then makes more intense efforts. Met with adamant refusal, he lapses into **despair**: more acute than depression, full of frustration and bewilderment, perhaps perplexed weeping. In a good outcome humorous laughter replaces it, the Adult realizing "There I go again!" "Despair is a concern of the Adult, while in depression it is the Child who has the executive power." Laughter is the opposite of despair; hopefulness, enthusiasm or a lively interest in one's surroundings is the opposite of depression.

**Worked example: "If It Weren't For You" (IWFY), Marital Type**, the commonest game between spouses.

- **Thesis.** Mrs. White complained that her husband restricted her social life. When he grew indulgent, she enrolled in dancing classes and discovered a morbid fear of dance floors. Out of many suitors her Child had "shrewdly chosen" a domineering man, who kept her from what she feared and from even knowing she feared it.
- **Antithesis.** Permissiveness: "Go ahead!" instead of "Don't you dare!" unmasks the phobia.
- **Aim.** Reassurance ("It's not that I'm afraid, it's that he won't let me"); alternatively vindication ("It's not that I'm not trying, it's that he holds me back").
- **Roles.** Restricted wife (as prudent Adult or petulant Child); domineering husband (as Adult or Parent).
- **Dynamics.** Phobic.
- **Examples.** The child's version is the same, with the actual parent in the husband's place.
- **Paradigm.** Social, Parent-Child: "You stay home and take care of the house." / "If it weren't for you, I could be out having fun." Psychological, Child-Child, the ulterior marriage contract: "You must always be here when I get home. I'm terrified of desertion." / "I will be if you help me avoid phobic situations."
- **Moves.** (1) Instruction-Compliance ("You stay home"—"All right"). (2) Instruction-Protest ("You stay home again"—"If it weren't for you").
- **Advantages.**
  - *Internal psychological*: socially acceptable surrender keeps her from neurotic fears.
  - *External psychological*: she avoids the feared situations, the outstanding motive here.
  - *Internal social*: the privilege of saying "If it weren't for you", which structures time with a husband she has little else in common with.
  - *External social*: the pastime "If It Weren't For Him" over morning coffee. A new neighbor who declines to play is dropped.
  - *Biological*: belligerence-petulance exchanges, his backhanded slap answered by her petulant kick in the shins.
  - *Existential*: "All men are tyrants."

## Antithesis: breaking up a game

Each game's entry gives its specific antithesis. These principles run through all of them:

- **Withhold what the game needs.** Decline the role the player needs (Persecutor, Rescuer, Patsy), the absolution he demands, the advice he will reject, the "Uproar" that ends the episode. Antithesis is distinct from **reprisal**: a reprisal means White has won.
- **Take an Adult contractual position.** State the rules of the relationship explicitly and early, then keep them strictly (fees, missed appointments, bookkeeping with a "Now I've Got You" player); answer the plea with the Adult question ("I don't expect anything. The question is, what do you expect of yourself?").
- **Stop the game rather than change roles.** The cure for "Alcoholic" lies in "getting him to stop playing the game altogether, rather than simply change from one role to another"; the antithesis of "Stupid" is "not to substitute another game, but simply to refrain from playing." Even so, someone afraid of intimacy may need another game as a substitute before a game-free relationship is possible.
- **Name it and let it be acknowledged.** A well-timed question ("You don't want to play that game, do you?") and a frank acknowledgment break up a game in its infancy. The irony of an apt name can itself be the antithesis ("Greenhouse").
- **Laugh.** An amateur who laughs at the outcome disconcerts professional debtors; "experienced con men are scared of marks who laugh after they have been taken."
- **Expect escalation and switches.** The player plays harder, then switches games: rebuffed at "Rapo", Potiphar's wife made "the classical switch into 'Let's You and Him Fight.'" A Second-Degree "See What You Made Me Do" player sulks when forced to take the initiative.
- **Exercise prudence.** Some people's stability is so precarious that losing their games may plunge them into "irreversible despair and even psychosis", and one spouse's improvement can bring the other's rapid deterioration. Lay "careful preliminary groundwork" and time the move; acutely disturbed people may need to be played along with at first. Antithetical moves can earn lifelong enmity: in everyday life they "should not be initiated unless one is prepared to carry them through ruthlessly and take the consequences." A friendly, harmless game may simply be joined.
- **Expect the bystanders to resist.** Rescuers, relatives, agencies and the public often have more invested in the game than the player. Clinicians who broke up "Alcoholic" instead of rescuing were frozen out by their lay committee, and the social worker who got "Indigence" clients into jobs was reprimanded.
- **The reward makes it possible.** Even precariously balanced personalities "can safely and joyfully relinquish their games if an appropriate partner can be found for the better relationship."

## Colloquialisms

Use the games' colloquial names: they are more dynamic and "actually more precise" ("Ain't It Awful" beats "verbalizing projected anal aggression"), and players appreciate them if used "with due regard to timing and sensibilities." The irony is directed against the games, never against the people who play them. In speech, use the full name rather than the acronym. A game is usually named by its internal social phrase, what the agent says to intimates ("If it weren't for you"); coin new names by aptness.

## The players: Sulks and Jerks

The more disturbed people are, the harder they play. In everyday life two classes play with the greatest conviction.

- **The Sulk** is a man angry at his mother since early childhood, for good "Child" reasons (she "deserted" him for a hospital or a new sibling, or farmed him out to remarry). A female Sulk is angry at father ("What do you expect of a woman who had a father like that?"). Sulking was deliberate at its inception, so the decision can be reversed at any age, on the same two terms as for a little boy at dinner time: he must be able to save face, and he must be offered something worthwhile in exchange for the privilege of sulking. Clumsiness or bullying works no better than with a sulky little boy: "in the long run, the patient will pay the therapist back for his mishandling." A male therapist must handle the female Sulk's plea with even more diplomacy or be thrown "into the wastebasket of 'men who are like father.'"
- **The Jerk** (or Square) is overly sensitive to Parental influences, so his Adult data processing and his Child's spontaneity are interfered with at critical moments, producing inappropriate or clumsy behavior. In extreme cases he merges into the Toady, the Show-off and the Cling. "There is a bit of Jerk in everyone, but the object of game analysis is to keep it at a minimum."

## Beyond games: autonomy

**Autonomy** is the release or recovery of three capacities.

- **Awareness**: "the capacity to see a coffeepot and hear the birds sing in one's own way, and not the way one was taught." Once the "good father" teaches "That's a jay, and this is a sparrow", the boy can no longer see or hear the birds. Awareness requires living in the here and now. Ask "Where is the mind when the body is here?" Driving to work, the Jerk's mind is already at the office door; he plays "Look How Hard I've Tried" and is "more dead than alive". The Sulk collects excuses for lateness for his game of "Look What They Made Me Do". The natural driver is alive in his craftsmanship. The aware person will not hurry, because he is living in the present with the sky and the trees; he "knows how he feels, where he is and when it is."
- **Spontaneity**: option, "the freedom to choose and express one's feelings from the assortment available (Parent feelings, Adult feelings and Child feelings)"; liberation from the compulsion to play games and have only the feelings one was taught to have.
- **Intimacy**: "the spontaneous, game-free candidness of an aware person", the uncorrupted natural Child living in the here and now. It tends to turn out well unless games intervene, and adaptation to Parental influences is what usually spoils it. Candidness mobilizes positive feelings even one-sidedly, which professional seducers exploit.

**Project, not resolution.** A **resolution** ("This is the last time I'll be late") is an act of will power, the Parent imposed on the Child, announced before the fact, "made only to be broken": an attempt to set up a game. A **project** ("I have a new project—being on time") is an Adult decision announced after the fact. In Berne's game-free paradigm, the therapist answers "I'll try to cooperate" as an Adult contract that he keeps (he had been returning late from his coffee break), not as a "supportive" opening for "I'm Only Trying to Help You". He guesses the patient's grade ("B+... Because you're afraid to get an A") without false modesty. She faces her Child squarely ("I had an A, and I went over my paper and crossed out three correct answers and put in three wrong ones"). Each is free to indulge a pastime that both know is a pastime, with no need to point it out. Neither is obliged to play a game: she need not play "Art Gallery" with a stranger, and he need not play "Psychiatry", letting chances to raise defense, transference and symbolism go by without anxiety.

**Attaining autonomy** is the overthrow, never final, of inherited irrelevancies. Adaptation to the parents was a series of early decisions, and decisions are reversible under favorable circumstances. In order:

1. Lift the weight of the tribal or family historical tradition.
2. Throw off the individual parental, social and cultural background.
3. Do the same with the demands of contemporary society.
4. Partly or wholly sacrifice the advantages derived from one's immediate social circle.
5. Give up the easy indulgences of being a Sulk or a Jerk.
6. Attain personal and social control, so that every class of behavior except perhaps dreams becomes a free choice.

Then one is ready for game-free relationships. In essence the whole preparation is "obtaining a friendly divorce from one's parents (and from other Parental influences) so that they may be agreeably visited on occasion, but are no longer dominant." Awareness, spontaneity and intimacy "may be frightening and even perilous to the unprepared": "This may mean that there is no hope for the human race, but there is hope for individual members of it."

**Good games.** Since much time must go to games under any social order, look for "good" ones: a good game is one "whose social contribution outweighs the complexity of its motivations, particularly if the player has come to terms with those motivations without futility or cynicism", contributing to the other players' well-being and to the unfolding of the one who is "it".

- **Alcoholic**: "How bad I've been; see if you can stop me." Five-handed (Alcoholic, Persecutor, Rescuer, Patsy, Connection); payoff is the morning after. Also "Dry Alcoholic", "Addict", "Have One".
- **Debtor**: debt as a life purpose. "Try and Collect" wins whichever way the creditor goes; "Try and Get Away With It".
- **Kick Me**: wears a "Please Don't Kick Me" sign, then asks "Why does this always happen to me?" Women's form "Threadbare".
- **Now I've Got You, You Son of a Bitch (NIGYSOB)**: seizes a trivial, defensible wrong to vent years of rage. Distinct from "Ain't It Awful".
- **See What You Made Me Do (SWYMD)**: blames the intruder or the person he deferred to. Pairs with "You Got Me Into This".
- **Corner**: a disingenuous refusal to play the other's game, taking them literally to avoid intimacy. Also the double-bind, "Lunch Bag".
- **Courtroom**: plaintiff, defendant, judge, jury: "They've got to say I'm right." Also "Furthermore".
- **Frigid Woman / Frigid Man**: provoke advances, then "All men are beasts", ending in "Uproar".
- **Harried**: takes on every role at once, then collapses.
- **If It Weren't For You (IWFY)**: the worked example above.
- **Look How Hard I've Tried**: tries, fails, is blameless. Also the ulcer variant "Look How Hard I Was Trying".
- **Sweetheart**: a derogatory anecdote ending "Isn't that right, sweetheart?"

**Party, Sexual and Underworld games:** read [party-sexual-underworld-games.md](party-sexual-underworld-games.md).

- **Ain't It Awful (AIA)**: the pastimes "Nowadays", "Broken Skin" and "Water Cooler"; as a game, covert gratification from misfortune (polysurgery).
- **Blemish**: "They are no good"; uneasy until the new person's blemish is found.
- **Schlemiel**: makes messes, says "I'm sorry", gets forgiven.
- **Why Don't You—Yes But (YDYB)**: a problem presented, every solution rejected; the payoff is the silence. The first game discovered.
- **Let's You and Him Fight (LYAHF)**: sets two men fighting over her.
- **Perversion**: "What do you expect from someone as strongly sexed as I am?"
- **Rapo**: leads a man on in order to reject him. "Kiss Off", "Indignation", Third Degree.
- **Stocking Game**: "Oh my, I have a run in my stocking."
- **Uproar**: a quarrel ending in a slammed door, to avoid sexual intimacy.
- **Cops and Robbers (C&R)**: "See if you can catch me"; being caught is the payoff.
- **How Do You Get Out of Here ("Want Out")**: simulates good behavior, sabotages release. Also "You've Got to Listen", "Bum Rap".
- **Let's Pull a Fast One on Joey (FOOJY)**: the con that backfires on the mark who has larceny in his veins.

**Consulting Room and Good games:** read [consulting-room-and-good-games.md](consulting-room-and-good-games.md). These are games played by therapists, by professionally trained patients and by lay clients, plus constructive games.

- **Greenhouse**: "Genuine Feelings" exhibited like rare flowers.
- **I'm Only Trying to Help You (ITHY)**: advice given and "failed"; the payoff is bewilderment at ingratitude.
- **Indigence**: "I'll look for employment (providing I don't have to find any)." Also "Veteran", "Clinic".
- **Peasant**: "Gee you're wonderful, Professor!", and she never gets better.
- **Psychiatry**: "It says here I am a healer." Also "Archaeology", "Self-Expression", "Tell Me This".
- **Stupid**: "I laugh with you at my own clumsiness and stupidity."
- **Wooden Leg**: "What do you expect of a man with a wooden leg?" Obverse "Rickshaw".
- Good games: **Busman's Holiday**, **Cavalier**, **Happy to Help**, **Homely Sage**, **They'll Be Glad They Knew Me**.
