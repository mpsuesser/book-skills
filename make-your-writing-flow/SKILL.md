# Make Your Writing Flow

**Transitions** are words or short phrases used to connect multiple related ideas together into a coherent whole. With them a writer builds complex paragraphs that **flow** smoothly from one sentence to the next. They do two jobs:

- They let the writer express thoughts and ideas in a logical manner by connecting one idea to the next.
- Used correctly, they help the reader move effortlessly through the text without feeling **jarred**.

Stringing thoughts and ideas together in a way that pleases a reader is what separates an amateur writer from a professional, and it can be taught: what makes writing flow is transitional words and phrases. They work in any type of writing, fiction and non-fiction alike; the book's examples run from business and politics to first- and third-person narration.

Ryan Deane's book catalogues them in **34 categories**, each named for the relation its transitions signal. Every entry of every category, with how to use it, is in [`transitions.md`](transitions.md).

## The 34 categories

The number is the book's; the definition is the book's. A phrase can sit in several categories (*however* is filed under five), because the category is set by the relation between the two statements, not by the word. Within each family, the last line is the test that tells its categories apart.

**Adding and stringing**
- **Addition (1)**: add extra information to further expand on a preceding sentence or statement.
- **Chain (5)**: string a series of related statements together.
- *Tell apart:* Addition tacks on the next item with one word or phrase; every Chain entry is a multi-part frame whose pieces run through the whole series (*First, ... next, ... finally, ...*; *Not just because ... but also because ...*).

**Ordering in text, time and space**
- **Sequence or Order (30)**: show a sequential arrangement of ideas or statements.
- **Continuation (12)**: continue on with a previous statement or to bring a topic to a close.
- **Time (34)**: express time (past, present, or future) within a statement.
- **Space, Place, or Location (32)**: show a spatial dimension within a statement.
- *Tell apart:* Sequence or Order arranges the ideas or statements themselves (*Firstly*, *Secondly*, *Initially*, *Then*); Continuation carries on from the last statement, takes stock of it, or closes the topic (*So far*, *With that in mind*, *Last of all*); Time places events in the past, present or future; Space, Place, or Location places them in space.

**Likeness and options**
- **Comparison (7)**: show and compare similarities or differences between ideas.
- **Similarity (31)**: show equality or a likeness between ideas.
- **Alternative (2)**: present two alternative ideas that can be substituted for one another.
- *Tell apart:* Similarity marks only likeness; Comparison measures likeness or difference, often against a benchmark figure (*By comparison*, *Whereas*); Alternative offers options where one can stand in for the other (*Either ... or ...*, *Instead*).

**Opposition and yielding**
- **Conflict (11)**: express a disagreement or conflict between two statements.
- **Contrast (13)**: compare conflicting ideas to each other with the goal of bringing them together.
- **Concession (8)**: make allowances or yield a point in an argument or statement.
- **Reservation (28)**: express a tone of hesitancy within a statement.
- **Exception (18)**: point out a criticism or objection to a preceding statement or argument.
- *Tell apart:* Conflict sets two statements against each other (*Whereas*, *To the contrary*, *Ironically*); Contrast holds two conflicting ideas in one view so both stand (*Some ... others ...*, *Apart from the fact*); Concession yields point A to hold point B (*Granted, ... but ...*); Reservation is the writer's own hesitancy inside one statement (*Admittedly*, *Even so*); Exception objects to, or carves an exception out of, what came before (*Except for*, *Other than*, *Besides*).

**Cause, consequence and condition**
- **Cause or Reason (4)**: help clarify the causes or reasons behind certain events or occurrences.
- **Effect or Result (14)**: highlight the consequences of an action or series of actions.
- **Logical (22)**: derive logical conclusions from a statement.
- **Purpose (23)**: express a reason or justification for doing something.
- **Condition (10)**: express that an action will take place if a certain condition is met.
- *Tell apart:* Cause or Reason looks back to why something happened (*Owing to*, *Partly because*); Effect or Result looks forward to what it brought about (*As a consequence*, *In the wake of*); Logical derives what must follow from a statement (*It follows that*, *It must be that*); Purpose gives the aim an action serves (*To this end*, *In order to*); Condition makes the action depend on something being met (*Provided that*, *Only if*).

**Explaining and backing**
- **Apposition (3)**: define or modify one statement with another; usually the apposition helps clarify the previous phrase by adding explanatory details.
- **Clarification (6)**: clarify a previous statement by adding additional details or restating an idea in a different way.
- **Illustration and Description (17)**: add illustrative or descriptive details to previous statements.
- **Evidence, Proof, and Facts (16)**: expand on previous information or help prove an argument.
- *Tell apart:* Apposition defines the previous phrase (*Namely*, *That is to say*); Clarification makes a previous statement clearer by specifying or rewording it (*In particular*, *Stated differently*); Illustration and Description adds an example, a case or something to look at (*A case in point*, *As can be seen*); Evidence, Proof, and Facts builds the proof: sources, method, inference from the facts (*As noted by*, *Empirically*, *On this basis*).

**Weight and scope**
- **Emphasis (15)**: call attention to or emphasize someone or something.
- **Importance (20)**: highlight statements that are of relative importance.
- **Generalization (19)**: generalize or use a blanket statement to describe something.
- **Qualifiers (24)**: modify a statement's meaning by either limiting it or enhancing it.
- **Quantifiers (25)**: express quantity or abundance within a statement.
- *Tell apart:* Emphasis turns up the force of a statement (*Undeniably*, *In point of fact*); Importance ranks statements against each other (*Of least importance* up to *Above all*); Generalization makes the blanket statement (*As a rule*, *On the whole*); Qualifiers limit or enhance its meaning (*Almost*, *Perhaps*, *Insofar as*); Quantifiers say how much or how many (*A great deal of*, *At most*).

**Opening, pointing back, returning and closing**
- **Introduction (21)**: mark the beginning of a statement or add further insight.
- **Reference (26)**: show that a statement is directly linked to something previously written or discussed.
- **Resumption (29)**: resume or restart a previously discussed statement.
- **Reformulation (27)**: help restate or reformulate an idea or statement.
- **Summary (33)**: summarize or conclude a group of previous statements.
- **Conclusion (9)**: formulate a closing statement to a series of previous statements.
- *Tell apart:* Introduction opens a statement or topic (*To begin with*, *At first glance*); Reference ties it to something written, said or reported elsewhere (*As noted above*, *According to*); Resumption picks a thread back up after a break or digression (*As I was saying*, *Anyway*); Reformulation says one idea again in other words (*Put differently*); Summary sums up a group of earlier statements (*In sum*, *As we have seen*); Conclusion delivers the closing statement, the inference drawn from them included (*In the last analysis*, *It follows that*).

## Choosing a transition

1. **Name the relation** the new statement bears to the one before it, in the words of a category definition above. Done when one category fits; where two in the same family both seem to, its *Tell apart* line decides.
2. **Open that category in [`transitions.md`](transitions.md)** and pick the entry that fits the sentence. Weigh:
   - **Slot**: sentence opener, word inside the clause, clause-final tag, multi-part frame spanning clauses or sentences, or a fragment that needs its next word.
   - **Strength**: how much the transition claims (*Partly because* against *Because*; *Almost always* against *Always*; *So perhaps* against *Therefore*).
   - **Register**: archaic (*Wherefore*, *Forasmuch as*), formal (*Inasmuch as*, *To return to the subject*) or conversational (*I mean*, *Anyway*, *To boot*).
   Done when the phrase sits grammatically in the sentence with every slot filled.
3. **Read across the join.** Done when a reader moves from the first idea to the second without feeling jarred.

## Revising for flow

Walk every join between sentences and between paragraphs, and at each one:

- **Unmarked relation**: the reader has to work out how the second idea bears on the first. Add a transition from that relation's category.
- **Wrong signal**: the transition names a relation other than the one present (*Therefore* before a statement that does not follow; *Similarly* before a difference). Replace it from the right category.
- **Worn transition**: the same one recurs at join after join. Vary it with another entry from the same category.
- **Ambiguous signal**: a phrase filed under several categories leaves the relation open in context. Check the last section of [`transitions.md`](transitions.md) and pick a phrase filed only under the relation meant.

Done when every join has been read and each carries the relation it means.
