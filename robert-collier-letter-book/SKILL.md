# The Robert Collier Letter Book

Robert Collier sold more than twenty million dollars of goods by mail, from coal and coke to books, raincoats, traveling bags, shirts and dresses. Every method in this book was proved by "actual letters sent to prospective buyers." Products and their reasons vary, but human reactions are the one constant: "We seldom try to sell merchandise. We sell ideas." His first rule is "Study your reader first—your product second."

## The bait

Writing letters that pay is like fishing: "it's not a matter of rules, but of bait!" Collier's whole method fits in two questions: "What is the bait that will tempt your reader? How can you tie up the thing you have to offer with that bait?"

- **The mental conversation.** Every reader is "deep in a discussion with himself" about the things that matter to him: his business, his loved ones, his advancement. Your letter butts in on it. Do what you would do in person: catch the trend of his thought, chime in on a related subject, then bring the talk around logically to your offer.
- **Point of contact.** The first line must connect with his interests and desires. Make it your point of contact "rather than rush in and try to tell him something about your proposition." If the offer does not lend itself to this, "Look for news value!" Divert his mind from his own affairs for a moment, then bring it back by showing how your offer fits them.
- **The right kind of attention.** Yelling "Fire!" gets everyone's attention and sells nothing. Attention must survive closer inspection and turn into interest. Once lost, interest is "twice as hard to win the second time."
- **Why anyone acts.** A man does what you ask for only two reasons: the hope that doing it brings him nearer his heart's desire, or the fear that failing to do it puts that desire farther off.

## Emotion first, reason second

Before you write a word, "decide in your own mind what effect you want to produce on your reader—what feeling you must arouse in him." Demosthenes' rival made crowds say "What a clever speaker!", while Demosthenes made them say "Let's go!" Aim the whole letter at "Let's go!" Selling "is just a matter of making people WANT some one thing you have, more than they want the money it costs them."

"When it is action you want, go after the emotions every time!" Two Wells letters prove it. One followed every rule and aimed at the intellect, and was "only moderately successful." The other aimed at the emotions and "literally pulled its head off." Give the reason its due as well: offer "a logical excuse for buying that they can tell to their friends," but base the real urge on a primary emotion. Beware the letter everyone admires for its cleverness. The good letter leaves the reader "hardly conscious of the letter itself," so absorbed is he in doing what you want. Evidence and cases: [word-pictures-and-motives.md](word-pictures-and-motives.md).

## Writing the letter

1. **Name the act and the feeling.** Decide the one thing the reader must do: order, pay, send the card or give. Then put yourself in his place and ask the questions Collier asks. How would I have to feel to place this order, grant this extension, or pay this man before all others? What would I want to know first? What would interest me most? What would I gain by accepting? What would I lose by refusing?
2. **Pick the motive.** The six prime motives are "love, gain, duty, pride, self-indulgence and self preservation." Stack several. Separate the motive that makes him *desire* the thing from the one that makes him *act* now. **Persuasion** is "finding the motive that will impel your reader to do as you wish, then stirring it to the point where it is stronger than his inertia, or his economical tendencies." Ask: "What has he to gain by doing as you wish? What to lose by refusing?"
3. **Build it on the Six Essentials.** Collier's list, in his order:
   1. **The opening** wins attention by fitting the reader's train of thought and making a point of contact with his interests. It rouses curiosity and leads him on to read. See [openings-and-attention.md](openings-and-attention.md).
   2. **The description or explanation** pictures the proposition, first outlining its important features and then filling in the details.
   3. **The motive or reason why** creates the longing. It describes "not your proposition but what it will do for him": the comfort, the pleasure, the profit.
   4. **The proof or guarantee** proves your statements are true, or builds confidence with a money-back guarantee.
   5. **The snapper or penalty** compels action at once by holding over the reader "the loss in money or prestige or opportunity" if he waits.
   6. **The close** "tells the reader just what to do and how to do it, and makes it easy for him to act at once."

   You may "mix them all up into one grand goulash," but the six, or close relatives, are in every successful letter. They are only mechanics. What sells is "the feel of your message," the letter of a man "full of an idea."
4. **Paint one word picture.** Build the proposition in the reader's mind from things he already knows. Find "the one point on which your sale is likely to hang and build your letter around it." Make every word a brush stroke on that point, and put the rest of the features in the circular. Sell what the product does for him, measured in his own unit: cubic feet of gas, not tons of coal. See [word-pictures-and-motives.md](word-pictures-and-motives.md).
5. **Give proof.** Quote a customer the reader knows or could know, by name and street. Then decline to ask him to take anyone's word: "Try it for yourself and see!" Back it with free examination or a money-back guarantee. "It usually pays to put a testimonial into every letter you write."
6. **Supply the impulse.** At the critical moment, "Too much urging will make him draw back. Too little will leave him where he is." Most men balk at a decision that costs money, so humor them. "Tell him not to decide now—on your main proposition," and set him a minor task instead: his size on the card, or permission to send the goods for a week. A favorable decision on that minor point carries the major one "in three cases out of four." Your enemy is **Manana**, "Tomorrow," which "more than all other causes put together" has lost sales.
7. **Set the hook.** Without a penalty in the close, the prospect slips away "like a fish off the hook." Answer three questions: Why must he act now? What does he gain by acting now? What does he lose by delay? Then hold a "Sword of Damocles" over him: a date after which no orders are taken, a stated reason the supply is limited, a price rise on a fixed date, or a combination offer good for a limited time. "Be definite—and be positive!" Give the exact quantity left, because "it carries conviction," and make it sound as though you mean it.
   The reader reads on for one reason only: "He expects a reward." So keep leading him on, "always holding back something for the climax." You want an order or a payment, not "favorable consideration."
8. **Close in two parts.** First comes persuasion and inducement: what he gains by ordering, what he risks by delay, a strong guarantee and a small-sounding cost. Then the barb: tell him exactly what to do, so plainly "he will not have a reason for not ordering." Name each action: put your name on the card, drop it in the mail, it is already stamped. "We are all mentally lazy," so dictate the action. "Seven times out of ten" a business man reading a letter plainly not from a customer reads the opening, glances at the middle, and jumps to the last paragraph "to see what it is all about, and how much it costs." So the close must carry the whole proposition.
9. **Test it against the ideal.** Imagine the ideal product: everything you could want it to have and to do for you. Write the letter that stresses every point of that ideal. Let it cool for a day. Then strike out "every descriptive phrase and adjective that cannot honestly be applied to your product." Enough will be left for a strong letter.

Collier's letters "set out with a definite goal in mind and they made every word carry them one step nearer that goal." The letter is done when all six essentials are present, it has one focal point, any low price carries its reason why, the penalty is definite, and the reader has one plain action to take.

## Rules that hold for every offer

- **Reason why for a low price.** Always give "a convincing reason why you are able to make a lower price than your competitor." A bare price cut is not believed, because there are "too many ways of skimping on quality." Bargains must also be backed by visible quality.
- **One article, one price, no choice.** "If you offer one article you will get twice as many orders as if you offered a choice of two or more articles!" Choice means hesitation, and "the customer who hesitates is lost to you." "Nine times out of ten, the deferred order is a lost order." Sell groups as a definite number at one price. Put any unavoidable color or size choice inconspicuously on the order card.
- **Keep an idea file** of good starters, descriptions and closers, "not to copy, but to inspire you to new and better ideas."
- **Adapt the idea, never the wording.** "The wording counts for little. It is the way you adapt the idea back of the successful letter that counts." An idea that is psychologically sound works on books, bags, blankets, stock or lots, and the adapting is the job. Rivals who copied Collier's letters word for word failed within two years: "It is the images back of them that counts!"
- **The scheme back of it.** "It is not the copy that counts so much as the scheme back of it" (Lanier). When a product stops selling, change the scheme first: the premium, the hurry-up, the reason for ordering now.
- **The letter carries the load.** Letter, circular and card are "just an advertisement, divided into its component parts." An advertisement mailed as it stands pulls less than a fourth as well as the same matter split into the three. The letter brings 65-75% of orders, the circular 15-25%, the card 5-10%. If the circular overshadows the letter, the letter is weak.
- **Length.** Short and snappy when you want only an inquiry. When he must commit to buy, tell the whole story and keep it interesting. Lincoln's answer serves: "Long enough to reach the ground!"
- **Positive ideas only.** The letter puts ideas into the reader's head, so leave out any negative one you would have to take out again before he buys.
- **Lists and repeat sales.** "The first sale is the hardest." The best mail buyer is one who has already bought kindred goods by mail. "The real profit in selling by mail lies not in the first sale but in the succeeding ones."
- **Test, and heed only the customer.** Test new letters against "the last successful circular" on every mailing. "The only voice we heeded was the voice of a customer." Properly run, mail selling is the safest business there is: "You need never risk anything but the cost of a test." Guard above all against "the one unpardonable sin of know it all."
