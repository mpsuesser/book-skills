# Word pictures, motives and the emotional urge

## Word pictures

Once you have his attention, drop the old-time preacher's "firstlys, and secondlys and thirdlys" and the long-winded description. Lead him "from one point of interest to another, with word pictures so clear, so simple, that he can almost see the things."

- **The mind thinks in pictures.** One illustration is worth a thousand words, but "one clear picture built up in the reader's mind by your words is worth a thousand drawings," because he colors it with his own imagination.
- **Build from the familiar.** Visualizing means "describing your proposition in terms of things the reader knows." Take a figure his mind readily grasps, add one point of interest here and another there, and let him build it "piece by piece in his own mind as a child builds a house of blocks." Lay the foundation and framework first, then add each piece in logical order, never starting with a wall or the roof. Washington Irving's Ichabod Crane, lank, with hands dangling "a mile out of his sleeves," is the model. In David Harum, the Lost Chord is described in horse-racing terms for a horsey friend.
- **Build with bricks he can handle**, meaning familiar words and images. Exaggerate and he will kick the whole structure over "like a child trying to build with blocks a house that will not come out right." Keep the picture more desirable "than the money or the time or the trouble it takes."
- **Familiar similes do the work**, because each stirs a memory he already holds, a foundation rather than shifting sands. Collier's examples: apples "like those with which Eve tempted Adam"; "As much chance as a gold-fish on a cat farm"; "Satisfying as sinking a ten-foot putt"; "painting a battleship with a tooth brush"; a company that does not advertise is "like a man who whispers to himself on a desert."
- **The sale is made in the reader's mind.** Register "a sequence of impressions" whose combined effect makes him want the thing "more than the money or trouble it costs him."
- **The difference is one of degree.** There is "nothing you can say about a 50 cent cigar that you cannot say about a 5 cent one." Expensive and ordinary goods do much the same things; the difference is the satisfaction they bring, and that lies largely in the buyer's mind. Your job is to build it there.

**Model descriptions:**

- **Ginger ale.** Lore like the French wine-makers', "Only three men know the secret," and stimulation like mountain air.
- **A rug** "so thick that she felt her shoes must be hidden in its pile."
- **Laundry.** "Linen white as almond blossoms," sold on "not 'how quick' but 'how well.'"
- **Hawaii.** "Four days beyond the Golden Gate," with "Four days away" repeated as a refrain, and "Why don't you go and capture your dreams?"
- **Ham.** Each attribute is certified with its reason: corn-fed, so firm and sweet.
- **A resort.** Reverse understatement: "a dead town... That is why it appeals to many live people," "an adventure in naturalness."
- **A gas burner and an oven.** Concrete mechanism: a nest of jets, the Dutch oven, the baker's arch, the heat indicator. Each feature is turned into what it does.
- **Perfume.** "Like golden sunshine mellowed by stained glass."
- **Bags.** "Most Traveling Bags are just plain carry-alls. You dump everything into the bottom... and then you rummage." Ten moisture-proof pockets give "a place for everything, and everything in its place."
- **Linen.** "So heavy it will stand alone, so strong it will wear forever."
- **Boys' suits.** Cloth perfected for British Admiralty sailors' trousers: "Your boy will outgrow it before he can wear it out!"

**Put life and a smile into it.** No one stands in line at a museum, a library or a travelogue, but the movies are packed: "most people cultivate their intellect only under the lash. They revel in emotion at any and all times." "So give them a thrill!" English food copy shows the humorous register. Cheese from a Guernsey herd "one-third cream," against "mirthless Stiltons made in hissing factories." Cakes from a rose-covered country house: "Could such cakes as these be made within earshot of a London motor-bus? We trow not."

**Get the story back of your product.** Weave a mustard into a picnic love story. Tell of the poor father who carved a wooden velocipede and became a toy manufacturer, or of the first rubber tire, made for a young veterinary's wheel-chair for his invalid mother. "Give your reader a laugh or a tear or a lump in his throat."

## Sell what it does, in the customer's unit

"Description... will never sell your product by the thousands. It is what it will do for the one who buys it that counts!" "The point which sells your customer is not what your product is, but what it will do for him!"

- **Gas coal.** At Powellton, West Virginia, coal and coke sold through brokers on price alone, and the mines worked three or four days a week. Collier reasoned that for some purposes this coal must give better results than any other, so buyers would pay a premium. Gas companies could pay 25-33% more per ton, figured on cubic feet of gas. So he ignored the price per ton and argued gas per pound and its cost. The letter: "How much are you paying per cubic foot for your gas? If we can show you how to cut the cost by a fourth, are you interested enough to prove it?" It quoted a named superintendent's figures, then offered "this chance to write new figures of your own in your plant." Send the card without money and a carload is shipped. If it did not save 25%, "that carload... won't cost you one cent"; if it did, a year's contract. "Remember, no saving-no cost." When buyers strayed after a bargain, Collier lab-tested the rival coal, figured the final cost of gas per cubic foot, and sent the figures "without comment." The business came back.
- **Coke.** Stove founders bought on sulphur content. One founder's figures showed Powellton coke melted as much as 15 tons of iron to one of coke, against 8-9 for ordinary coke. That was 47¢ to melt a ton of iron, against 80-90¢. A letter told the story of a cupola foreman reporting a 15-to-1 melt. His boss asked if he was "getting your politics mixed with your melting figures" (Bryan's 16 to 1); the president attended the next melt and it was proved. The close: if you figure coke cost "not on the delivered price of the coke, but on tons of iron melted... we have some figures that will interest you. May we send them?" The result was a near monopoly of the stove business in the territory.
- **The lesson.** "Coal and coke were drugs on the market and could only be sold on a price basis, whereas cubic feet of gas, or thermal units of steam, or melted tons of iron were things that sold themselves."
- **Ivory soap.** Competitors' soap carried about 15% water against about 5% in Ivory. Tom Beck wrote every laundry asking "whether they were buying soap or water," since it was cheaper to draw water from the faucet than to pay freight and soap prices for it.

## Tested selling sentences

Elmer Wheeler showed that most selling is guesswork. Few salesmen can say which words sold the customer, and the humble clerk does the actual selling. So give the clerk the words an executive would use. "Hidden in every spool of thread, or row of safety pins, are reasons why customers will buy." The sentences that sell, again and again, are **"TESTED SELLING SENTENCES"**: each names one concrete thing the product does.

- Safety pins "will not burst open in the garment and injure baby."
- A fly swatter is square, "and gets them in the corners."
- A child can put on a buttonless union suit by himself.
- "This suit has long pants, Sonny, like your Dad wears."
- A pipe clerk tapping a pipe on the counter, asked if it was unbreakable, answered "Why I've been tapping it on the counter all day!" and sold pipes all week.

"Just because a sentence sounds reasonable to you, does not necessarily indicate it will sell!" The same holds for the printed word: test openings and phrases the way Wheeler tested sentences. When one proves unusually successful, develop it to the utmost and extend it to similar ideas.

## The six prime motives

People are like automobiles: pushed, pulled, or moved by their own motive power, and the fuel is desire. Arousing it is "the gentle art of exercising persuasion." The six prime motives are **love, gain, duty, pride, self-indulgence and self preservation**. They are usually mixed.

Put yourself in the reader's place and ask:

- Will it make me richer, healthier or happier?
- Will it raise my standing with others, or let me do, write or say anything better?
- Is it something everyone should have?
- Will it gratify a passion?
- Will it help those I love?
- Will it prevent loss of money or of others' respect?

The new writer grabs the arguments nearest to hand, which are his own selfish interests. The experienced writer asks these questions, "picks the motive that is strongest and presents it from the viewpoint of the reader alone."

- **Mix the motives.** Pride alone seldom sells a car. Reinforce it with self-indulgence, with love and duty toward the family, and with a large dash of gain: the old car hurts his standing, repairs and gas eat up the difference, and the saving is not possible later.
- **Desire motive and action motive.** Separate the motive that makes him want the thing from the one that makes him take the action you want. With a bill, sell him the idea that his credit means more than the money.
- **Love** "is always the strongest motive," and the hardest to work into a letter without harping on it. A reader resents love used "as a leverage to dig money out of him." Claiming love of children as the reason to sell an encyclopedia gets laughed at. A cash-register letter showed the deft way: "If some one were to make your boy a thief... is it right to put temptation in the way of other men's boys?"
- **Gain** is the easiest: "we are a gullible race." We believe more readily that someone is unselfishly helping us make or save money than that he is helping our loved ones. Tell a man that only two cars are left, that the price will be advanced 25 percent when the new stock comes in, and that as an old customer he has one held for him at the old price, and he will believe you. Gain also sells investments and forecasting services; see [raising-money.md](raising-money.md).
- **Pride.**
  - A buggy maker's letter said the factory manager reported that Mr. Jones "stands pretty high," so if he drove our buggy his friends would buy. The president then offered his own built-to-order buggy, "envied by friends."
  - Clothes don't make the man, "But you know yourself how helpful they are in getting him a hearing."
  - Collier's re-selling enclosure for coats defined being "correctly dressed" as being conservatively dressed by the standards of men of high repute.
  - Culture and etiquette sell on the fear that "some little slip" will cost a man opportunity or prestige.
- **Vanity** is pride's subtler form. Once attention is won, it is by far the strongest motive in Collier's experience. He does not mean the vanity that buys a cosmetic, but "that unconscious vanity which makes a man want to feel important in his own eyes and makes him strut mentally." Use it subtly. The devices built on it are in [offer-devices.md](offer-devices.md).
- **Fear of embarrassment.** The "Book of Etiquette" had gathered dust for 10-15 years. Nelson Doubleday then sold a million copies on the fear that "some unconscious gaucherie" would embarrass the reader, a "Keeping Up with the Joneses" appeal. "Can You Talk About Books with the rest of them?" is the same appeal.
- **Ambition.** Pelton's "Power of Will" had failed under its first publisher, who sold it on a description of its contents. A bookkeeper bought the plates and gambled his last $200 on an appeal to ambition. It brought $2,000 in direct orders, and he pyramided that to over $2,000,000 in fifteen years.

## Emotion over intellect: the evidence

- **The two Wells letters.** The first offered the revised four-volume edition at "1/4 less than the Original Price of Two!" It stated the facts: a million sold, every page revised, 100 paintings. It made the offer with a year of the Review of Reviews, a week's free examination, and "The enclosed card obligates us only—Not You!" It was only moderately successful. The second opened on the fossils and dinosaur eggs, then "Not these things." It moved to the life principle that met every danger by growing gills, lungs, fur and feathers, so that the reader's "future is of his own making." It gave a reason why for the price: 100,000 sets contracted, under 5,000 left, selling at a 500-a-day clip. It ended "Then try to lay it down!" and "The payments?—You will laugh at them!" It "literally pulled its head off."
- **The Simonds war history ads.** Three intellectual ads (a Roosevelt testimonial, "Colleges Study This History," honors from governments) brought reasonable orders. Emotional ones tripled and quadrupled the returns: Foch's message "My right has been driven in... I will now attack," "The Coming of the Yanks" at Chateau-Thierry, and "The full story... has never been written" except around "some confidential table." The intellectual kind was dropped entirely.
- **Tabloids** outsell clean papers ten to one.
- **Convincing the intellect is not enough.** You must rouse "urgent desire," the feeling that effort and cost are "of no account compared with the satisfaction." Lacking that, "you might as well throw it away. It will never make you money."
- **Human nature, not merchandise.** "It is not merchandise you are selling, but human nature, human reactions." The movies have guaranteed laughs (a butler diving into the whipped cream) and guaranteed tears (a boy praying for his daddy's safe return). In the same way, an approach that sold a set of books can be adapted with little change to shoes, socks or luggage.
- **Draft before seeing the product.** Collier often wrote the first draft knowing no more about the product than the average reader. He put in everything he would want in it as a buyer, then studied the product and compared it with that ideal. Many times the approach found this way was the most effective. "Familiarity with human reactions... is even more important" than knowledge of the product.
