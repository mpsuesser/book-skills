# Collecting by mail

Getting paid is as important as getting the order, and a collection letter is a sales letter. Its job is to make the debtor *want* to pay.

## The two levers

"There are only two ways to collect old accounts. The first is persuasion. The second is the threat of court action or loss of credit standing." Put another way, what impels a debtor to pay is "Two things only—fear or persuasion."

- **Fear works and costs you.** Stir the fear of lost credit, a judgment or a garnishee, and you will get your money if he can pay. You will also lose his good will, and his trade once he can buy elsewhere.
- **Persuasion leaves a smile.** It extracts the money and leaves "the pleasant glow of a favor done for a friend." "You can catch more flies with honey than with vinegar."
- **Pleasant is not weak.** Courtesy makes it all the more necessary to state the demand "plainly, unmistakably." Nothing lulls a debtor into security like a diffident "Trusting to hear from you" letter. Collier revised one series that sent a vague, wordy request for the personal favor of "advising me when I can expect a remittance." A debtor who got it would put off payment at his convenience.

## Alternate the series

The most successful series combines every form and alternates them. A threat frightens some debtors into paying and angers the rest. A stronger threat after it, or the action it implied, only makes those readers angrier.

**Stroking the cat.** Ruffle the fur with a threat and the sparks fly: you land some payments. Then smooth it down with a cash-up offer, an inspirational letter or a stunt, and you land many more. Then stir it again with a stronger threat. Keep alternating, and "you will get every cent it is possible to collect."

- **The threat beats the action.** "The threat of action is even more effective than the action itself provided your debtor believes your threat is not mere bluff."
- **The law is a last resort.** It is seldom profitable unless a large sum is involved. A lawyer's letter is one of the most effective collectors there is, but it seldom pays to go beyond the letter.
- **Close collections are good collections.** Follow up the moment an account lags. "Camp on his trail" and keep reminding him, courteously but firmly, so that when he is able to pay, you are the one he pays.

## Persuasion appeals

- **Honesty and pride in credit.**
  - Lincoln walked miles to return a few cents' change; Mark Twain paid his company's debts. Both show the "I-will-owe-no-man-a-penny" spirit.
  - Assume the 99% who are honest. "Only a very, very small percentage are deliberately dishonest," but honest people get classed with dead-beats because they are careless and put off small sums.
  - "That difference between 'eventually' and 'now' is what marks the line between the 'gilt edged' customer and the 'poor pay.'"
  - Offer the pleasure of being a "preferred risk" whom any merchant will gladly credit.
- **The small-account argument.** Small accounts add up. Your profit depends on promptness across a mass of $1 and $2 accounts, and "you will not consciously be instrumental in working a hardship." The aggrieved version: "A feather is not very heavy, but have you ever carried a feather bed upstairs?" Hundreds of small accounts make a tremendous load, and a small account is easily eaten up by postage and letters before it is paid. "I know you don't want to appear unfair."
- **The busy man.** "A person who is busy can be forgiven for forgetting a little bill like the above." For an executive: "With a man like you, it is not a problem of how to pay, but when—because you're so busy." Ask him to pencil his "OK" on the bill and drop it in the out-going basket.
- **The joke.** "May we not, like Jeff's creditor, request your autograph on a check for $1?" The setup is Mutt and Jeff: Jeff's bad news is a request for his autograph, on a check for the eight bucks he owes.
- **Re-sell the purchase.** When the product needs further work from the buyer, as courses and sets of books do, you often must re-sell him on it before you can collect. "An occasional inspirational letter of the right type is worth its weight in gold—and usually brings it back in the form of remittances!" A course can mail the next lesson before the overdue payment arrives, with testimonials and "TODAY—that's the word for you."

## Threat appeals

- **Credit standing, made visible.** Show the account in red ink: "John Johnson—Bills Receivable—$25.00" becomes "John Johnson—Account Overdue $25.00" unless his check arrives by the 17th. Add the effect this will have on the credit men of every store in the association. Trace the history of the debtor from the Stone Age slab and mallet, to debtor's prison, to today's credit standing.
- **The legal department letter.** The account is 90 days past due, we are COMPELLED, and obtaining goods with intent to defraud is criminal. It comes up in one week. An English jurist said he would give up his coat rather than risk his waistcoat in a lawsuit: "how much more true... when the facts are so strongly against you."

## Get the letter read

"The greatest danger of collection letters is that after the first one, people recognize them for what they are from a glance at the corner card of the envelope, and throw them away unread." Vary the appearance in every way possible. The hard part, and the important part, is getting the debtor's attention.

- **The miniature letter, envelope and bill.** The Business Week used these, then followed with a letter of ordinary size: "The attached bill is so small, that we don't want to take up much space in bringing it to the attention of a man in your position."
- **The string letter.** Louis Victor Eytinge tied the letter and bill together with the piece of string the debtor was to tie around his finger: "Tie the string—and you won't forget!"
- **The pin letter** adapts the string idea and works about as well.
- **The blue letter.** A blue envelope and letterhead say how blue you feel at not getting his check.
- **The gravestone letter.** A black border, "Sacred to the memory of John Jones... whose account died after a lingering illness." In small type: if Jones still lives, evidence in the form of a remittance will be welcome. Stunts like this can be carried too far, but the idea behind them is sound.
- **A row of question marks.** "That is the way we are beginning to feel" about the goods you have not paid for.

## Cash-ups and premiums for prompt payment

A good cash-up offer on installment accounts "will frequently bring in as high as 30 to 40 percent of payments in full" from accounts that would otherwise be slow even with monthly installments.

- **The favor form.** "Will you accept a 3-volume set... in return for a favor I want you to do?" The favor is to pay the balance in full now. Give the reason why the gift is possible: only two or three hundred sets are left, the rights to the books go with them, so the last sets are being given away. "To be one of them, you'll have to mail your remittance right away."
- **The gift form.** "A GIFT FOR YOU! If you are prompt!" Only a few hundred sets are left of a handbook that sold for $5.00, "not even enough to print new advertising matter describing it." Pay the full statement now and the set comes free. "If the books are all gone when your remittance comes, we will return that remittance intact."

## The hardest case: goods never ordered

A company held nearly 600,000 one-dollar accounts for perfume, initialed handkerchiefs and cosmetic sets sent unordered. Better Business Bureaus were advising people to throw such goods away. Threats were impossible, so persuasion had to be used with care. Mollify first with a smile, but first establish that something is owed.

1. **The letter and bill combined.** The item and its $1 price as headline, then "A person who is busy can be forgiven for forgetting a little bill like the above." Ask him to wrap a check or dollar bill in the letter; the envelope is already stamped.
2. **"The voice with the smile,"** about two weeks later. This was the Mutt and Jeff autograph letter.
3. **Two slightly aggrieved letters.** The first used the question marks and "We've written you several letters—but not a line from you have we had." The second used the feather bed and ended "RSVP $1."

The series brought dollars from about 60% of recipients and the merchandise back from about 25% more. Only about 15% followed the advice to throw the goods away or refuse payment. "If a letter will bring back the money from such a debtor... it ought to bring home the bacon no matter where it is used."
