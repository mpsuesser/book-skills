# Openings and getting the letter read

The opening is the first of the Six Essentials. It has one job: to fit the reader's train of thought, make a point of contact with his interests, and lead him on. Collier cites John Caples's finding that the headline accounts for 60% of an advertisement's pull. In the same way "the start of a letter makes or breaks the letter," because a reader who is not interested at the start never reaches the rest. Even before the start, though, the envelope has to be opened.

## Chime in on the mental conversation

Find what this reader is already thinking about and begin there. These are Collier's examples of bait, by reader:

- **Mother of a month-old baby, selling a carriage.** Open on the carriage the baby "is going to be seen in," and admired in.
- **Father of a boy of six to eight.** Open on the boy going to college, where the other boys' fathers are a railroad president, a banker and a governor, and on the moment the boy says "This is my father, boys." Then ask: "Will he be proud of you?"
- **Druggist.** He has run up front half a dozen times for stogies, court plaster and a stamp; now ask him to take a minute.
- **Householder, selling burglary insurance.** Give last month's burglary count for his own city, then: "I can have your valuables covered by noon."
- **Farmer.** "Any man who owns a cow loses a calf once in a while."
- **Merchant.** Open on the little tragedy of the customer who "didn't buy anything."
- **Mother, selling boys' clothes.** "About that boy of yours—" he wants clothes like his father's.
- **Motorist.** Open on a windshield in a rainstorm.
- **Housewife.** "After you have your breakfast dishes washed..."
- **Any man.** Tell a third-party story: Mr. Fuller and his cold shaving water.
- **Insurance agents.** Begin with mud turtles going PLUNK off a log in childhood, then turn to business prospects.
- **Business girls, selling dresses.** "Being well dressed is not merely a matter of money... Yet, you are spending twice as much for your clothes as you should!"
- **Executives.** "At some desk in your Bank sits a man on whom you have your eye." This letter offered a free copy of a magazine to hand to that man, and over 10% of 10,000 business heads sent for it.

## News interest

"Tell a man something new and you have his attention. Give it a personal twist or show its relation to his business and you have his interest." The business man reads the murder, the scandal and the sports before the financial page.

- **Story-teaser headlines.** "When the Rattlesnake Struck!", "The Oldest Man in the World", "Was This the Flood of the Biblical Story?" and "Will a Yellow King Rule the World?" Each leads the reader a few lines; after that, "your own fault if you can not make your story so interesting" that it carries him to the order blank.
- **Tie an old product to today's news.** For a history of nations Collier opened with "A hundred years ago all Europe was in arms against Napoleon—Today almost the whole world is arrayed against the Kaiser." When Tut-ankh-Amen's tomb filled the papers, he sold Wells on the prehistoric chapters. A newly found tablet confirming the finding of Moses carried a last-chance letter after 250,000 sets had already sold. "If you can tie in with what people are thinking about and interested in, you can sell anything."
- **Topical openers.** "How's the garden?" at the suburban station in spring (selling House and Garden). "Pay-day—what does it mean to you?" New Year's planning in January.
- **Price-reduction news** interests readers when the product already has an established market. Examples: a date to mark on the calendar; an advertising rate rising $1 a line on October 1st, so order before the 30th; a $14.85 bag about to go to $20.
- **News nuggets as the opening.** "How's Business, Mr. Jones?" opened with real items, such as bonds preceding steam shovels by about four months, then raised the questions every executive was asking. The magazine answered them.
- **Diversions that come back to him.** These openings start away from the offer, then turn toward it:
  - "Look for the Priceless Ingredient" in a tale of Hakeem the Wise One, which turns out to be the honor of the maker.
  - Grandfather's buggy went no faster than Caesar's chariot, leading to the automobile.
  - The Eskimo's moss lamp in the igloo, leading to wall insulation.
  - The man who left the Patent Office in 1886 because "everything had been invented," leading to a lubrication saving.
  - A stammerer's confession: "For 20 years I was an exile... And then I learned to talk!"
  - "Who are the 12 Greatest Living American Women?" leading to one name beyond question, the author being sold.
- **Questions about his own position.** "If your expenses were doubled tomorrow...", "Suppose a good job were open where you work. Could you fill it?", "You've got to have more money—", or life insurance sold on "17 ways to use it now" rather than "you have to die to win."
- **The clever enclosure.** A check for $6.20 made out to the reader, to endorse and return before December 10th. Or the headline "'Enclosed find check for 25% of your Tire Bill.'"
- **Open on something he nods to.** "You know how the little folks just love a good story..." The parent agrees at once, and the letter moves on from there through what children read, proof, the bargain and the free trial "in swift succession." The folder pulled as high as 8%.
- **Statistics made vivid.** Use figures for the reader's own city, never national totals.

## Tested opening forms

"There is no one best method of approaching your reader." Approaches that work in most cases take "the guess out of advertising." Use them unless you know better, adapt them, and keep testing new starters against them. A firm that writes the same people often cannot live on one opening; they tire of it. Collier found a dozen variants that pulled as well as "I wonder if you would be good enough to do me a favor?", and many quite different styles that greatly outpulled it.

- **The hypothetical offer.** "If Dr. Eliot of Harvard were to say to you—'Come around to my home to-night'..." to learn the essentials of a liberal education in fifteen minutes a day: "You'd go, wouldn't you?" Another ran "If the world's greatest Efficiency Expert—" would follow you around and check every move... "Well, that is, in effect, just what Harrington Emerson offers you."
- **"Give me Five Minutes, and I'll give you..."** This is a proven order-getter, adaptable to anything. Collier's variants: "Give Me Two Minutes—And I'll Give You The Secret of a Goodly Profit Without Investment!" for Christmas cards, and "Give me Five Days—And I'll Give You The Secret of Learning Any Subject!" for a book series. He also suggests "Give me 5 days, and I'll give you relief from itching feet."
- **"How."** Haldeman-Julius found that a "How" in a Little Blue Book title frequently doubled or even quadrupled its pull, and that "How to" led all his magic words. The others were "Truth," "Life" and "Love" (the strongest), then "At last," "New," "Advice," "Facts you should know about—" and "Cultural Help." "The Art of Controversy" sold far better as "How to Argue Logically." "Evolution Made Plain" flopped; "Facts You Should Know About Evolution" sold in big volume.
- **Question bursts.** Use a string of "Do you know...?" questions the product answers, as in "After the War—What?" and "A Message to You from the Great Men of Medicine! Do You Know?", which listed seventeen of them. Or ask a riddle and answer it: "What is the 'Unpardonable Sin' in all Nature?... Stagnation—Standing Still!" A variant answers the stereotype question by question: "Will you set me right on one point about yourself:... A physical weakling?... A failure? Why, he was the founder of modern business!" The ladder form is "Will you risk $1 for the Secret of Youth?" or "Would you risk $1 for the most effective collection letter we have ever seen?"
- **The exclusion opener.** "Men who know it all need go no further into this letter than this paragraph," or women who "know it all" are not invited to read. It flatters the reader who stays.
- **The cumulative "When..."** "When a man can turn an $80,000 loss... into a $121,000 Net Profit... When he can sell, by mail, $3,000,000 worth of Traveling Bags... Then there must be something so fundamentally sound" about his method.
- **A story or joke that points the moral.** One letter told of a fisherman whose tarpon was swallowed by a shark ("where I come from, we bait with sharks!"). The moral: someone can always top a claim, "So I shall say only that our book is DIFFERENT." In Collier's test, the proven "Give me Two Minutes" approach beat it.
- **The personal question.** "What size dress do you wear?", "What size hat do you wear?", "What size shoe do you wear?", or "Is your address, as written above, correct?"
- **The experiment.** "Will you try this experiment? Ask your jeweler to show you a perfect deep-sea pearl. Put the enclosed Deltah manufactured pearl alongside it. Then try to tell them apart!" Another: tear off the enclosed cloth sample and compare it with a strip cut from your own trouser seam.
- **The educational test.** "Do you know how to distinguish pure Silk Socks from 'loaded' or 'fibre' silk? There are three ways:" the fire test, the water test and the feeling test. Then show the product passing all three.
- **The announcement.** "Once in a blue moon the announcement is made of a new invention of such great importance as to warrant calling your attention to it—IN ADVANCE OF MANUFACTURE!" Adapted from a book announcement, it sold another 50,000 bags.
- **Proof as the opening.** "When 10,000 men from all over the country send all the way out here just to get a raincoat, there must be something unusual about these coats."
- **A historical paradox.** For a boring machine Collier wrote "When Millions Were Actually Thrown Into the Gutter!" The canals of 1830 were "the most expensive gutters in the world"; today's street trenches are the most expensive ditches.
- **A trait that gets under the skin.** "Some men are born gamblers." It was used on hotel men, who need not gamble on carpet cushion.
- **The rating or questionnaire.** "Can You Talk About Plays And the Like With the Rest of Them?" with a questionnaire to rate the reader; and "Have you ever been officially rated for Personality and Business Traits?" These are vanity openers; see [offer-devices.md](offer-devices.md).
- **The favor, permission and privilege openers** ("Will you do me a favor?", "I am going to send you, within the next few days...", "The enclosed card is of real value to you") are offer structures. They are given in full in [offer-devices.md](offer-devices.md).

## The outside: getting the envelope opened

When people recognize your envelopes as circulars on sight, opening them becomes a problem. Novelty is invaluable to anyone who mails often, because readers "are never able to discount in advance what is inside." Once the reader is inside, "their job is done," and the letter must win him.

- **Catch-phrases on the envelope.** Treat them as headlines and test them against other headlines or a plain corner card. They are effective as a rule only on third-class mail. On first-class mail a plain corner card wins "almost invariably," because the only point of paying for a 2¢ stamp is the look of a personal message, and a catch-phrase destroys that look. "If you want to use an attention getter on the outside of the envelope, save half your postage by sending your message third class." Really effective teasers are scarce. It is generally better to make the envelope so personal-looking and attractive that he wants to know who it is from.
- **Disguise the circular.** If your corner card brands the envelope, use a box number with an armorial design or monogram. Try a window envelope. Change color and size from mailing to mailing (No. 6 to baronial, to Monarch, to No. 9, and back), and vary the corner card.
- **Attention ranking.** In Collier's experience a miniature letter in an envelope about the size of a calling card is the most effective piece. Next comes an imitation handwritten letter with a matching hand fill-in. Then Hooven letters with the recipient's name repeated in the body. After those come stunt letters: a penny pasted at the top, a new dime, a German mark.
- **Telegram forms.** A letter on a pseudo-telegraph form in a yellow window envelope like Western Union's is very effective as a variation. A "giant" telegram in 18-point type, in a giant envelope, did even better. Both are novelties that wear out quickly.
- **The giant letter.** It is 9 by 14 inches, a doubled four-page sheet, with a full-size picture of the bag opened inside and a description of every feature. Use it when the previous offer has combed the lists and the next one must look as different as possible. A cowhide sample tipped on added 15-30% to orders. A "$1,000 Reward" on the last page drew the most comment and was the most convincing proof the bag was as claimed. It was then reused on raincoats and overcoats. Giant letters greatly outpulled ordinary ones on topcoats, but they grew "a bit passé" from overwork.
- **Pictures on the envelope.** A man's picture, taken from a trade paper or house organ and pasted on the envelope addressed to him "Care of" his company, is subtly flattering. A post-card view of the sender's town, though unrelated to the product, added about 10%. It won attention and made it believable that a small-town concern can serve more economically than high-rent stores.
- **Vary the appearance of repeat mailings.** Customers mailed 10-12 times a year stopped opening envelopes and letterheads that never changed. Changing size, color and corner cards was "like circularizing a brand new list."

## The letter itself: details that raise the count

The start, the description, the argument and the close decide a letter. Once the appeal is right, the details "make that order record mount."

- **Look personal.** "Anything which tended to make your letter seem more personal added appreciably to the number of your orders." Collier tested metered against stamped postage, precancelled against plain stamps, pen against facsimile signature, fill-ins against running heads, processed against stencil fill-ins, ink colors, and printing through a ribbon. Hooven (individually typed) letters added nothing over good fill-ins until the reader's name was worked into the body of the first page two or three times; then they doubled the orders. People cannot tell processed letters from typed ones, but they do not believe a letter personal unless they see an allusion to themselves in the body.
- **Fill in name and address and send first class** when writing to executives, to give them an excuse for treating the message as one of import.
- **Stamps.** Two red 2¢ stamps pull more than one 4¢. One red 2¢ beats two green 1¢. A brown 1½¢ looks like a 4¢ and beats a green 1¢, but does no better than two ½¢ stamps. Stamps beat meter and indicia in Collier's experience, though a black-ribbon 1¢ meter frequently outpulled a 2¢ stamp.
- **Fold.** A military fold that shows only the salutation and first line increased orders by as much as 10%. Indenting the main paragraph works better than underlining or capitals.
- **Letterheads.** A pictorial letterhead pays only when the picture adds "essential background that would not be possible without them." A lithographed nature scene doubled orders for a nature library, while many other offers pulled better on plain paper. A small reproduction tipped into the upper left corner added nearly 50% for gravure prints; a small full-color reproduction raised returns on calendars. In a personality business the founder's picture on the letterhead adds 10, 15 or 20%; in another account Collier puts it at frequently 5-10%.
- **Tip-on samples** prove what words cannot: leather for traveling bags, and cloth for coats to show wool content, color and feel.
- **Special delivery.** On a test of 1,000, special delivery pulled 9% against 3% for ordinary postage. On a full mailing it woke men at night with "Visions of sickness and sudden death." The rate fell to 6%, and the mailing angered so many customers that even double the orders would not have been worth it.
- **A first-name salutation** ("Dear Jack") brought remarkable orders and angered so many customers that it was dropped along with special delivery.
- **Expert opinion is no guide.** An expert said only a fool would try a certain mailing form; tests showed it added 35%.

## Folders and circulars

A folder is an advertisement through the mail. It needs an attention-winning illustration and headline, an interest-arousing start, clear description, logical argument and a clincher, and a coupon or card that makes ordering easy. Obvious stunts are often overdone and defeat their purpose. On circulars that tell stories, tell "enough of each story to whet the reader's appetite and make him want more, but not enough to satisfy his curiosity or enable him to fill in the answer for himself." Collier calls this "the essence of all successful circular-writing." For shirts, an illustration of shirts hanging on a line outpulled the others. Inside, "the 1, 2, 3, 4 method" of listing features, with pointing arrows, made the strongest appeal of all. The last page carried the free-trial, no-money offer and a strong testimonial or two.
