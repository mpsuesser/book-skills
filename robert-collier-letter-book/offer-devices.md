# Offer devices: proof, impulse, hook and the letters that carry them

Each device below is an idea to adapt, not wording to copy. Results are as Collier reports them.

## Proof

New Process (Warren, Pennsylvania) grew from nothing to over $1,000,000 a year on one basis: proof.

- **Why it works.** "We are a credulous people," but we discount everyone's claim to be best or cheapest. Claims laughed at from the seller "were accepted at face value when they came from the mouths of their customers." A third person's enthusiasm makes us prick up our ears; back it with positive proof and we will risk our money.
- **The formula.** Name a local customer, then refuse to lean on any authority, then offer the trial: "You know Jim Jones, who lives over on Vesey Street... Here is what he says... But we don't ask you to take his word for it... Try it for yourself and see!" Even a congressman's word is waved aside in favor of a week's free tryout. "All the argument in the world is not equal to proof such as this."
- **How the first customers make the proof.** At the start New Process had no customers to quote. It relied on "free-examination, no-money-until-you-have-tried-it-for-a-week" to establish confidence. It turned every order into a satisfied customer, and every satisfied customer into a testimonial. "The testimonials were bait" for every man in that writer's town.
- **Personalized proof.** Write a different letter for each town, each quoting a local customer by street address: "Perhaps you know Mr. Smith. If so, you know he is not given to exaggeration." Add a list of other named local wearers. It is expensive, and it was worth it. A P.S. naming well-known local buyers (a judge, a steel company's assistant treasurer) did the same in city mailings. Retailers later pressured the quoted men, and when the letters had to fall back on state-wide proof they lost money.
- **Proof that travels in the envelope.** An enclosed manufactured pearl to compare with a jeweler's real one; a cloth sample to tear; a "$1,000 Reward" offer; a lab test of the rival's coal.
- **Put a testimonial in every letter**, even collection letters, because a customer often fails to pay because he is not quite sold on the value. Bought celebrity endorsements have given testimonials a bad odor, but one with "the ring of truth about it" always dispels doubt.

## Free trial and the guarantee

By mail the hardest task is winning confidence, which is why free trial and the money-back guarantee exist. Free trial also shows your confidence that the goods back up your claims.

- **Lines that carry the trial.**
  - "The enclosed card obligates us only—Not You!"
  - "The postal order is simply an opportunity for the books to sell themselves to you."
  - "Signing and sending the enclosed card puts the burden of proof upon us."
  - "We'll pay the charges both ways. Could we give you any stronger evidence of our faith?"
  - "You must wear the smile of satisfaction, or it's no sale."
  - "If for any reason—or no reason" you are not satisfied, send it back.
- **Make the guarantee about results.** One guarantee read: if within six months "$1 hasn't grown to $60," full refund. Another: refund within a year if you cannot attribute $1,000 of additional earnings to the books. "Could anything be fairer than that?"
- **Pay-the-postman, with a reason.** "There's no margin in that for collection expense... so we ask you to pay the postman." Pair it with "The $4.98 is yours when you want it," or "Your money will have a string to it. It's not mine until you say so."
- **The comparison guarantee.** "If you can equal it anywhere for less than $25.00—SEND IT BACK!" Or "If you've ever bought its equal for double the price—just send ours back."

## Supplying the impulse

Give him "a push without seeming to do so," so that going forward with the crowd is easier than standing still. The sideshow barker's helpers start the push toward the ticket window at the critical moment. Find "some easy preliminary task on which you can set that motive busy," then make it easier to keep going than to turn back.

- **The car dealer's sequence.** "Come and look," then sit in it, then drive it Sunday, then "Now, what time shall I send it around tomorrow?"
- **The yacht seller** (who sold $25,000,000 of yachts). Pictures and the suggestion of forgetting the office on a Caribbean cruise. Then a special-delivery invitation to a trial spin with Mrs. Prospect, the wheel in his hands, and plans of a boat being built for someone else. When the prospect states his preferences, "they have a boat in the building with those very features."
- **"Don't decide now."** "Just fill in your height, your weight and your collar size... Wear it for a week... Then decide." Then the coat lies around, the bill comes, the box is thrown away, and returning it is too much trouble. Collier calls this "not just the best kind of sale, of course, but probably the average sale."
- **Why buy by mail at all?** Only two reasons: the buyer is convinced he saves money, and the seller "makes it even easier for you to get his coat than to go to the neighborhood store."
- **Minor tasks that carry the major decision.**
  - "Figure it out for yourself... Now if you'll pick up that pencil you were figuring with a moment ago..."
  - Samples in time for next Saturday's sale.
  - "A modern and actual Aladdin's lamp lies in the return card attached. Rub it with your pencil."
  - "The time to decide is while the facts are fresh in mind," with a form to register your decision either way.
  - Check on the card the types of merchandise you want, to acknowledge the catalog and stay on the list.
  - Return the card YES or NO "as a courtesy."
  - "Don't file this away to think over."

## The hook and the penalty

"Only two reasons why your reader will do as you tell him." Either you have made him want it so badly he reaches for the card, or you have "aroused in him the fear that he will lose something worth while." The debtor fears for his credit, the buyer for his bargain, the merchant for your trade, the young man for his advancement. "When you want to inspire fear, be definite! Be specific!"

- **Exact quantities.** "Only 46 sets left!", "Only 157 Sets Left!", 790 left-over Ulsters, output limited to 19,961 coats, cloth enough for only 450 suits. Each is backed by a reason.
- **Reasons for the limit.**
  - A new edition is coming with a changed binding, and costs have raised its price over 50%.
  - Rights to the books end with this edition.
  - Paper, labor and binding cost twice to four times as much.
  - The cloth will not come again for eight weeks.
  - The faculty can read only ten stories a day.
  - Coats are made up in advance, so a fixed number exists.
  - Each city gets an allotment ("You are one of the men selected").
  - Only 125 stores, one to a section, can carry the line.
  - Only 1,000 extra copies of each issue are printed.
- **Scarcity in arithmetic.** "If only 4% of our readers accept this offer, it will take all the sets we have on hand." Then: a new edition would cost five times the price. Shakespeare in twenty pocket volumes for $1.00 pulled 4% and better, and cleaned out the stock on the first mailing.
- **Dates.** "Don't send me any money after the 1st," because it will be returned unless at the new price of $50 a share instead of $40. Rates rise $1 a line on October 1st. "Our doors will open at 9 o'clock Monday morning." Books close in two weeks. "Cards received too late will be acknowledged with a note of regret."
- **Mean it.** Say positively that no orders will be taken after the date and that there will be no last-minute concessions. "You will lose a few last-minute orders, but you will gain ten times the number" in those who act while the order card is in their hands.
- **Last-chance lines.**
  - "Not tomorrow, nor after lunch—for things to be done after lunch are frequently not done at all—but now, while this letter is before you."
  - "In the great book of Time there is but one word—'NOW'."
  - "I'll be holding one set for you for a few days."
  - "Better still, pencil your name on it Now—before you run any chance of forgetting it."
  - "You will forgive me if I seem insistent, but—will you use the post-card now? I'm afraid you'll mislay it."
  - "On second thought, better mail it right away—while you have it in your hand."
- **Telling him what to do.**
  - "'Now, what am I to do?' you ask. Simply send your order to me personally. Just say—'Make my suit as you agreed in your letter.'"
  - Pin your check or a dollar bill to this letter.
  - Wrap a dollar bill in this letter.
  - Write "Send me that prospectus, Please" across the edge.
  - "Nothing to sign... Your name's already on it."
  - "Just your penciled 'O.K.'"
- **Sign-offs that carry the offer.** "Yours for ten days only," "Yours—if you are prompt," "Yours up to 790," "Yours 'til then," "I'll be waiting," "Yours for mutual co-operation."
- **The P.S.** It is the last thing read, so it carries an extra premium for prompt reply, a deluxe binding, named local buyers, or the condition that cards must come back at once.

## Premiums and the hurry-up

The premium is the most common way of "sugar-coating" the pill. It is also, more often than not, the hurry-up that makes the reader order now. "An old dodge, of course, but why worry whether a scheme is old or new when it brings a million dollars worth of orders?" Southern merchants call the principle **Lagnappe**: the little extra thrown in, by which a merchant is judged.

- **O. Henry.** The offer was five volumes of Jack London free if he ordered right away. Its first year sold over 50,000 sets, over $1,000,000. In the second year "Before the Price Goes Up!" stopped working: the price had in fact gone up, and people no longer believed further raises possible. One changed line, "Last Chance to Get Jack London free!", restored the results overnight and made a second million-dollar year. "The threat of losing the premium was something different."
- **Simonds war history.** Readers felt the inside story was not yet out and would wait. A premium of six little volumes of "True Stories" of the war, free if ordered at once, sold nearly 35,000 sets in under a year. "Your last chance to get True Stories free!" sold 25,000 more. "Here again it was the scheme that sold rather than the copy." Other premiums moved 5,000-10,000.
- **A reason why for a free premium.** "We are very near the end of our big edition, with not enough sets left to justify printing circulars and letters to sell them alone." Or the remnant is "not enough to sell in our usual way," so it is given away.
- **Premium size.** A premium too valuable to be had without commitment brings orders and no sales. A fountain pen given for examining a book set brought eight times as many orders on one list "but with no more sales!" The silk-stocking debacle is the other example (see [campaigns-and-testing.md](campaigns-and-testing.md)). Richard Harding Davis's short story "The Man Who Could Not Lose" was "the happy medium": it greatly increased orders without increasing returns.
- **The premium as selector.** When you can no longer pick your subscribers from occupational lists, "you have to so arrange your offer that the type of reader you want will pick you. And that is the main purpose of a premium." Bruce Barton's two books winnowed out serious readers; 25,000 were wanted and 25,000 sold. A Life Extension course drew men of 35-45 who were losing vitality; it brought about 50,000 readers. Its reason why for being free: the course "will appeal most strongly only to the more intelligent and wide-awake... the very people that we want as readers."
- **Pre-empt "too good to be free."** The man on London Bridge offering gold sovereigns found no takers. But "we have too much faith in the intelligence" of readers to think they will refuse value because they are not asked to pay.
- **Put the premium in the product.** McGraw-Hill held that the best premium is something in the magazine itself. Sold like a premium book, a coming series of articles pulled as well as a separate premium.
- **A gift for examining.** "Will you accept one of the new, large-size, self-filling Fountain Pens, with your name die-stamped upon it in raised letters, in return for a little favor I want you to do?" The pens cost 2-4¢ to stamp. Doctors' orders for the HandiBag went from about 4% to 12 and 14%, and 60,000 bags sold in the first two months. "Did that pull? Does honey draw flies?"
- **Two books for one card.** "DID YOU EVER GET $10 FOR NOTHING?" offered two $5 books with a subscription if the card came before January 1st.
- **A gift with an order.** A fountain pen-pencil with the dentist's name in gold leaf came free with his next $5 order of supplies: "I only have 500... Among over 10,000 customers... this quantity will not last very long."
- **A premium that rescues a slow item.** When bathrobes stalled, slippers were offered free to anyone who would send for and try a robe, and the stock melted away. A muffler nobody wanted moved when a slip offering it at a special price went into every overcoat shipped; more had to be bought.

## Reason-why patterns for a low price

- **Idle factory months.** "You know how most factories are—busy and working overtime eight or ten months of the year, and practically idle the rest. And those lean months—like the famines of ancient Egypt—eat up most of the profits of the fat ones." Making coats in the off-season gave $28.75 against up to $47, and sold about 21,000 overcoats in two months.
- **A market dip caught.** Wool hit the toboggan and was bought near the bottom, or a mill sent "S.O.S. calls" when overstocked and needing money, so its whole lot was taken below any price.
- **A test of direct selling.** An old manufacturer tests selling direct at the dealer's price: "If the response... is not tremendously large, the manufacturer's plan will be discontinued."
- **The author's sacrifice.** Wells took one-seventh of his royalty to reach a million readers. Rinehart accepted one-tenth her usual royalty on a first edition.
- **Volume.** Contracts for 100,000 sets; runs of 25,000-50,000 against a bookstore's 1,000-2,000.
- **Quality first, saving second.** "No man is going to the trouble of sending away for things he can just as well get around the corner unless he feels that he can save money," so always bring in the saving. But "a bargain offer is of no use unless you make it convincing," and low price "never gets far unless backed by reasonable quality." Often the most successful pieces sold "the quality or comfort or usefulness of the product, and then brought in the money-saving as an after-thought."
- **Direct from factory to you**, with overhead and middlemen cut out.
- **"You benefit by our need."** "We don't know how much more expensive these new locks... These first bags will enable us to find out." Or an introductory edition prices the books "merely to gauge the demand," so "You can help us."
- **Old customers before the jobber.** "Rather than offer them to some jobber at a 'bargain' price, I'm going to offer them to my old customers."
- **The price ladder.** Ask how much the reader would expect to pay ("$30.00? $50.00? $100.00?"), then come down: "not even their regular price of $13.50" but a special introductory price of $7.85. Socks ran "75¢?... No sir... $1... That's more like it," then $7.95 for twelve pairs. "The price? You'll smile when you hear it." Shrink the cost to the day or week: "worth seven cents a day to you," three cents of money and five minutes of time a day, "50 cents a week," "10 cents a week." "My salary? You'll smile when you hear it—10 cents a week."

## The damaged-set letter

"The old standby," it "never failed us after any big sale." It worked on books, Civil War photographs, traveling bags, bed blankets and overcoats.

- **The basis.** After a big sale some sets come back rubbed: corners bunged, a torn page, thumb marks. One hurt volume makes the whole set technically damaged.
- **The letter.** In the rush of selling 75,000 sets there was no time to sort the imperfect ones. They are damaged "so slightly that you would have to make a close inspection," but cannot be sold as perfect. Rather than give the binders the profit of re-binding, the firm will let the advantage go to a few people "who love good books for the books' sake and not for trifling details about them," at 30% off. "Only 157 Sets Left!" Add a homely detail, such as gray and blue linings mixed up at the binder's ("Some subscribers might even prefer blue and gray linings"). Ship on approval at our expense, and "the best ones will naturally go to those who reply first."
- **Results.** About 3,000 damaged Simonds sets brought about 10,000 orders; the extras were filled profitably with new books. Damaged blankets sold the 20,000 pairs that a dud letter had left, plus 10,000 more. The Warren shipping clerk complained he was getting "damned tired kicking holes in the sides of the packing cartons just to make those bags look damaged!"
- **The left-over variant.** "790 Left-over Ulsters at a Big Discount!" In the rush there was no time to watch sizes and colors; all sizes remain, but no complete range in any one color. Odds-and-ends cloth let the factory make more, and it brought about 10,000 orders. Keep its circular deliberately simple, to fit the idea that so few are left. "A Few Overcoats at a Great Saving" told of 21,631 sold and 582 left, "not enough to do any heavy national advertising about." It pulled almost as well. Clearance sales to a store's own customers give the same reason: last season's stock must go before new models arrive.

## The favor letter

**The favor appeal**: "the man we feel most kindly towards is the one we have just done a favor." A manager feuding with a competitor asked the rival how he handled customers who abused terms. The rival "opened up like a rose to the morning sun," and they became friends. It works on indifference as well as enmity. It was "one of the most effective appeals we ever had," with a dozen variations on a score of products, and "I have seldom known it to fail."

- **The structure** (the raincoat letter, which sold all leftovers plus about 20,000 more coats at 2-3% on an $18.75 product):
  1. "Will you do me a favor?"
  2. Explain the new product and the merchant's risk: "you can never be sure of such things until after you have sunk a lot of money in them. Which brings me to the favor."
  3. The favor is to wear one for a week and write me.
  4. "But I did not start out with the idea of selling YOU the coat."
  5. Give three measurements on the card.
  6. If you keep it, pay the special introductory price; otherwise send it back, and "in payment for the week's wear, tell me frankly your honest opinion."
  7. Return the card whether or not you accept, "to insure against its falling into other hands."
  8. Your opinion is valuable only NOW, before we commit to production.
- **Variants.**
  - "This letter is purely personal and does not seek business. Rather, it invites a courtesy from you."
  - "Before giving this offer wide publicity, we are laying it before a number of men whose judgment we value."
  - "How big an edition shall I print next?"
  - "I'd do the same for you were our positions reversed."
  - "Will you give me a little information about yourself—just the sports that you go in for most?" Here half price on the magazine is the thank-you.
  - The giant topcoat letter asked readers to name the price: "$19? $18? $17?"
  - For a cash-up: "Will you accept a 3-volume set... in return for a favor I want you to do?" See [collections.md](collections.md).

## The permission letter

This is an assumptive send that asks only for permission. It was used on books, socks, topcoats, Christmas cards, collection-letter series and a business forecast.

1. "I am going to send you within the next few days..."
2. "...probably not like any you have ever seen before because:" followed by numbered differences.
3. "There is only one thing: I don't want to send it without your permission. You can grant that in a moment on the enclosed card." Or: "I can't send the hose until you tell me the size."
4. No obligation; return it for any reason or no reason.
5. The price ladder, the guarantee, and a limit.
6. "I'm the one who is asking to send them. Won't you let me?"

The seven-little-books version ("Would you like to see $1.00 grow to $60.00...?") went to lists that had never heard of the books. It pulled as high as 9%, and from two million letters it brought over $1,000,000 of orders in six months.

## Vanity devices

"We all like to feel important." Feed the reader's vanity "without making it too apparent that this is the real purpose." It is "the strongest" motive in Collier's experience, a "harmless strain of vanity" in everyone. "Why blaze new trails, when there is a paved road already laid?"

- **The registered card.** The Literary Digest used this for 25 years and more: "The enclosed card is of real value to you, and has been registered in your name. It is for your personal use only. If you cannot use it, we should feel obliged if you would return or destroy it." Publishers have mailed it repeatedly, and the public still takes it at face value. Collier's versions are the Wells **10-day Option** ("not transferable... If you can't use it, we should appreciate your kindness in destroying it") and the **Special Privilege Card** ("we have numbered it and limited it to your own personal use"), allotted by city. The Wells Option sold 90,000 sets in four months; the Special Privilege Card bag letter pulled 2-4% on book-buyer lists.
- **The Charter Subscriber.** "Because your name is included on a selected list of those we should prefer to have as our Charter Subscribers." Its reason why: a key group "whose opinions are respected" will discuss the magazine "in the right circles." It averaged nearly 9%.
- **Elected membership.** "You have been elected to full membership... Here is your membership certificate." The Literary Guild added a numbered list of advantages, a free gift volume, and a price guarantee against costs rising 30%.
- **The waived fee.** The Advertising Club let in "a few important names... WITHOUT HAVING TO PAY THE INITIATION OF $100 and your name is one of those I have suggested."
- **A board or staff place.** "You have been designated as one qualified to serve on our Board of 'Player Patrons'"; the patrons give judgment, not money, and as readers get half price. Or an Advisory Editorial Staff: "Mind you, this is no mere bluff. This will require some real work on your part," with a free subscription barred by post-office rules, so two years for the price of one instead. It "pulled amazingly high returns."
- **"Your name has been suggested."** Collier used it for a theater stock that needed "a few representative names from each section," and for a country club limited to 400 whose members must pass "the most captious Board of Governors." The theater letter opened: "I should like to buy $10.00 worth of your personal prestige."
- **Make him ask for the literature.** "I have a present for you. To get it, you must ask for it, and it would be nice if you would say Please." The present was a prospectus, with only 500 copies.
- **Rate him.** A personality inventory (worth $15-25 privately) was offered free with six issues for $1. Its limit was real: raters can handle only so many, and late certificates are returned with their dollars. It pulled 6% on business-book buyers, over 5% on management-course students, and as high as 10% on a psychology list.
- **Hand it to your man.** A bank president was offered a free copy to give the promising man at the desk. The follow-up for quantities read "We don't care what you pay for it," "let you set your own price," and "YOU, John Jones, will be the man back of it!"

## Small units and the $1 offer

- **The first-lesson-free offer.** "Absolutely Free the first Lesson." Notify us within two weeks or send $4.85 for twelve. "Whether I extend this offer to any other groups will depend entirely upon your response."
- **A coin card** asking 25¢ for a single lesson pulled nearly as well as a free copy, on one list 12%, and closed more subscriptions.
- **"It is just about as easy to get $1 as 50 cents."** "Will you risk $1 for the Secret of Youth?" sold 25,000 extra copies. Sell on one lesson only, never dividing the interest, then deliver a second lesson as a surprise so he gets more than he expected. Circulars at 2½-3¢ pulled 5-10%, which on a $1 item "is doing well." Varying the lesson keeps bringing different classes of reader and keeps the list alive.
- **A booklet** added to a $1 letter raised its pull two and a half times. This is a rare case of the circular doing the pulling; ordinarily a good letter alone pulls about 75% as many orders as with the best circular.

## One envelope, two products: the wife's envelope

Selling women's or household goods to the head of the house would divert interest from the main offer. Instead, put a separate sealed envelope inside, marked "for the wife only," holding its own letter and circular for blankets, linens, towels or lingerie. It is "the one way I have ever seen in which you can sell two separate and unrelated products in a single envelope, without interfering with the pulling power of your main offer."

## Re-sell after the order

Right after ordering, many buyers feel they were "too precipitate" and look for a way to back out. "Re-sell the customer on his purchase" in the acknowledgment, the bill, and an enclosure he sees before he uncovers the goods, because "the sale is not made until the goods are paid for." On the back of the enclosure, put a "Friend's Order Blank" good at the low price only if sent at once.
