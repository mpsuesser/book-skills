# Raising money by mail

"People will give, when you have stirred their emotions. People will invest, when you have aroused their cupidity. And people want to know the future, so if you can persuade them that you are any sort of a Seer or a Prophet, they will buy your forecasting service." The question is the one the book opens with: what do they want, and what is the bait?

## Charity: one victim, then reason

- **Statistics leave readers cold.** You read "with comparative indifference" that 36,000 people are killed and 1,000,000 injured each year in auto accidents. See one child run down and you will never be indifferent again. "We cannot visualize them in the mass. The only way to make us feel them is to tie them into the story of one victim." The best fund method shows the picture of one crippled child, tells his pitiful story, "and then leaving it to the reader to multiply that story by thousands."
- **Emotion first, then a swift shift to the intellect.** Work up pity, then give a logical reason to give, lest a similar catastrophe come close to the reader. English war propaganda tried broken treaties and failed. It succeeded by dramatizing individual victims such as Edith Cavell, then following with reasonable argument about what might happen to us.
- **The child's own words.** A tuberculosis association reproduced a childish scrawl across the top of its letter: "Thank you for helping to save my daddy's life." Underneath, it passed the thanks on to last year's Christmas Seal buyers. Then it asked: the seals have already been sent, and in the rush you overlooked the remittance. The follow-up carried a snapshot of an under-nourished youngster in the corner and a letter in his voice ("I am Junior Smith. This is my picture."). Its P.S. said the earlier letter "was evidently overlooked," and "We must help hundreds of others like Junior Smith."
- **The application.** Summer camps send "Before and After" pictures: a pale boy in the city heat, then the same boy after a month of fresh air, food and exercise. They add campfire, swimming and fishing scenes, "all the things you loved to do when you were a boy." The letter is the boy's own application: "a good boy who wants to go to Camp but has no money... One dollar a day will pay his way." The reader returns the application with his contribution.
- **The appeal to reason.** "What helps one helps all," and the reader needs only enough sympathy on top of that to give. "Human beings do not come like buttons from a mold, but in individual packages. There is a prize in every package." The reader is one of the fortunate, who has found his prize; will he share a little? Say that more than 80% goes direct to the needy, itemize what it buys, and call on the Good Samaritan.
- **Help people help themselves.** Donors are tired after years of relief calls. "What a relief it would be to lend a hand to a man, who would use whatever you gave him to start afresh." The Quakers were teaching soft-coal miners crafts and gardening. The letter asked for equipment, or "$10 will feed a whole family for a week. Will you adopt one for just one week?"
- **Friends writing to friends.** Gather a central group of well-known citizens and have each write a personal appeal, in his own name, to ten friends. "Will YOU join me in helping an old friend of ours out of a serious 'hole?'" A shelter was eight months behind on rent and served with a dispossess notice. Each was asked to put up $10: "I promised for myself and assured him I thought I could promise for you, Jim."
- **Vanity** is the principal method beyond these. Many a man will give freely to see his name blazoned across a newspaper page who would give for no other reason, and small contributors can be given the same satisfaction.

## Causes

For a league to enforce public economy, a brand-new penny was pasted at the top of the letter, under the headline "Here Is the Most Profitable Invention Ever Made—The Idea that Borrowed Money Must Pay INTEREST!"

- The penny, put out at 4% compound interest in the year One, would now be worth many times the wealth of the world. Franklin's $1,000 bequest grew the same way.
- If a penny can grow like that, imagine a public debt of $36,000,000,000 growing by $4,000,000,000 a year.
- "You are now working for the Government one day in every four."
- Cite a past success: the league had stopped payments that saved $300,000,000 a year.
- Ask for $1 for one year's work, and say why numbers matter: only as the voice of a large section of the public can the league have weight with Congress.

It brought a good response.

## Investments

The appeal for a bond is SAFETY with a high return. For a speculation, the appeal is the chance of a high return, admitted as a gamble.

- **Bonds of an industrial loan company.** "You Can Laugh At Money Worries If You Use This Plan!"
  - **Confidential and in advance.** "This letter, coming to you in advance of any public announcement, is intended for your eyes alone."
  - **The promise.** As little as $20 a month could mean retiring in ten years.
  - **The reason the business is safe.** Banks serve only 15% of the population; small-loan companies serve the other 85% under a uniform small-loan law. The business has no sales problem and no dusty inventory: "Its shelves are bare every night." Every dollar works 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. Loans are never over $300, and losses ran under one-tenth of 1% in eight years.
  - **The terms.** The bond's privileges are numbered: 6% interest, a share in earnings, and conversion into stock.
  - **The wisdom of putting money to work.** Everything "increases after its kind," as Genesis repeats. In the parable of the talents, money is put out at interest and the idle servant is rebuked. "How many dollars are sitting around your home, staring at you idly."
  - **The limit.** Only 500 bonds now, and the next issue only to those who apply. Good for 10 days, withdrawable within 20.
- **Mining stock.**
  - **Open on the neighbors' multiples.** Lake Shore, Teck Hughes from 11 cents a share, Wright-Hargreaves tenfold, Macassa fourteenfold in two years.
  - **Ask for no money yet.** "THIS LETTER DOES NOT ASK YOU FOR A SINGLE PENNY. ALL WE WANT FROM YOU NOW IS AN EXPRESSION OF INTEREST." The reader pledges $10-$100, binding only if enough others pledge to make up the $100,000 needed.
  - **Proof.** The engineer's assays, the newspaper headlines, and "ore does not rain down from heaven. It is forced up."
  - **Answer "why isn't it funded?"** People look for sure things: one mine begged at 30¢ a share and later sold around $19. Then admit the gamble: "We don't offer it as a 'sure thing.' It is a speculation." The insiders have put in $90,000 of their own.
  - **The limit.** Books close in two weeks.
  - **Rules.** Offerings under $30,000 were then practically unrestricted. A $100,000 offering required filing a prospectus.
- **A theater stock.** "I should like to buy $10.00 worth of your personal prestige—and here is an Option Certificate for one share of Bonus stock as payment in advance."
  - **The circular.** It argued under sub-heads: Safety ("The safest security on earth is the earth itself"), the Hub of Nine Millions, Location, What Others Have Done, the Class A Stock, the Director, Earnings figured on only half capacity.
  - **The limit.** The Option was good for ten days; after the theater opened, "the problem will probably be to find any to sell."
- **Dignity.** Financial literature is "a deadlier lot of stuff" than any other advertising, "all done in the name of dignity." "Dignity is a cloak for every jackass in high position," an excuse for "complacent stagnation." Collier rewrote a stock circular that had attained "the usual of dignity—no one even knew it existed" in the interesting way. The results differed as sharply as the dignified and the interesting Wells letters. Leaders, too, want interest, not dignity: "it is only when you interest them that they will read—or order!"

## Forecasting and inside-information services

These appeal to gain.

- **The hook.** "How To Read the Future So You Can Laugh at Business Worries In the Months To Come!" Open with the broker who wished "To know TODAY what the market will do next Thursday."
- **The argument.** "It is not personal ability that makes people rich or successful, so much as OPPORTUNITY": one man made $35,000,000 out of a $253 investment through inside knowledge. Then the news: "The nation's financial capital has moved from the banks of Wall Street to the banks of the Potomac."
- **The offer.** Give the writer's credentials, then an eight-week trial for $1 at little more than half the regular cost.
- **The conversion letter for trial takers.** "1937 will be, for insiders, a 'buying year.'" Russell Sage's secret was "Buying straw hats in January!" The mass of people guess wrong, buying at the peak and selling on the toboggan. Next year anyone will read the signs that only insiders can read now. The full year costs $1 a month with no money now, but mail at once to keep the letters coming without a break.
