# Campaigns, lists and testing

## Lists

- **The best list** is "the buyers of other sets" by mail, or more generally people "who have bought kindred books by mail." "The easiest man to sell is one who has previously bought books by mail." Occupational lists pay sometimes. Telephone books and automobile registrations pay only occasionally.
- **City dwellers.** "Contrary to general belief," it is "far easier to sell the dwellers in the cities by mail than it is the farmer." This holds for apparel and even more for books. Orders for suits came in as freely from New York and Chicago as from small towns.
- **Book buyers for merchandise.** Collier had 40,000 bags to sell. Rather than waste letters on telephone users, he turned to the millions of book buyers, a virgin field. An adapted Wells "Option" letter pulled 2, 3 and even 4% on a $14.85 bag. Within a week a two-million mailing followed, and 50,000 bags sold in one short summer season.
- **"You should be able to sell 10 percent of your customer list any related product."** You will not get 10% every time if you mail monthly. The Harvard Classics buyers took about 10% of a twenty-volume fiction shelf, with orders costing about 35¢ apiece: an ordinary letter and an uninteresting circular pulled because interest was already won.
- **A diversified list.** A magazine must give advertisers a fair cross section, and "the ideal list is a diversified list." Circularizing the professions will not keep a list above 100,000; past that, let the premium select the reader (see [offer-devices.md](offer-devices.md)).
- **Women** were never sold on a large scale except from special lists: women on customer lists, wives of customers, and good mail buyers. Lists of women picked up by the hundred thousand never paid.
- **Hard-to-find buyers.** Rent a list that is right for the product. For boys' suits Collier used a magazine read by boys of ten to fourteen.
- **Dead-beats.** "It is not safe to assume... that 90 percent of the people are honest." A class of "professional curiosity-seekers and 'dead-beats'" sends for anything that needs no down payment; a credit association pooling 15-20 companies held records of 300,000 and more. Guard against them, then assume 90% and more are honest. At 3,000 orders a day Collier shipped good-looking orders without investigation and checked only the doubtful ones. He normally wrote off 30% of gross for returns, cancellations, collection expense and losses, and the rush raised that only 3%.
- **Prize-letter returns have a catch.** Collier's highest return ever was 200% in books ordered: 1,500 letters brought orders for 3,000 copies. But the 1,500 had written in to express interest in the author's magazine article, and other lists gave ordinary returns.

## Testing

- **A test is the only risk.** "You need never risk anything but the cost of a test." Wells: "Give us a sixty-day option and we'll find out!" Test, find the sets sold per thousand names, multiply by the names available, and you know what to contract for. If the test fails, fill the orders or return the money, and you are out only the cost of the test. A letter can be tested before the product exists: Collier mailed 1,000 to each of ten lists on books not yet written, then wrote them at night for a month.
- **The yardstick test.** On every test, mail against the new circular "a thousand of the last successful circular we had used on that product." If the new one outpulls the old off-season, it will in season too; if not, discard it. Collier devoted January and February to such tests.
- **Test two or more angles.** "As usual," Collier tried half a dozen letters and used the best. When two pulled equally, he alternated them. Every big mailing carried tests of new letters, and new approaches surprisingly often outpulled everything before them.
- **The customer's voice only.** "Individual opinions counted for little with us, no matter whose they were."
- **Try anything once.** Collier would "be guilty of every [sin] in the Decalogue rather than the one unpardonable sin of know it all." Mail selling keeps changing: "What you learn today you must unlearn tomorrow." Then "along comes some darned fool [who] knows none of the rules and sells a million on the very plan you said could not be worked!"
- **Watch the cause of a dud.** Blankets passed their test, then conditions changed before the mailing and the letter was a dud. A season's weather killed bathrobes.
- **The cost of one assumption.** Someone reasoned that women are more susceptible to free offers, and mailed silk stockings to miscellaneous women's lists with a favor offer: wear one pair, keep it as a gift, return the other five or pay. It drew 600,000 pairs ordered in three months, over $750,000. Then 72% of over 100,000 boxes came back less one pair, about 10,000 accounts were uncollectible, and jobbers offered a quarter of cost. Collier tested twelve to fourteen appeals and found four that pulled. He used each on the whole list a month apart: a special discount to teachers for the names of five parents, "the first removal sale in our history," a 1¢ sale and a combination sale. The stock sold out, and the year cleared $18,000.
- **Products the mail ruled out**, despite orders: shoes, since more than half came back on fit; suits, with about 40% returned; trunks, which arrived damaged after two weeks by freight; clocks and watches, for mechanical complaints; and athletic winter underwear, which was bought and returned. Shirts sold best of all because many sizes made fitting easy.

## Season and timing

- **Books** sell best in January and February, the season of resolutions and inspirational courses. **Merchandise** sells worst then, because store bargain sales make savings hard to show.
- **Before the season.** People buy by mail "far more readily before the season for that product has begun, or at its very start." Once the season is under way they turn to local stores rather than risk delay. When competitors copied a bag and sold it in stores at $1 less, February tests did about as well as June. Collier mailed in February and sold more than half his quota before they moved, rested through their season, and resumed afterward.
- **Spring and fall.** Spring was only half as good as fall for topcoats and suits; the same letter brought twice the orders in fall. Spring was best for shirts.
- **Moving news.** Public taste for war books dropped sharply about a year after the Armistice.

## Economics and results to plan by

- **The first sale costs most.** It may cost 10, 20 or even 50% of the selling price; the second should seldom cost over 5 or 10%. Big houses spend several dollars for a first order and offer the public small units of $1 or $2, because a small satisfactory transaction establishes confidence as surely as a big one. New Process began with a $1 lot of initialed handkerchiefs. Wm. H. Wise & Co. began with a $2-3 scrap book, then sold many thousand $100 sets.
- **Cost per order.**
  - Harvard Classics: 10,000 circulars at about 4¢ each went to two-year-old leads that salesmen had given up. They pulled 4½% for a $39 set, under $1 an order. The follow-ups brought about 2½%, 1½%, 1% and a final 2%.
  - Wells: a 2½¢ circular pulling 3½% on a $7.50 combination brought 26¢ of orders for each circular, a selling cost under 10%.
  - Bags: about 4½¢ a circular including list rental, about $2 a sale at 2% and down to $1.
  - "After the War—What?": about 3% on customer lists and 1% on occupational lists at 2¾-3¢ each. That is about $1 an order at 3% and $3 at 1% on a $33 sale, a profit both ways.
- **The Hooven, the first-class fill-in, and the giant letter** each cost more and earned it where tested (see [openings-and-attention.md](openings-and-attention.md)).
- **Returns.** O. Henry returns averaged about 10% and ran as high as 14%.
- **The overselling trade-off.** The seven-books letter oversold, because "no book ever written could quite come up to such a promise." With shipping at 16¢, it is "better to get from 4 to 9 percent of orders and have 40 percent of them returned, than to receive the 1 or 2 percent most publishers are satisfied with and have only 10 percent of them come back." It built a customer list of over 100,000 names in a year.
- **Banner year.** About 18,000,000 circulars brought about $6,000,000 in gross orders.

## Keeping a campaign alive

- **Never all eggs in one basket.** While one product sells best, test hardest for its successor. Lanier and Stone did this, and so should you.
- **A set's life.** "Three years... usually marks the life of any set of books that has a big, popular sale." Two million dollars of O. Henry in two years saturated the book-buyer lists.
- **A new reason for buying.** When every regular list has been worked twice, you need "not only a different letter, but a new reason for buying." Collier's examples: a revised edition, a new tie-in with the news, a new premium, a new format.
- **Make each offer as different as possible from the previous one.** Bargain appeals and reservation stunts wear out. Find "something of interest in itself, yet closely tied up" with the product, such as the Unpardonable Sin letter for Wells.
- **Personalizing.** John Blair started New Process with $100 borrowed and one idea, after an undertaker in Kane bought the only black raincoat: "Now she can rain all she wants to!" Black raincoats in every size for undertakers sold 400 coats from 10,000 letters, then 400 more, then 400 more. Then came ministers and priests, then coats, suits and furnishings, reaching about $2,000,000 a year in fifteen years. The whole appeal was "personalizing": a house devoted to each group's interests alone, never the same letter to undertakers as to doctors, and testimonials from men in the same line.
- **Repeat sales.** "If you cannot sell a second product to... the people who bought your first one, then there is something wrong with your methods or your product." A magazine that cannot renew "somewhere near half its subscribers is headed for a fall."
- **Renewals.** Rely less on notices that a subscription is expiring and more on re-selling what the coming issues will do; this about doubled renewals. Renewal openers:
  - "Just a Few Weeks More, and Then... Your Subscription Expires!"
  - "Is your name and address, as given above, correct?... so that we may prove or correct our stencil," which keeps the name on the list without the loss of a single issue.
  - "What size hat do you wear?"
  - "It doesn't matter if you have already taken it for the six years—YOU NEED TO READ THIS NEW SYSTEM!"

  Two of a five-letter series renewed 20% of expiring trial subscriptions.
- **Follow-up series.** Each letter starts where the last left off. The middle letters reiterate in different words and pictures. The last is "the old standby—the last-chance offer": it plays up the saving, repeats the gains of acting now and the losses of delay, says no money and no risk, and makes ordering easy. A long four-page mopping-up letter pulled 2% after the previous letter's 1%; follow-ups six to eight were fruitless.
- **Turn trial takers into subscribers** with a series that re-sells the full term. The Mind, Inc. follow-ups were each "a sales letter in itself."

## Campaigns Collier reports

- **Harvard Classics (Collier's, 1913).** Six earlier attempts at a mail department had failed; one man spent $25,000 and sold eighteen sets. The letter that worked was the Dr. Eliot hypothetical opening. It sold culture to the man without college ("Perhaps he is nearer the standard of his college friends than he had thought"), the ease of fifteen minutes a day, and free trial. The circular contrasted a heap of books hiding the Harvard buildings with the orderly Five-Foot Shelf, under Emerson's line about a man who might read fifty years "yet die in the first alcove." Collier's rule from it: "a good circular will add from 25 to 33 percent to the pulling power of almost any letter." First-six-month profits ran 34½%.
- **O. Henry (Review of Reviews).** The sales were falling off, and Collier was told to push other products. His letter assumed the reader already knew O. Henry and opened on the bargain and a premium. Over 50,000 sets, more than $1,000,000, sold in twelve months; the Efficiency course sold under $150,000. Another letter ran a contest for the best letter on the reader's favorite story. Its purpose was to find out "what it is about O. Henry that appeals," and it quoted a superintendent of schools: "Nobody will come to meals."
- **Wells' Outline of History.** A test letter built on the reviews disappointed. Rebuilt on the prehistoric features with the 10-day Option, the offer sold 90,000 sets in four months against a two-year contract for 83,000; 60,000 more sold that year in a separate edition. Ads paid only once the circular's "The Oldest Man in the World" pages were made into ads. In total over 360,000 sets sold in three years, the last two letters adding 100,000 after 250,000. "The particular form that your letter takes is far less important than the chord it happens to strike."
- **Simonds' History of the World War.** 5,000 sets had sold in two years before the True Stories premium; about 70,000 sold in the next two.
- **Kipling.** 5,000 leftover sets sold in a few weeks, about $150,000, on a "long winter evenings" letter. It teased specific stories and said paper was scarce.
- **New Process turnaround.** Personalization by state failed: the letters needed nearly 1½% to profit and got under ½ of 1%. On June 1 the house was $80,000 in the red with a staggering inventory. Collier moved the mailings to book-buyer lists and used the Special Privilege Card, the favor letter, the reason-why ulster letter and the damaged-set letter. By December 31 it showed $121,000 net profit. This proved "the best mail order buyer is one who has already bought other products by mail, and that it is ideas that sell goods—not mere descriptions of the goods themselves."
- **The Secret of the Ages** sold about 300,000 orders, plus 30,000 in combination with a magazine.
- **The Business Week (McGraw-Hill).** The goal was 75,000 high executives of firms rated $75,000 and better, out of 200,000. Six letters were tested. One spoke as the magazine applying for a job: "If any one of your employees has MY job..." with "My salary?... 10 cents a week." Another used the "Unpardonable Sin" of complacency, another a productivity yardstick. They pulled 9% and 8% on outside lists, which the client ruled out, and 2-3% on executives. An introductory 17 weeks for $1 reached 7% on the executive list.
- **A dress shop from $20 a week.** A resident buyer's assistant with $3,000 of backing had to stock, pay rent and live on it. He spent $20 a week on 1,000 third-class letters to business girls. The reason why: as resident buyers for a dozen out-of-town stores, they pick up the manufacturers' single samples and odd lots below wholesale. The letters brought $200-300 a week from the first week. By seven months he had covered all expenses plus $18 profit; by the end of the year he did $1,000 a week, which had taken the old firm ten years. Then he opened a second and a third office. Sales letters to the customer list ("'Name your own price,' said the manufacturer. And we did.") were even more productive. "Any one who has something to sell can use the mails in one way or another."
