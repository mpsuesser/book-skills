# Representational systems, submodalities and the swish pattern

## Representational systems

The colloquial "modality" comes from NLP's **representational systems**: the sensory channels through which we represent the world. The usual set is **VAKOG**: Visual, Auditory, Kinesthetic, Olfactory, Gustatory. NLP's **"4 tuple"** combines olfactory and gustatory into one channel. The idea partly descends from Fritz Perls's Gestalt therapy, a "here and now" therapy in which a problem is solved in the present through full awareness, and sensory perception is part of that awareness. "Feeling" there covers emotion as well as the bodily sensations that come with emotion.

**What to discard.** Diagnosing a "primary modality" (a "preferred representational system") is refuted and limiting, and it pigeonholes the senses. A swinging pocket watch isn't simply a "visual" induction. It also has the kinesthetics of eyes and head moving, spatial elements, sounds, and internal memories.

### Sensory predicates

Predicates (verbs, adverbs, adjectives) suggest the modality someone is using *in that moment*:

- Visual: "shiny and red".
- Auditory: sounds, loud, ringing.
- Kinesthetic: rough, feels, solid.

Listen for descriptive words, especially descriptions of sensory experience. A subject who loves how your eyes look is processing visually, and specifically through eye contact. That tells you (a) how they are thinking now and (b) which ingredients you have to work with. A hypnotized partner murmuring "It's fuzzy, and I feel so far away, and everything is dark..." is having kinesthetic and visual experiences, and has to turn attention to those senses to report them. You now know where their attention is, so deepen it or explore more attributes of those senses.

Two limits. "I *hear* what you're saying" or "a *sunny* personality" may be cultural turns of phrase, so always ask why a person is using those words. And language is incomplete: talk about your voice doesn't mean they aren't processing everything else too.

### Matching modal language

Matching tone and word choice builds rapport, and sensory predicates show where you can meet someone. Overdone in a new partnership it comes off "unnatural and skeevy", so find a balance. Matching means working actively with their processes, not echoing their exact words. If they say being under your control feels like a buzz that's hard to describe, go there: "We both know that my power over you transcends words... something that your body knows innately, like a high..." Encourage partners to be feedback-oriented and verbally responsive, in trance and out. Hearing how they processed a film they loved or a fond memory teaches you how to build strong memories for them.

### Be extensive

Bandler advises matching the primary system first, then overlapping into all the others. The premise is outdated, but the principle holds: make experiences rich.

- **Across senses.** For a hallucinated sexy cage, give the metal's feel, the view through the bars, the rattle when they struggle. Not every suggestion needs every sense.
- **Zoom in on one moment.** Eye contact has visual fixation, but also the feel of the eyes, focusing and unfocusing, the emotion of intimacy showing up as bodily sensation, and an awareness of silence or breath. Consider the **negative space** too: the gaps in an experience. Narrow them, or widen perception?
- **External and internal channels.** You choose how to describe the scene (external), and you can also describe how they interpret and feel it (internal).
- **When a suggestion won't land** (orgasm on command, say), work its accompaniments: the sounds they'd make, vision dimming and turning unimportant, the emotional or mental climax, the tensing, what the pleasure actually feels like, what is around them, what goes on in their head.
- **Leave room.** Describe a forest and they will spontaneously supply detail. Don't over-specify suggestions.

### Switching systems

NLP changes which system someone processes through in order to give them new options. In therapy, a rejection that is only *felt* gets visualized, then the visual fades. In play: they love your voice, so pace it ("Hearing my voice to the exclusion of all else...") and then switch ("It's almost like my voice is a physical sensation, going into your ears, making your brain melt away..."). You haven't taken the sound away. You've given them more ways to experience it, and you can say so: the more ways they experience you, the easier it is to fall under your spell.

A switch can also mark a change of tone. Going from dark visual brainwashing (seeing themselves on their knees, looking up at you) to bimbofication, you can stay visual (edges fuzzing as if a little drunk, a pink tint over their thoughts) or switch to kinesthetic (their sex drive taking over, their IQ dropping as they're aroused). The shift of sense emphasizes the shift of play. There are no hard rules.

### Gestalt contact

Perls: experience occurs at the boundary between organism and environment, and "contact" is "the simplest and first reality." The **contact boundary** is wherever contact happens, concrete (skin meeting air) or abstract (the felt closeness of lovers). A therapist worries when a client is "out of touch" with parts of themselves. In play, create and move contact between abstractions. Name the concept that holds their attraction and sexuality, and all the ways it makes them feel, so it becomes something they can sense. Then shift its contact: they feel that concept moving closer to you, until you are entwined with how they experience attraction. A lighter version is contact between their brain and your words, and what that boundary feels like. Framing an abstraction as sensory gives both partners ways to explore and describe it.

## Submodalities

A **submodality** describes the qualities, the qualia, of a sense: visual brightness, color, sharpness, motion. Bandler and Todd Epstein developed the idea while asking why two people with similar ideas and strategies get very different results. They called it **"pragmagraphics"** (pragmatics plus holography). Submodalities began as a tool for changing how people feel about events or beliefs. A daunting project pictured bright and intense, or far and fuzzy, can be dimmed so it isn't blinding, or brought closer and into focus so it seems within reach. As a model of sensory experience, submodalities are more useful than the modalities they are built on.

### Submodalities are metaphors

The image a subject "sees" of their pleasure is a visual metaphor for the feeling. Alter the image (larger, brighter) and the feeling follows through the association. So the *change* is a metaphor too, and culturally "smaller to bigger" and "darker to brighter" both mean "more". Context decides direction, though: trance may read better as an image going darker or smaller, matching fading, eye closure and dulled senses. You can argue for your reading ("as you watch the image of yourself getting darker... dimming your consciousness..."), but the point isn't to dictate or to "get it right". Responses aren't binary either. Stay curious and permissively ambiguous ("when you see it get brighter, what kinds of things do you notice about how you feel?"), and other qualities will shift on their own: temperature, texture, emotion.

### The senses are one system

Senses we talk about separately are interconnected. In a gallery you still feel the floor and hear the room; you've tuned those out, not lost them. Under sensory deprivation, people imagine what they can't sense: blindfolded, they picture their partner or a sense of location. Sight has kinesthetic parts: eye and head movement, body position relative to what is seen, balance, the feelings that sights produce. The usual advice to engage all of a person's senses means internal and holistic aspects, not just external description:

- the feel of their own eyes as they stare into yours;
- the emotions in their body;
- your voice vibrating the air and their eardrums;
- the thrill in their spine.

"Emphasizing internal awareness is naturally hypnotic." Leaning into the physical and emotional feel of hearing you also teaches them to notice cross-sensory shifts, and they will start having them spontaneously.

### Basic form

Change the qualities of a suggestion, a feeling or a memory, and you change the experience.

1. **Identify the submodalities** through dialogue. For a shared erotic memory, ask: "What is the most vivid sense for you? What kinds of aspects of that sense really stand out?" Example questions ("Is it very dark, or bright?") lead: they will focus on, or fabricate, what you named. To avoid that, keep the questions general and read the submodalities out of the answers. "I can sort of see myself doing x" suggests a visually ill-defined image with some motion.
2. **Change the submodalities.** Do it as an *active* process ("I want you to make that memory look brighter"), as a *passive* one ("Watch as the memory gets brighter"; passive responsiveness adds D/s and control), or as *their choice* ("Take one aspect of your experience and play with it, change it however you like").
3. **Refocus on the whole.** Ask "Now how does it feel?" between shifts, so they notice the contrast.

### Changing beliefs

Beliefs are bound up with past experience. They drive behavior (believing you are good at hypnosis makes you confident), and they often come from generalization (one nipple-play orgasm, and now it is "possible"). In kink, change them for fun or brainwashing.

1. **Get the belief's submodalities**: "What does this belief feel like? If you had to visualize it, what would it look like? Does it have weight, texture, color, etc? What's the strongest one?"
2. **Get the submodalities of something uncertain**, something they have no strong opinion on, such as a flavor of ice cream they're indifferent to.
3. **Shift the old belief into the uncertain submodalities**, one at a time, which is more thorough: vividness fading, texture going dull. Active or passive.
4. **Introduce the new belief in the uncertain submodalities.** "Now imagine the sense of believing that I have total control over you when you're wearing my collar. To start with, it feels dull, and looks unclear..."
5. **Diminish the old belief further**, until thinking of it brings no feeling: "That old feeling of thinking you can resist me fades even more..."
6. **Give the new belief the old belief's submodalities**: senses sharpening, the image brightening, the texture intensifying.
7. **Future pace**: "When you imagine yourself wearing the collar later, what does it feel like?"

### The swish pattern

The swish pattern is Bandler's best-known submodality technique. The hypnotist makes a noise to mark a strong anchoring process that *replaces* one concept, feeling or experience with another.

1. **Identify the first concept's submodalities**, usually visual. For a baseline of not being turned on: "What does it feel like? If you were to picture it, what would it look like?" Traditionally the image goes into a frame, like a picture, which dissociates them from it.
2. **Identify the replacement's submodalities** (being horny), vividly imagined.
3. **Intensify the replacement**: tune it up, brighter and more vivid, "so easy to hold, something that sticks to you."
4. **Swish.** They picture the first concept, and on your signal it is replaced by the second. The frame makes the swap easier but isn't required. The traditional signal is a "whoosh" noise. Any distinctive sound works, but since you are building a trigger, pick one you don't use for other phenomena.
5. **Anchor several times, quickly.** Some versions have them close and open their eyes in time with each swap, adding a visual anchor.
6. **Future pace**: ask how it feels now to think of not being horny, then send them forward ("Can you imagine feeling like this later? Trying to recall what it feels like to not feel horny, but only finding pleasure and arousal?").
