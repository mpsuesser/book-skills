# The meta model, its updates, chunking and meta states

The meta model is a way to analyze someone's language linguistically: to find what they aren't saying (and what they are), and to ask the right questions. NLP calls the patterns **meta model "violations"** and the clarifying moves "challenges", which specify generalizations, recover deletions and clarify distortions.

How to use them in hypnokink:

- Hearing a pattern doesn't mean it's limiting. Use judgment about whether the incompleteness needs clarity or is simply useful hypnotically, and don't push on every sentence.
- NLP assumes a violation signals a negative limitation. A hypnokinkster treats it as an "interesting and enjoyable places to push someone": a chance to discover something, change a perspective, or collect more ingredients for patter.
- The templates are deliberately simplistic. Real speech mixes several patterns at once. What matters is the mindset of noticing how someone transforms their experience, not walking down a checklist or memorizing question forms.
- Asking and clarifying is itself hypnotic, because a question makes someone go inward and process. Some questions push a person further into their internal experience.
- It applies before, during and after a scene. A subject who says "I'm really fractionated" has a deep structure worth exploring. Do they want more? Is it a physical sensation? A change in their thinking? Do they feel slower?

## The original patterns

Each pattern below gives the cue, an example a subject might say, and the clarifying move.

**Generalizations**

- **Universal quantifiers**: always, never, every, nothing, all. These imply there is no alternative or exception. *"I never seem to go into trance."* Offer a counterexample ("Never? Even when you were watching that movie?"), or take the claim to a joking extreme.
- **Modal operators of necessity or probability**: must, can, should, will, and their negatives. These assume the speaker's actions are limited. *"I have to go deeper into trance..."* Challenge their surety ("How do you know that you have to? What's giving you that compulsion?"), or show them more options (in D/s, fewer): "What would happen if you did or didn't?"

**Deletions**

- **Simple deletion**: something is missing. *"It feels so good..."* Ask "What is making you feel so good? What do you feel that lets you know?" The answers give you information "you can use against them."
- **Lack of referential index**: an unspecified noun (they, people, those, that). *"It's really happening..."* Ask "What is it exactly that you are doing? What is happening?"
- **Comparative deletion**: more, most, better, less, worse, with no stated standard. *"It's just so much more intense!"* Ask "More intense compared to what?"
- **Unspecified verb**: an action that isn't solidly measurable. *"I feel like I'm not improving as a subject."* Ask "What does it mean to improve? What exact qualities does 'improving' have?"
- **Nominalization**: a noun that isn't a physical thing, often a verb turned into a noun. It can't be touched, standardized or measured. *"I want evil hypnosis."* Ask "What does 'evil hypnosis' feel like? How does an 'evil' person behave?"

**Distortions**

- **Mind reading**: assuming how someone else thinks, feels or will behave. *"I know you want me to be more expressive."* Ask "How do you know what I want?" or "What makes you think that?"
- **Lost performative**: a value judgment whose source is missing. *"It's important to have a blank mind in trance."* Ask "Who says that?" or "How do you know that the people who say that know it for a fact?"
- **Cause-effect**: if/then, because, makes, causes; a correlation presented as a connection. *"When my eyes are closed, I go really deep."* Challenge the link ("How do you know those two things are related?"), or explore its values: are there levels of closed, like heavy or fluttering? Does it carry over to other things the body does that you could harness to move that feeling?
- **Complex equivalence**: means, because, equals; two ideas treated as invariably connected. *"I'm really analytical, so I'm not very good at going into trance."* Ask "When did those two things start meaning the same thing?"
- **Presupposition**: part of the message has to be assumed true for the sentence to parse. *"I really want to go into trance!"* Answer from its deep structure: "Why do you assume that you aren't already in trance?"

## Extended patterns (Hall, from Korzybski's general semantics)

L. Michael Hall summarizes general semantics in three principles:

1. **Non-Identity**: you can't say "a thing is what it is" or "a word means what it means". Objectivity is unrealistic.
2. **Contradiction**: whether something "is not" something depends on the frame of reference, and is usually a matter of degree rather than yes or no.
3. **The Middles**: binaries barely exist; there are almost always shades of gray.

The patterns Hall added:

- **Over/under defined terms**: abstract quality words. They are over defined when taken at dictionary value as objective truth, and under defined because they don't tell the whole story. *"What's the best way to hypnotize someone?"* Ask "What kind of evidence would tell you that one way is 'best?'" or "Why do you think there is one 'best' method?"
- **Delusional verbal splits**: language that breaks a whole into parts, so the whole is lost ("body and mind", "my brain and I", "parts"). *"My kinesthetic stuff isn't as strong as my visual stuff."* Question the split, show the whole, or combine the parts: "Aren't there visual aspects to kinesthetic things and vice versa?"
- **Either/or phrases**: a kind of double bind that implies fewer options than exist. *"Should we do some bimbo play? Or should we do robots, instead?"* Look for other options or a gradient: "What if we did both?"
- **Multiordinality**: a nominalization so general it can apply to itself (love, thoughts, happiness). *"I'm stuck thinking about my thinking."* Ask "What are those thoughts like? How are they different from the thoughts you're thinking about?"
- **Static or signal words**: a nominalization presented as a definite, universal fact from an unknown source. *"This is just how being a subject is."* Ask "What do you mean by 'being a subject?' Are there times where that quality changes?"
- **Pseudo-words**: invented expressions with no objective referent. *"I feel totally empty, it's so good..."* Ask "What does emptiness feel like? What tells you that you're empty?"
- **Identification**: an equation that deletes the differences (is, are, were, am). *"I'm a challenging subject."* Ask "What specifically makes you challenging? Aren't there contexts and situations where that definition doesn't fit?"
- **Personalizing**: taking a statement as aimed at oneself, tied to self-image. *"They said that they don't like brats... They must not like playing with me."* Dig into the cause and effect, and offer alternatives and counter-evidence.
- **Metaphors**: comparisons that lose or distort information. *"I feel so foggy..."* Explore or deconstruct the metaphor: "What does that fogginess feel like or represent? What else do you feel? Are there other words that describe what you're experiencing?"

## Using the meta model hypnotically, and backwards

**Macro versus micro.** At the macro level, the meta model serves goals and change. At the micro level, it means discerning the qualities of the map. If the goal is turning a partner into a doll, the micro questions are these: How do they categorize being a doll? Is it inherently erotic, or how does it connect to eroticism? Which memories does it touch? What does "doll" mean in their different frames of reference? You also don't have to "fix" anything. Use their generalizations instead. A subject who says *"I feel... just like an object..."* after "You look like a doll" has generalized doll into object, which hands you a broad set of references to link. "Creating connections between 'frames' or contexts is an indispensable hypnotic tool." (Objects don't have will. A dolly is a specific, pretty kind of object that exists to be played with and looked at.)

**Worked scene: questioning as induction.** In the book's example, a negotiated video-chat scene opens with a subject who wants to feel very submissive.

1. The hypnotist asks what's driving the wish.
2. The subject says they haven't gone deep enough before. That is a comparative deletion plus a complex equivalence: submission is tied to depth.
3. The hypnotist shifts focus to depth: "how deep have you gotten?"
4. The hypnotist asks what "medium deep" feels like, and learns it is floaty, which pulls the subject into recalling trance.
5. The hypnotist reflects it back ("It feels floaty?"), going with their flow and clarifying the nominalization.
6. The hypnotist asks where in the body the feeling sits, building anchor points.
7. The hypnotist asks where they notice the word "submit", building a verbal anchor. The answer is a hot, tingly feeling.
8. The hypnotist has the subject compare that feeling with the floaty one, which makes them examine their own equivalence.
9. The hypnotist moves to direct suggestion ("And you feel kind of hot and tingly right now, right?").
10. The hypnotist rewrites the map: "So you must be in deep trance, then. Because you said you couldn't really be in medium trance while feeling like this."

From there the hypnotist can explore what "deep trance" (another nominalization) feels like, or dig further into the equivalence. The "diagnostic" tone is a flavor and a technique, not therapy.

## Chunking

**Chunking** is how we categorize information. *Chunk up* to a broader class (car to automobiles), *chunk down* to parts (steering wheel, seats), or *chunk laterally* to things with similar qualities (car to bus). Chunking isn't objective. Chunk a car up to "things that get you where you want to go" and a horse becomes a lateral chunk.

Questions for the hypnotist:

- What else belongs to this higher frame (object) that I can connect to?
- What smaller aspects (of dolls) can I use to narrow focus?
- What language are we both using that shifts what this represents?

Find a middle chunk: between "doll" and "object" sits "toy". Saying the word shifts the frame, and chunking down from it gives you material: toys have owners, exist for amusement, bring delight, are whimsical. There is no right or wrong way to chunk. "It's more about how well you can 'sell' it." You might get from doll to pet through a shared quality such as ownership, or by chunking up to a higher class of erotic activities.

## Meta states

Virginia Satir's classic pattern is "How do you feel about feeling that?" Going *up* a level changes the subject's reference point. "How do you feel about feeling excited?" sends them inward to make judgments: what they ought to feel, what the excitement is actually like, their identity and self-image. Hall reports Satir's view that any answer to that question articulates the person's "self-esteem". Bandler and Grinder add that changes at the level of self-esteem matter enormously, because self-image organizes the whole of a person's experience.

So the meta model *chunks down* to clarify, but you can also *chunk up* into meta states. Someone in a broader, more analytical frame about themselves is partly dissociated from the immediate reality, which makes change easier. This is especially useful in brainwashing: change their behavior, then change how they feel about the change. Because that works at the level of self-image, it can become "helpfully persistent".

## Words carry worlds

Robert Dilts: "A word is worth a thousand pictures." A single word ("peach") carries images, memories and categories that are unique to each person. Anchors and triggers are words built to evoke a specific response through association, but every word does this to some degree. That is why a person's words tell you so much about what they are processing.
