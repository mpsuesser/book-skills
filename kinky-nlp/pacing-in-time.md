# Future pacing and past pacing

**Future pacing** means having someone move forward in time and go through some future event in imagination, in trance or out of it, in a brief phrase or a full guided scene. In therapy it is rehearsal: a nervous speaker imagines preparing, doing well, and feeling good afterward. NLP builds many of its other techniques on it. In kink it serves:

- **Long-term brainwashing**: a future in which they are even more submissive and responsive to you.
- **One-off play**: fractionating quickly by taking them through the experience of an hour or more of going in and out of trance.
- **Triggers with "stick"**: a future in which they respond effortlessly.
- **Masochism and fear**: giving threats more weight.

## Basic outline

"The only truly necessary ingredient for future pacing is someone imagining the future." The full form has three movements. Skip or reorder them as fluency grows.

### 1. "You Are Here"

Establish when they are, as a baseline to travel from and return to. Either go with their flow, pacing how they are processing right now, or emphasize that they are in the present. Don't assume they are in the present. A subject being made sexually frustrated may be flashing back to past wanting, or to a story they read, and someone in dollspace may have a different baseline. One pacing line can carry all three tenses: "Right now, you're deep in your own desire, so focused on the feeling of wanting, maybe comparing this to other times you've wanted something strongly, maybe wondering if you're going to get what you need..." That anchors the present ("right now"), hedges toward the past ("other times"), and leads into the future ("wondering if"). It is a complete simple future pace on its own.

### 2. "You Are There"

"What if..." and "How do you think it will feel when..." are potent, because they require answers from the future. Decide how much *you* describe the future and how much *they* imagine it unprompted.

- **Follow the natural progression of their current state.** Frustration already asks "When or will I ever be relieved of this?"
- **Direct guidance.** "Maybe it will take a very long time before you find release. Can you imagine hours of your body yearning for pleasure?" Then flesh it out: time slowing when they need to cum, growing more desperate and more malleable, thirty minutes, an hour, two hours, an erotic sense of despair and powerlessness.
- **Ambiguous prompts.** "What would it be like to be held in frustration for so long? How would it feel, what would it do to you? How long are you imagining?" Answering aloud forces them to process the future in order to communicate it. Left internal, they may run through many options or fixate on one. Both work.
- **Associated or dissociated.** They may feel it happening to them, or watch it from a distance. Encourage either or both, or let it happen spontaneously.

Future pacing builds an emotional state or experience to anchor to, so emphasize how clear and vivid it is, how it is changing them, and how easy it is to hold and recall. You can do this conversationally. Framing the paces as questions opens a dialogue, and the more the two of you talk through a scenario, the more concrete it feels. This is especially useful outside trance, or with a partner who is still verbal.

### 3. "You Are Back (And It's Different)"

Return them to the present with their *now* changed. Any imaginative or intense experience shifts perspective, so this step is optional, but you can deliberately help the change along. Integration imagery:

- "keep it close to you as you wake up / return to the present";
- "a memory that weaves itself into your current experience";
- "a box that puts itself in your mind and shapes the way you behave".

This is also an opening for amnesia. Once they are back, emphasize the difference and anchor it to the present: "Doesn't that feel so much more vivid now?... you can feel the way that that brings you so much closer to breaking for me." Explaining what you did adds impact: "I messed with your brain by making it go through something that didn't even really happen, and you STILL are fucked up by it. Isn't that just so good?"

## Applications

- **Realistic future pacing.** This is rehearsal for what is likely to happen. Have them imagine being even more obedient to you and explore together the steps that lead there; it shows how they feel about it and makes it seem achievable. Later they live through the real event carrying the memory of the pace, which is concrete proof of your influence and lets you take credit for real changes. "Are you thinking about what it will be like when you find yourself automatically responding to a trigger for the first time?" intensifies that first response when it arrives.
- **Making triggers stick.** Triggers and anchors are associated responses, so stronger associations make them stronger. While building one, have them go through responding to it in different ways and situations. This broadens the association into a rich sense memory. For a voice-pleasure anchor: feel it now, then imagine hearing the voice later, tomorrow, far in the future, and how strongly it will hit, "even as the response changes over time it still is deep inside of you." Make allowance for the anchor's feel to shift; that is natural and still a good response. The same method suits complex persona anchors: dollification, robot play, bimbofication, pet play.
- **Sadistic pacing.** "What if" sets up a playful threat: "What if I made you babble like a dummy in public? Can you imagine the humiliation?" How realistic the scenario is changes the experience. For unlikely or pure-fantasy futures, it helps if the partner's disbelief is suspended, as in certain trance or dissociated spaces.
- **Presuppositions.** Imagining a future presupposes things about it, and time-based presuppositions carry a future pace. "Isn't it such a hot idea that when we go out to dinner, you'll still be helpless to me when I trigger you?" presupposes both the helplessness and the public play, and makes them imagine both. "When" implies certainty more than "if", though tone and framing matter more than the word, and an "if" in a convincing context reads as given. As a Trojan horse, the line also implies they are helplessly responsive *now*. They imagine the future and map it onto the present. Talking the scenario through (how they will respond on the date) "proves" the presupposition, because the whole conversation rests on it happening.

## Past pacing

Pace backward instead of forward.

- **"What if" in the past.** "Wouldn't it be amazing if you already had been deeply hypnotized for the last hour?" They travel back, imagine it, and return to the present adjusted. They come back with echoes of the imagined feeling, or with desire for it (or aversion and fear, if it was sadistic). It works like a parent's "You'd have so much time to relax if you'd already done the dishes!" Past pacing is **sneaky future pacing**: "Imagine if I'd brainwashed you years ago" leads them to wonder what years of it would make them. Combine the two: "What if you had spent the last year only masturbating about my power over you? Can you imagine how eager you'd be to think about that the next time you got off?"
- **Contrast and memory play.** Send them back to create juxtaposition: "Do you remember when you weren't feeling so sure about whether you'd like hypnosis? Look at you now, so needy for it, right?" The quick comparison puts them in the past and briefly dissociates them from the present, which is excellent for long-term conditioning. Dwell on a specific memory and they will feel more connected to their "past self", or more alienated from it, depending on your framing. The qualifier leads: "Doesn't that feel so far away?" versus the open "How far away does that feel to you, now?"
- **Memory is unreliable**, which makes it easy to play with, especially in trance. Feelings recalled from a year ago are already colored by everything since, and accurate recall is hard. That is part of why "resetting" someone to clear triggers or influence doesn't help. Point out the fuzzy details; there will always be some. Obscuring one memory gets a person used to the feeling of not remembering well. That opens the way to more amnesia or confusion, and you can anchor the sensation of reaching for something and not finding it into other suggestions. You can also alter their memory of the past, in part or whole. That carries risk and calls for delicacy and safety awareness. Changing how someone sees their past self has a big effect on their present.
