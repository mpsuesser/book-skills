# Milton model patterns, indirect language, hedges and presuppositions

The Milton model sums itself up this way: "Purposefully representing the world incompletely through language is conducive to achieving hypnotic trance." Erickson, in Stephen Gilligan's words, "so skillfully and consistently violated virtually every Meta-Model rule that it required the development of the 'Milton Model,' a sort of inverted pattern of the former." It aims to codify his "artfully vague" style. Remember that NLP's account is a step removed from Erickson himself; it is an interpretation through NLP's own lens.

## NLP's view of trance

Bandler and Grinder gave Erickson's method three steps:

1. "Pacing and distraction of the dominant (language) hemisphere"
2. "Utilization of the dominant hemisphere, language processing which occurs below the level of awareness"
3. "Accessing of the non-dominant hemisphere"

Read this outdated technobabble as the conscious/unconscious metaphor: get conscious thought moving in your direction and distract it, then use what happens outside awareness. Examples are the sense that a suggested action is just *happening to* the subject, or memories they aren't thinking about shaping the trance.

Bandler holds that we have different "patterns" for everything we do (working, watching a movie, talking), each analogous to a kind of trance, each with its own capabilities. Different intimate engagements produce different trances. Subjects may visualize more vividly, have spontaneous flashbacks, or focus differently. The hypnokink question is what someone's different processing lets you reach: new footholds to influence them, take advantage of them, brainwash them, or give them intense experiences. Finding hidden aspects and skills in a partner is sexy in its own right.

**Working mid-range.** You don't need to memorize the patterns. Ask instead:

- How can I communicate in a way that makes my partner process parts of my speech differently?
- How can I be ambiguous and lead them to make the assumptions I'm implying?
- Which part of them processes what, and which parts process without thinking about it?
- Where am I leading them, and what am I implying?

Make the surface structure vague enough that the subject finds their own deep structure. Their transderivational search is then a transformation of their perspective, their map, and not just of the message. In "Good bimbos babble their thoughts", "bimbos" is a generalization they apply to themselves, and "good" (who made that classification?) means they have to perform.

**Anatomy of one line.** "I learned that it's easy to go into a deep trance if you want it in a certain way."

- "I learned that..." presupposes an outside, implicitly authoritative source.
- "It's easy to... if you..." asserts causality.
- "go into a deep trance" is an embedded phrase that works as a suggestion on its own, especially with a subtle tone marker.
- "want it in a certain way" is ambiguous; it is easily parsed as "the way I'm feeling right now".

## The patterns, used for suggestion

The meta model's patterns, inverted:

- **Universal quantifiers** imply there are no exceptions. *"Every time you notice yourself making the action to pay attention, it becomes less and less conscious."*
- **Modal operators** assume something is needed or not needed. *"You don't have to do anything special to feel controlled."*
- **Lack of referential index** lets the subject identify with the statement indirectly. *"Helpless toys tend to lose themselves in daydreams."*
- **Comparative deletion** has the subject supply the standard. *"Are you noticing yourself feeling weaker?" "That's better now, isn't it?"*
- **Unspecified verb** has the subject fill in the blanks. *"All you need is a slight shift to surrender, and your mind can make that change now."*
- **Nominalization**: a noun that can't be put in a wheelbarrow. The subject supplies its qualities from their own experience. *"You can feel my control over you."*
- **Mind reading**: "I know", "it's obvious". If it is consistent with what you know of them, you needn't say how you know. *"I know how badly you want to go deep for me."*
- **Lost performative**: a value judgment with no source, which sounds authoritative. *"It's good to obey."*
- **Cause-effect**: if/then, because, makes. It is "a very important part of hypnotic language". It needn't be objectively true, but it works better when believable. *"You're already starting to go into trance just because you're letting my words into your head."*
- **Complex equivalence**: means, because, equals. It differs from cause-effect only in linking concepts rather than actions. *"I can see the muscles around your eyes softening; you're starting to give in."*
- **Presupposition**: part of the suggestion must be assumed true for it to parse (see below). *"As you go a little bit deeper for me, can you figure out which part of your mind is going to relax first?"*

Additional patterns:

- **Tag question**: "right?", "isn't it?". It signals that you want a response and extends the yes set. *"It's so sweet to submit, right?"* (See [binds-and-tag-questions.md](binds-and-tag-questions.md).)
- **Double bind**: a choice in which both options are desired outcomes, usually with a presupposition, giving an illusion of choice. *"Do you want to go deep into trance right now, or would you rather I tease you a little more first?"* (Also in binds-and-tag-questions.md.)
- **Embedded command**: a suggestion inside a suggestion, often marked by a subtle shift in tone or body language. It is the true message, or an added one, wrapped in packaging. *"I'm so happy you realized you want to* go deep into trance."
- **Conversational postulate**: a question that seems to need an answer but works as an instruction. *"Can you do me a favor and watch this crystal as I twirl it in front of your eyes?"*
- **Extended quote**: quotes nested inside quotes, like nested stories: a friend said a hypnotist told them that subjects find different depths when they shift focus. It confuses, and shifts frames of reference.
- **Selectional restriction**: personifying an inanimate thing. *"Are your eyes getting more and more obsessed with that spiral?"*
- **Factive (awareness) predicates**: using awareness to presume something is real. *"You must be noticing that your breathing has changed."*

## Direct versus indirect

Tacking "maybe" onto a suggestion is not indirection by itself. An indirectness ladder for one suggestion:

1. "Feel x." This is the imperative, the most direct form, used from a position of power.
2. "You feel x." Adding the subject softens it.
3. "You can feel x." This adds permission.
4. "Maybe you can feel x." This adds uncertainty, a hedge.
5. "Maybe you can feel a little bit x." Shrinking the feeling allows a broader range of response.
6. "I wonder if maybe you can feel a little bit x." The focal point moves to the hypnotist's perception, and the statement need not be parsed as true. It is so common in patter, though, that it rarely shifts attention on its own.
7. "My friend John felt a little bit x when he did what you're doing." This reframes the suggestion entirely.
8. "I don't know when you're going to start to feel x..." This is a classic Ericksonian presupposition: x will happen, and only *when* is open.

The ladder shows two mechanisms:

- **Packaging**: each added word softens a command into a suggestion "in the truest sense of the word", leaving something to unwrap. Claims that it "bypasses the conscious mind" are metaphor. At minimum, it sounds more conversational and polite.
- **Moving the focal point away from the subject**, which is close to meta states and chunking. "From what I see, you're going nice and deep into trance" makes them (a) assume it is your true experience and (b) process it from your point of view.

Spoken constantly, these forms sound stiff and rehearsed. Start from intent: to move focus onto yourself, say something like "I was thinking a lot about this because I found it really interesting..." Study how ordinary conversation softens speech and shifts focus. Tone and timing count too: an emphasized "I wonder", followed by a pause, works differently from a flowing one. Indirect *language* is not the same as indirect *hypnosis*. Indirectness depends on frame, psychological ecology and abstract concepts that words alone can't capture.

## Verbal hedges

A **hedge** is a word that calls a statement's certainty into question or makes it vaguer (maybe, sometimes, almost, relatively, seems like). It distances the speaker from the claim, and it reads as polite. "Might" can carry real uncertainty: "You might experience trance in a way that you're familiar with, or maybe you won't" offers ambiguity and two options. It can also be a hedge that isn't meant to be rejected: "I think you might find yourself responding as fully as you need to." Hedging makes the listener process *why* something is hedged. The statement's uncertainty can then become uncertainty or ambiguity in their response, which is useful: "Maybe there's almost a sort of dumbness around the edges of your mind."

Three kinds of hedge:

- **Minimizing the threshold** ("a little bit"): a small feeling is easy to match, so experience correlates closely with the suggestion. "A little bit of pleasure starting in your body" works where "intense pleasure" might not, which makes it a strong transition tool. *"Can you feel a little bit of that trance infecting your brain?"* ("Infecting" adds playful danger.)
- **Moving ownership of the information** ("I've been told"): the subject has to shift perspective. It presupposes that others have had the experience, so it is real, and the subject reaches that conclusion themselves. *"I've been told that turning into a doll can feel incredibly good."*
- **Softening a connection** ("just", "isn't it?"): it bridges concepts from different categories. *"Trance is just another kind of pattern we go through, isn't it?"*

## Presuppositions

A **presupposition** is part of an utterance that must be assumed true for the whole to make sense. "Do you think it will feel good when you go into trance?" presupposes trance. In suggestion, presuppositions get implicit buy-in through packaging, often by downplaying the presupposed content. The listener may or may not consciously hear the implication, but will reach some conclusion about it. That surface-to-deep transformation is the hypnotic opportunity.

### Forms

The forms below simplify the appendix of *Patterns*, Vol. I.

**Existence-based (simple):** language that implies something exists.

- Names: *"Sleepingirl wrote that hypnosis is really hot."*
- Pronouns: *"I saw them go into deep trance."*
- Descriptors: my partner, my friend.
- Nouns: *"I like pocket watches that tick loudly."*
- Quantifiers: some, each, every, few. *"Some of the responses you are having might seem new to you."*

**Time-based**, the most common in hypnosis:

- Subordinate clauses of time: before, after, when, during, while, as. *"...while you go into trance."*
- Change-of-time verbs and adverbs: start, continue, begin, end, stop, yet, still, anymore. "Continue to give in" presupposes giving in has begun.
- Cleft sentences: "it is x that...". *"It's the way your body responds to me that makes you really crazy."*
- Ordinal markers: first, second, lastly. "The last thing you have to do..." presupposes the rest is done.
- Repetitive cue words: again, also, too. "Feel that little switch again."
- Repetitive verbs and adverbs: return, repeat, restore. *"Do you hear my words repeating in your cute little head?"*

**Quality-based:**

- Relative clauses: "Lots of the feelings that you are getting are just simple signs of deep trance."
- Comparatives: more, less, -er. "Even more suggestible now" presupposes they were suggestible before.
- Comparative "as": "If it's as easy as putting you into trance..."
- Qualifiers: only, just, except. *"Do you think anyone can control you this easily, or just me?"*
- Change-of-place verbs: come, go, left, arrived. *"Your thoughts have left the part of your brain that processes them consciously."*
- Change-of-state verbs: transform, become. *"Are you turning into someone who can't help themselves?"*
- Awareness words: realize, notice, aware. *"Did you notice the way that your muscles let go...?"*
- Commentary words: interesting, happily, slowly, easy. *"I find it so fascinating how you eagerly obey me."*

### Using them

- **Hard to disagree with.** "Are you thinking about which part of you will go into trance first?" layers three things: a question of awareness (the focus), trance coming in parts, and trance happening at all. Answering yes or no leaves the presupposition standing. Refuting it means parsing the whole and breaking conversational convention.
- **The gut check still rules.** An internal "Wait, I'm not going to go into trance" matters whether or not they voice it. So presuppose what tracks with what the subject expects or is open to, which is pacing again. Context decides.
- **Packaging the underlying message.** For arousal, you can package it as existence ("You can feel some of that arousal"), as timing ("Before you feel arousal", "how that arousal starts to flood your mind"), or as awareness and quality ("Do you notice the arousal", "that insistent arousal"). Presupposing is misdirection, a **Trojan horse**: "You will feel arousal" stops being the focal point. Track where the emphasis and attention actually fall.
- **Practice by spotting them** in ordinary speech, your own and other people's, and in writing, sorting each one into existence, time or quality. The way you presuppose in normal conversation is the most natural way to presuppose in hypnosis.

### Non-sneaky presuppositions

NLP stresses that presuppositions go unnoticed. Hypnokink partners often notice them, and they don't lose potency when noticed: "sneaky hypnotic language" is part of the fantasy and the trope. A "...yet" at the end of a tease earns a bright smile because it celebrates a shared grammar. If a subject is frustrated that they "see it coming", reframe it as connection, or as a chance for their brain to hyperfocus and loop on the language. You can *emphasize* presupposed content instead of hiding it: with tone, pauses or touch, or out loud ("Did you notice the fun language I'm using to get your brain all excited?"). You can even explain how it works: "So nice that I said 'when,' isn't it? So you immediately focus on anticipating..." The presupposition then becomes the place you direct attention, which may hide other parts of the suggestion or make them fixate on the whole of it. Watch which happens and use their brain "against" them.

### Compound suggestions

Erickson stacked presuppositions ("to confuse the conscious mind", a metaphorical explanation), in a kind of shotgun approach. A dissected example: "When you finally realize that you're slowly, helplessly turning into a doll, it's because of that part of you that is accepting my words just right..."

- "When you finally realize" presupposes the realization will come.
- "Realize" presupposes the transformation is already happening; only their awareness of it is in question.
- "Slowly, helplessly" is quality commentary on the transformation.
- "Turning into" presupposes change (they weren't a doll before).
- "That part of you that is accepting" presupposes a part exists.
- "When... it's because" is cause-effect.
- "Part of you" is a nominalization.
- "Accepting my words" is an unspecified verb.
- "That part" (which part?) and "just right" (how?) are ambiguous, which covers your bases and makes the line easy to identify with.

There are no rules for how to conceptualize a suggestion. Some of this analysis is needlessly complex theorizing and some is useful introspection, so take what helps.
