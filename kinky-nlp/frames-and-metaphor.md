# Frames, reframing, conceptual metaphor and hypnotic metaphor

## Frames

Robert Dilts: "A psychological 'frame' refers to a general focus or direction that provides an overall guidance for thoughts and actions during an interaction." A frame is the context of an experience: the motivations, beliefs, values and histories through which the experience is perceived. Like a physical frame, it puts clear boundaries around its contents. NLP separates **primary experience** (sensory intake) from **secondary experience** (the verbal and symbolic map). It also separates perceiving what is outside from producing experience inside (thoughts, daydreams, fantasies).

Any experience prompts a search for meaning, some of it conscious and some gut. Because no experience is objective, the frame defines the event, when it happens and again each time it is recalled.

When you frame a suggestion, you decide what is in it. "That feeling of your eyes fluttering is such a good anchor for you to know that you're going into trance" frames a sensation as success inside a shared wish for trance. "Failing" isn't in the frame, and distraction goes unmentioned.

### Components of a frame

- **Time**: when the experience is processed. Going deeper *now* carries immediacy, desire or hesitancy. As something that *will* happen, it carries longing, anticipation and curiosity. As something that *has* happened, it carries nostalgia, forgetfulness or a strong sense memory. Telling someone they are *about* to experience something adds anticipation, a precipice, compared with telling them they *are* experiencing it.
- **Good versus bad**: success or failure, supposed to happen or not, productive or not. Shifting to get comfortable can be framed as a "failure" (they broke trance; they "shouldn't need to move"), or as an opportunity to refocus and go deeper.
- **Who**: whose eyes the experience is seen through. Their own, or their partner's, society's, or the hypnosis community's? A subject who drops in moments may wonder whether you'll think they're too easy, or whether it will excite you. You can direct them into any of these viewpoints.
- **Meta/logical level**: how close the experience or suggestion sits to them (below).

### Logical levels

The idea comes from Bateson: a category sits at a higher logical level than its members ("flower" above a Grandiflora rose). Dilts adapted it as **neuro-logical levels**, which answer "How close is this information to me, personally?" Compare "What you did was hot" with "You are hot". Dilts's "ABCs":

| Level | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A. Who I **A**m (identity, closest) | Who? | "You are sexy." |
| B. My **B**eliefs (values, reasons) | Why? | "The reason why you go into trance is sexy." |
| C. My **C**apabilities | How? | "The ways that make you able to go into trance are sexy." |
| D. What I **D**o/Have **D**one | What? | "Going into trance is sexy." |
| E. My **E**nvironment (furthest) | Where/when? | "This moment in which you're going into trance is sexy." |

Higher levels contain the lower ones, and the levels interact. People take comments on what they *do* personally, as comments on who they *are*. Comments on who they are prompt "What does that mean about my behavior or beliefs?" For objectification, the same suggestion can run through every level: "This is the perfect place for an object to be", "You are acting exactly like a doll", "Your thoughts are just object thoughts", "You are an object." It pays to be thorough across the levels, and to save the most direct "You are x" for when the subject is already feeling the others.

## Reframing

**Reframing** is purposefully changing the reference point, or aspects of how someone understands something, which changes its meaning and how they process it. Therapy reframes bad as good. Hypnokink reframes good as better, one perspective as another, or (sadistically) good as bad. Dilts: "The heart of reframing is to make the distinction between the intention [...] and the behavior." Attach a different intention or context to the same act and it feels different. Change the frame and you change the meaning.

Example: a regular play date gets extra significance from "We're so used to getting time with each other, but... we're so lucky that this is something that we're able to have. Did you ever think that you'd get to go into such profound trance so often? That you'd be so weak to it?" That line shifts the *time* frame (back to before this was routine). Its "weakness" also praises their capabilities level in sexy disguise.

Make it a habit to ask which frame someone is looking through (time, good vs bad, who, logical level, and any other attribute you can name). Then use these linguistic tools:

- **Conjunction reframe.** Swap the connector (and, but, although, while, so, even though). "You're noticing your heavy body, *and* you're also noticing my voice filling your head" gives the two equal weight. "...*but* you're also noticing my voice..." foregrounds the second and leads away from the first, which suits a transition. Try different conjunctions and feel the change.
- **One word reframe.** Take one word (usually a descriptive one), list its near-synonyms, and substitute. Synonyms are never exact. For "a little nervous", the list might be anticipatory, tense, shy, fearful, excited. "So that means you're just *anticipating* what's coming, right?" makes it neutral or positive; "Oh, are you feeling *scared*?" is playful intimidation. Chain the substitutions so the frame moves step by step: *nervous* → *anticipating* → *excited* → *thrilling*.
- **Comparison/similarity reframe**, for drastic moves. List the attributes of the current frame and of the target frame, find or reframe their way to overlapping attributes, and bridge through them. To take consensual play toward a "forced" feel, consensual attributes include consent, care, attention, respect, enjoyment, letting go, excitement, obedience and permission. Forced attributes include weakness, resistance, obedience, surrender, losing, manipulated, violation, loss of control and excitement. Obedience and excitement overlap, so: "Doesn't the *care* I'm giving you feel a little *dark*? Isn't your *excitement* kind of *betraying* you, how *weak* you are to this?" You don't need to name the starting frame; they are in it. If you aren't sure of their exact perspective, talk only about the target frame and leave the connection implied, which is also more ambiguous.

## Conceptual metaphor

For this book, a **metaphor** is anything that shows a relationship between two things. The simile distinction doesn't matter, and stories and symbolism (allegory, Aesop) count. Metaphors clarify, but more importantly they expand our perception of the original idea. In "Your love's the rain / My heart, the flower" ("Água de Beber"), each listener brings their own flower associations (blooms, beautiful, dies untended, wild or cultivated) and comes away with more ways to describe both things. Metaphors can be *dynamic*, moving in parallel with a story ("Will it wither and fade / Or bloom to the sky?"). The listener already knows the trajectory, which the teller can rely on or use as misdirection.

Lakoff and Johnson, *Metaphors We Live By*: "Our ordinary conceptual system, in terms of which we both think and act, is fundamentally metaphorical in nature." We understand argument *through* war (win, attack, defend, shoot down). The two domains are mapped onto each other by culture and by individual history.

The types:

- **Structural**: one concept structured like another. Argument is war. Time is money (spend, waste, worth).
- **Orientational**: spatial and bodily, and varying by culture. Happy is up. Future and progress are forward. Status is up.
- **Ontological**: giving substance to the insubstantial, as with NLP's nominalization. The mind is a machine (not working, gears spinning, "programming" someone). The mind is a brittle object (breaking a mind, fragile thoughts).
- **Boundary/container**: giving a concept edges, an inside and an outside (compare Gestalt contact). Before, during and after an activity. Inside the forest. Going *into*, entering, coming *out of* trance.
- **Personification**: the book "told" me; my computer is "dying"; the unconscious "keeps me safe", "knows better than I do".
- **Metonymy**: one thing standing in for another. Part for whole ("a pretty face"). Creator for work ("I enjoy reading sleepingirl"). Place for people ("the class said").

**Metaphor in hypnosis.** Effective suggestion connects concepts, and those connections run on existing metaphorical associations. The most pervasive is *trance is down*: unconsciousness is down (lying down to sleep, "deep" sleep), so depth, sinking, counting down, downward touch, heaviness. Floating or flying asks the subject to buy a *new* metaphor, so it needs texture. "You might feel like you're floating" in a vacuum is thin. Instead, acknowledge the accepted down (pacing), then offer up: sometimes trance is weighty, sometimes weightless, as though it has disconnected feeling from body and their consciousness floats in a vast, liquid space. Making abstract "trance" concrete this way hits a sweet spot: partly abstract still, but with enough purchase to hold on to.

**Compound and connecting metaphors (scene flow).** Every metaphor opens more metaphors. Take "Your brain feels fuzzy". What else is fuzzy: a blanket, a TV screen, drunken vision, a cat? Commit to one ("as though someone's put an obscuring lens over it") and extend it ("And just like that lens is over your mind, you find it covers your other senses too as your hearing dulls, your vision blurs..."), and new branches appear. Work backwards from a goal as well. For bimbofication, list the qualities involved (transformation, intelligence draining, consensual misogyny, time) and find concepts that share them: a caterpillar becoming a butterfly, metamorphosis inside a cocoon or mummification. They needn't be poetic; this is like chunking.

**Metaphors are faulty.** Every comparison loses or transforms information, so a metaphor can expand in the wrong direction or narrow your view. Trance-as-container misleads: subjects are often in trance before anyone realizes, still in it after "waking", and in/out may not matter for the play at all. Taking mind-as-machine literally leaves people confused when suggestions are taken up in more nuanced ways than input and output. Personifying the unconscious has the safety problem covered in SKILL.md. Different metaphors highlight different qualities, so use several and stay flexible.

## Hypnotic metaphor

Mapping hypnosis onto day and night (the sun always sets, the moon rises) sets an expectation that trance will definitely come, and that they will definitely come back out. Therapeutic metaphors (Erickson revived them in the early-to-mid 1900s) are goal-oriented: an insight for a problem, like the persevering athlete told to a smoker who wants to quit. You can eroticize that, but you needn't stop there: "the only 'goal' in kink is to have an enjoyable experience."

**TDS without "the" unconscious.** Hearing a metaphor triggers a transderivational search: the listener goes inward to produce a meaning, often after a delay, so leave space. NLP says this "bypasses consciousness". It doesn't have to; people consciously work out metaphors (high-school English, *Lord of the Flies*). There is a gut response first, with associations from history, language and culture, and that drives further conscious processing, which is useful in trance too. A metaphor can always be expanded further.

### Constructing a metaphor

Treat this as a guideline, for a one-line metaphor or a full story.

1. **Search for symbols.** A symbol is any aspect of a concept that can stand for something else; descending stairs stands for going deeper.
   - Latch onto the concept you want to represent. It can be broad (trance, submission, comfort, fear, pleasure, pain) or specific (transformative objectification, intense devotion, the sensation of mindlessness, being too dumb to read). Choose by mutual desire, or on a whim as the trance unfolds.
   - Draw symbols from the partner's own history and hobbies (utilization); shared narrative carries extra power. If they love nature walks, play there.
   - Inventory the scenario: temperature, weather, the sights, sounds and smells of nature, walking itself (automatic or deliberate, muscles working and tiring), time passing, enjoyment.
2. **Consider direction.** Scenes involve change: awake to trance, smart to dumb, moving to still, brainwashed to more brainwashed. A general sense of direction is enough. Find the before/after built into your ingredients (the walk begins in anticipation, curiosity and readiness, and ends in satisfaction, tiredness and new knowledge) and make it parallel the play's journey. A metaphor shares the play's narrative arc (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution). The six common story shapes work as templates:
   - Rags to riches: a rise.
   - Riches to rags: a fall.
   - Icarus: a rise, then a fall.
   - Oedipus: a fall, a rise, a fall.
   - Cinderella: a rise, a fall, a rise.
   - Man in a hole: a fall, then a rise.

   A Cinderella-shaped walk would see interest rise, fall into distraction, and rise again with renewed focus. Map that onto what they are experiencing.
3. **Sell your metaphor.** NLP holds that the best metaphors are processed unconsciously. That axiom is "false, but not useless". To steer the meaning, or for partners (neurodivergent or otherwise) who find metaphorical ambiguity hard, say it outright. "This reminds me of..." are near-magic words; then explain *why*. For example: your descent into brainwashing is like your nature walks, setting out with expectations, always finding something new, and coming back changed with new memories. Connect specific symbols: in a new place you notice your senses differently, the trees, the ground, the sky through the canopy. Then press it home: "Doesn't that seem familiar right now?... you're not the same person who walked into the forest." Telling versus not telling is a gradient. Starting explicit and growing vaguer works well, and they will make connections you never stated.
4. **Change the metaphor over time.** Each symbol has qualities that can shift (bigger or smaller, brighter or darker, louder or quieter, a walk that gets stronger or fades, time running faster or slower), and the shift is itself symbolic. Forest imagery sharpening and brightening can mean growing perception of their own submission. In intelligence reduction, their senses might dull. They could just as well heighten, since someone no longer thinking might focus on the physical. Anything explainable works, and you can always sell the allegory. Submodalities feed this.
5. **Flow.** All of the above can be done responsively, like starting to drive with no destination and taking cues from the road. Pick a topic and watch their responses for elements to use as symbols. A painting reveals itself over time: what else does? Ask your partner. Vary it: a painter who can't see the work until it's finished, or one who sees it all but no one else can. Then consider what changes along the way: lost time, a creative surge, the painter's emotions, an erotic painting. "Thinking out loud" is legitimate patter, and it surfaces nuances neither of you had considered.
6. **End on insight.** For an exit or punctuation, circle back to the aspect you most want to highlight. "Your brain made all of those connections about that idea of them working on something important, things you might not even be fully aware of right now, but things that changed your past and future experience." You can spell out your own reading (the painter takes time with the art, you take time with your submission, I take time with you), but leave them room for their own connections. You don't need to know everything they derived.
