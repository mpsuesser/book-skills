# Double binds and tag questions

## Two kinds of double bind

NLP borrowed the double bind from Gregory Bateson, who worked on schizophrenia with colleagues including Jay Haley (*Toward a Theory of Schizophrenia*, 1956), and distorted it. Hypnokink then synthesized both versions. The distinction to hold: **"A Bateson bind is a scenario where there is no perceived correct response, and an NLP bind is a scenario where all perceived responses are correct."**

### The Bateson double bind

A Bateson bind leaves someone stuck "between a rock and a hard place": contradictory messages, and no clear right action. An example is affection rebuffed while further engagement is encouraged. The ingredients:

- **Two or more people**, one of them under the influence, authority or superiority of the other(s). Parent over child is classic, but a child can bind a parent who feels they lack control.
- **Patterns, not a single event.** The person experiences being stuck as a habit, so it can't be resolved as a one-off.
- **A primary injunction**: there is a "right" thing to do, and failing brings "punishment" (verbal, withdrawal of attention, stigma). It takes the form "Don't do this, or I'll punish you" or "If you don't do this, I'll punish you". Example: a boss's end-of-day deadline.
- **A secondary (sometimes tertiary) injunction** that conflicts with the first, so every way of complying fails something else. Example: the boss piles on extra work as "acknowledgment of your skill", so avoiding more work means defying "Do the work or you will be punished".
- **An important relationship** the person won't jeopardize, combined with an inability to comment on the paradox. Some people experience their whole world as no-win.
- **Opacity**: the person may not see what is binding them. A child under an unspoken rule never to question a parent, who watches the parent do wrong, can feel paralyzed without knowing why.

Bateson binds can be wholly circumstantial or nonverbal. Their hallmark is the *feeling of being trapped*.

Bateson cites an Erickson seminar. A chain smoker was repeatedly offered a cigarette, but each time Erickson "inadvertently" turned away to answer a question and withdrew it. The ingredients were present: an important relationship, contradictory messages (giving and taking away), and comment blocked (a seminar was going on, and it was all "inadvertent"). The outcome was amnesia ("What cigarette?") and a reversal from "He doesn't give" to "I don't want." The amnesia here means a changed perception of the event, the typical way people cope with being bound. Feeling stuck, rolling something over, flipping focus between inside and out: all of these are hypnotic patterns.

### The NLP double bind

The NLP bind is linguistic and leans heavily on presupposition: "Do you want to go into the deepest trance by looking at my eyes, or focusing on my pocket watch?" Its traits:

- It is often a question with "or": "Are you ready to go deeper, or are you ready for something more intense?"
- It offers a real or perceived choice of two while downplaying the others. "Now, or after dinner?" leaves out "not at all" and "another day".
- It can be rejected if it isn't a true bind ("Can it wait until tomorrow?").
- It aims at one outcome chosen by the operator. In "A quick trance or a long trance?", trance happens either way.
- It can be "unconscious", ambiguous enough that it can't truly be answered consciously: "I wonder if your feet will go into trance before your head, or vice versa..." Strengthen this by changing perspective ("I" vs "you", "I wonder..." vs "Do you think...", "Who knows if...").
- It is often composed with other Milton patterns: "You'll be a great subject if you listen really carefully, or if you let my suggestions float in unnoticed..." That line adds if/then causality and presuppositions.

**Binds of other sizes.** "Would you like to go into trance now?" is a *single* bind: a positive answer binds the subject to the action. A bind can ride a yes set or a cause-effect: "Do you think that going deeper into trance like you are right now means that I'm weakening your will?" A *triple* bind: "...now, or later, or would you like me to choose?"

**Generating binds.** Take the goal (trance) and break it into the situation's variables, then offer pairs:

- Position: sitting up or lying down.
- Timing: immediately or in a moment.
- Body: eyes open or closed; hands in your lap or hanging down.
- Props: a watch or a spiral.
- Feeling: excited or pleasantly nervous; aroused or too deep to be turned on.
- Attention: my voice or my eyes; the left ear or the right.
- Environment, who is involved, what you're doing.

## Splicing the two

**Choice and no choice.** A Bateson bind needs the trapped feeling; an NLP bind relies on sneakiness and on the subject feeling that they chose. One move can be both. Ask "Are you ready for me to fuck up your mind, now, or do you need a break?", get a blushing "now", and then *don't* signal that hypnosis has started. The subject can't tell whether it is happening, and won't want to ask. Their continual checking becomes hypnotic, and the sneaky hypnotist uses it. The trope reversed works too: saying "Don't go into trance..." while swinging a pocket watch. In hypnokink there should never be risk of real punishment or disappointment, so these are *playful* Bateson binds. Any incongruent messages and expectations qualify:

- Getting a bimbo to act smart.
- Forbidding orgasm with the "punishment" left unclear.
- Calling self-brainwashing dangerous while rewarding each step toward it.

**Implications.** Bateson treats the history, environment and mindset behind a bind as just as important as its wording. In "If you loved me, I wouldn't have to ask you to do this", the unspoken history (a pattern of never letting the partner prove otherwise, a childhood full of nagging) is part of the bind. Borrow that for healthy binds: reduce the verbal content and leave it implied. On a time-limited video call, "Well, got to fuck your shit up at some point..." leaves the subject wondering when. Not acting on the remark, or changing the subject, creates the Bateson element. "How badly do you want it?" then presupposes desire and urgency. The hidden option ("I don't want it") isn't even downplayed, but in context the answers run from "kind of badly" to "really badly" (unless they're going for bratty).

## The hypnokink bind

There is a third perspective. Erotic hypnotists almost *expect* their partners to recognize a bind, because that's part of the fun. You can usually read recognition in a knowing smile or a furrowed brow. The bind is then fully voluntary, informed and consented to, even appreciated, and it is no less a bind. With a knowing subject, bind more fully: "Do you think your desire to go into trance affects whether you notice it happening immediately, or in a little while, or even if it slips past your awareness?" That is a metaquestion: a yes or no about whether their desire matters leaves the presupposition of trance untouched. Feeling it now, later or not at all are all covered, and any confusion while they parse it adds a Bateson element. From there:

- **Continue as patter**: "...I don't know what's going to happen, but I enjoy wondering about it, and maybe you'd like to enjoy wondering with me..."
- **Bind further and steer**: "...You should decide: Is it important to you to consider this, or is it something that you can just let go of?"

## Collapsing the bind

Release is part of the tool. A clear, explicit release is freeing. This is the same contrast that powers "confusion" or "overload" inductions such as the 7+/-2: a racing mind, then sudden clear instruction. For an NLP bind such as "Do you think you'll be completely mindless, or keep enough of yourself to watch your own brain fade away?", add punctuation: "I think you'll go back and forth, feeling your own consciousness slip through your fingers..." This breaks the bind without having to pick an option: you build on it while moving to a place where the hypnotist calls the shots.

**Timing.** A scene is peaks and valleys, and collapsing a bind can be a peak: the induction itself, a change of focus, the granting of permission. If a partner is struggling with the paradox of whether they're in trance, add to it ("Are you, or aren't you? Hmm?") and watch their breathing and the moment they might crack. One way to peak is to snap them out so they feel "Oh, I must have been and oh, I wish I still was --", then drop them straight back down.

## Tag questions

A **tag question** turns a statement into a question with a tag: "right?", "isn't it?", "aren't you?". In everyday English it softens speech. It verifies something you think you know, lowers your authority, signals openness to correction, and invites conversation ("It's going to be warm out today, right?"). In hypnosis it does something different: "You're really feeling it intensely, aren't you?"

- **Sounding conversational.** A tag sounds natural only if tags are already part of your speech. Many tags cited in NLP aren't common in conversation and make you sound like a stereotypical sneaky hypnotist. That is fine if you want to signal what you're doing to a subject who knows the tropes. Your spoken and texted habits may differ, so go by your own usage. Tags make a scene more inviting and permissive, and invite a verbal or nonverbal response or simply participation.
- **Authority and processing.** Tags generally lower perceived credibility. Research on tags from sources of differing reliability found that, from authoritative sources, tags did not reduce perceived accuracy; they made people process the tagged information more thoroughly. A hypnotist has perceived authority (assumed knowledge, skillfully used; no kinky power dynamic required). So "It's getting easier to focus, isn't it?" doesn't invite criticism. It puts more emphasis on the suggestion and invites an internal response.
- **Yes set, pacing to leading.** A tag makes the subject parse the claim and check it against their model, which is a hypnotic inward look. Compare the imperative "Feel the way my voice sneaks inside your head" (explicit leading, a command) with the present continuous "You're feeling the way my voice sneaks inside your head" (describing). Tags on imperatives ("Take a seat, won't you?") just soften a command into a request. More often they go on statements in any tense: "You're starting to notice yourself letting go, right?", "...didn't you?", "...aren't you?". These *appear* to observe while they actually direct, which makes them a way to move from pacing into leading. A "yes" to one can be intense: "Oh my god, they're right; that is a part of what I'm feeling just like all the other things they said before..."
- **Tone signaling.** A rising tag ("That's a really intense response, isn't it?") feels light, curious and open. A falling tag ("That's a really intense response, isn't it.") states authoritative knowledge with a more dominant connotation. Shifting between the two creates contrast and moves the mood.
- **Pause, then tag.** Say "It looks like you really want this...", wait for the stiffening or the gasp, then add "...Right?" The tag then acknowledges a live response, and acknowledging responses is a key tool. Leave breathing room. The scene should be a dialogue, even a nonverbal one, not a stream of suggestions. A well-timed tag works as a mind read ("If I can read your mind, I can write it"), and being seen that personally produces intense experiences. The tag also makes the subject re-parse the suggestion, which adds emphasis, prompts a response from a new angle, or lifts them to a meta level about their own reaction.
- **Framing responses.** A question pulls for a verbal answer, which can be hard in trance. Before and during trance, frame any response as welcome. Acknowledge nonverbal signals ("That's right" lets them know it counts), and say things like "You don't have to answer." The feeling that one's natural responses are communicative, wanted and done "correctly" is cultivated continuously, not set up with one sentence.
