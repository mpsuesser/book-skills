# The Attractive Character

John Alanis's rule for attraction: to attract someone you have to be attractive, and he means your personality. The **Attractive Character** is the personality you design for your market to be attracted to, and you weave it into your social content, ads, sales videos, webinars, emails and fulfillment. "The Attractive Character is the thing that binds people to your value ladder. They come in looking for a result, but they stay because of their relationship with you." Jenna Kutcher: a brand is the image and personality the business applies to its offers; the Attractive Character is that brand applied to your offers to raise their value.

Each part of yourself you share attracts the segment that relates to it, builds trust and leads to purchases. Brunson's wrestling stories brought in male athletes; his story of the twins and fertility struggles brought in wives, mothers and families.

The character has four **elements**, one **identity** and six **storylines**.

## Elements

1. **Backstory.** People won't care about your success or follow you until they know you've been where they are now. The mistake is posturing about how great you are today; be vulnerable and share the journey. Dig past the surface: Dr. Chad Woolner's first answer for why he became a chiropractor was "paid well, Fridays off"; asked again and again, he told how a chiropractor healed his wife after a car accident and he changed his major from dental school. The origin story changes the relationship. "You will get tired of hearing your backstory way before your market gets tired of it." Ask: what backstories can you share that build a better relationship between you, the Attractive Character, and your dream customers?
2. **Parables.** The best teachers teach in parables. Teach every concept with a small story so it sticks. His parable for investing versus buying: Olympic gold medalist and UFC fighter Mark Schultz took all the cash from college freshman Russell's wallet for his "Total Violence" tape, because if he gave it free Russell would never watch it, and having paid, he would. Tell it before you ask for an investment. Build a "Rolodex of parables"; they can come from other people's lives.
3. **Character flaws.** Flaws make you relatable and real; every believable, three-dimensional character has them (Superman has kryptonite and a family to protect). Marvel beat DC with normal humans who had flaws and got powers (Spider-Man, Iron Man, the Hulk). A perfect facade alienates; vulnerability earns empathy.
4. **Polarity.** Neutral is boring. "When Attractive Characters try to win the votes of everyone, they end up reaching no one." Take positions on hard matters and stick to them: drawing a line in the sand splits people into those who agree, those who are neutral and those who disagree, and turns fair-weather fans into die-hard fans. You will get haters and raving fans, and the raving fans buy (Howard Stern). "If you're neutral, no one will hate you, but no one will know who you are either."

## Identity

Pick one; it shapes how you communicate, and the stories you tell match it. If you are struggling to create the character, re-examine the identity.
- **The Leader**: leads the audience from one place to another. Has a backstory like theirs, knows the hurdles and pitfalls, and has usually already achieved the result.
- **The Adventurer (or Crusader)**: curious, doesn't have all the answers, sets out to discover the ultimate truth and brings back treasures. Where the Leader leads the journey, the Adventurer brings back answers.
- **The Reporter (or Evangelist)**: hasn't blazed the trail yet; interviews dozens, hundreds or thousands of people and shares what they learn. Brunson started this way (think Larry King or Oprah); his status rose by association with the high-status people he interviewed, and it evolved into coaching. A good way to start in a niche you are excited to explore.
- **The Reluctant Hero** (Brunson today): humble, would rather stay out of the spotlight, but has secrets so important that they must overcome their shyness and share them, out of a moral duty.

## Storylines

1. **Loss and redemption**: success, then a trial, losing it all, then redemption. Gives people in loss faith and hope.
2. **Us versus them**: polarizes. "Us" is the people who do what it takes to succeed with what you sell; "them" is those who won't comply. A rallying cry against what your people don't want to become.
3. **Before and after**: the transformation (weight-loss photos, a home before and after). Ask: what was life like before you got this result, and what is it like now?
4. **Amazing discovery**: what you are discovering, how it helped you, and how it can help them.
5. **Secret telling**: secrets you know or have heard. Pulls someone into a story better than almost anything.
6. **Third-person testimonial**: other people's successes, as social proof. Collect as many as you can and tell them over and over.

## Worked examples

**Liz Benny, "the Queen of Kapow."** Dream customers: want profitable businesses that impact the world, feel called to great things, want financial freedom and fulfillment. Backstory: "Playing It Safe" (degree, master's, safe job, unfulfilled). Parable: "Systems Create Profit" ($25,000 her first year; after reading *The E-Myth Revisited*, $181,932 profit the next). Flaws: dyslexia (she says "I'm dyslexic" on her webinars); an unfounded fear of not knowing enough (you only need to be one step ahead). Polarity: "You Can Be 100% Yourself and Succeed"; "Scammers Suck." Identity: Reluctant Hero.

**CashFlow Tactics**: several founders become several Attractive Characters under one brand, each playing a role (Ryan as Captain America, Brad as Tony Stark, Jimmy as the Hulk). Dream customers: driven by personal freedom, tired of trading time for money. Backstories: the Freedom Fighter (the 401(k) path means being stuck); the Mad Scientist (the numbers; freedom is mathematically impossible with Wall Street, and there is a path to it in 10 years or less); the Drill Sergeant (fought overseas for freedom, then lost his own in the corporate rat race). Parable: Stop Fighting Gravity (97 percent of traditional financial advice is dangerous, misleading or wrong). Flaws: constantly chasing goals ("blissfully dissatisfied"); analysis paralysis; a "Level 10 Quickstart" chasing shiny objects. Polarity: Do the Opposite (do what everyone else does and you get what everyone else has); Goldfish Advice ("You cannot lead what you don't live"). Identities: Ryan the Adventurer, Brad the Reporter, Jimmy the Leader.

## The worksheet

Fill it in before you advertise:
- **Name**
- **Dream customers**
- **Backstory**: the journey that got the character to where they are today
- **Parables**: stories that help dream customers understand an important point
- **Character flaws**: shared to make the character more relatable
- **Polarity**: beliefs the character holds that attract dream customers and repel everyone else
- **Identity**: circle one (Leader, Adventurer, Reporter, Reluctant Hero) and say why
