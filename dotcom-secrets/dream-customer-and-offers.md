# Dream customer, Hook Story Offer, funnel hacking

## The Secret Formula, question by question

The doodle: a fish (who), the pond it swims in (where), the bait on the hook (hook, story, offer), and the fish carried to the top of a mountain (the result). Answer all four before building anything. Brunson prices these as the questions he would work through in a $100,000-a-day consultation.

You attract customers with the content and offers you put into the marketplace, so a business owner who dislikes their customers made them: "If you change your bait, you'll change your customer."

### 1. Who is your dream customer?

Most people start from a product idea and never ask who it is for. You will spend more time with these people than with your family, so choose them the way you would choose a spouse. The failure he names: selling to beginners with no money, and spending 99 percent of his time teaching them how to buy a domain and set up hosting.

Ask: Who are my dream clients? What do they look like? What are they passionate about? What are their goals, dreams and desires? What offers could I create that would attract them and repel everyone else?

**Customer avatar**, the procedure:
1. Create an avatar (he made one man and one woman, because he serves both).
2. Give each a name, list their characteristics, find an image that fits, print it and hang it on the wall.
3. Write every offer and every message to that one person.

His avatars: Julie (successful, driven, has a message to share, values personal growth over money, has already started a business with some success and wants to grow it) and Mike (a former athlete, has helped change someone's life in a small way and wants to help more, values growth over money, has built a business and wants more impact). Six years later, about 100 people paying $50,000 each were almost all Julies and Mikes.

John Lee Dumas's avatar "Jimmy" (37, a wife and two kids, a cubicle job he hates, a 25-minute commute in and 35 minutes home) turned into a decision test: "WWJD: What would Jimmy do?" Every piece of content, call to action, intro and outro speaks to that one person, and knowing him tells you where he hangs out.

Narrowing the who changes results without changing the product. Dean and Robyn Holland sold makeup brushes to "all women who use makeup"; they narrowed to women with PCOS, as Robyn was, changed only the words on the landing page and in the ads, and went from $34,000 in the whole prior year to more than $100,000 in 60 days.

### 2. Where are they congregating?

"The real power of the internet is the power of congregations." Like a church, people gather around shared interests (kids at Magic: The Gathering, wrestlers on TheMat.com). Your job is to figure out who they are, then find where they already congregate. List their:
- top websites; forums and message boards; Facebook groups
- influencers they follow on Facebook and Instagram
- podcasts; email newsletters; blogs; YouTube channels
- the Google keywords they search

### 3. What bait will attract them?

The bait is the **hook, story and offer** (below). Done right, it attracts the customers you want and repels the ones you don't. "DotCom Secrets Labs: 108 Proven Split Test Winners" means nothing to a beginner who doesn't know what a split test is, and is irresistible to his dream customer. The offer must match what that customer wants.

### 4. What unique result can you create for them?

"A business is not about products and services. A business is about what result you can get for your clients." People come for a specific result, not your product; once you sell the result, price stops being the barrier. Exercise: imagine clients could pay you anything to get their result. What would you do to guarantee their success? Where would you lead them? That is your pinnacle offer (his: a done-for-you funnel for $1 million up front plus a large percentage of sales), and the path to it is the Value Ladder.

## Hook, Story, Offer

Every piece of bait (ad, email, landing page, upsell, webinar, phone call) has a hook, a story and an offer. "If something isn't working in your funnel, it's always either the hook, the story, or the offer." He builds them in reverse: offer first, then the story, then the hook.

His demonstration: auctioning his own $600 iPhone to 2,000 men with the hook "In the next ten minutes, someone here will be willing to give me at least $100,000 for this phone," then telling the stories of what was on it (the Books app holding more than $750,000 of courses he had converted to audio, his contacts list, lifetime Voxer access he normally sold as $100,000-a-year coaching, his other apps). It sold for $750,000.

There are two ways to have the lowest-priced product in your market: cut the price (and your margins), or raise the value so far that selling it for what it is worth makes it seem inexpensive.

### The irresistible offer

Three kinds of business: **physical products**; **information products** (authors, speakers, coaches, consultants, affiliates: courses, membership sites, live events); **services** (local: restaurants, dentists, chiropractors, Realtors, financial planners; online: software, design).

A **commodity** is something someone can also buy somewhere else, so you are forced to compete on price, which takes away the margins that pay for ads, a team and more value. Kennedy: there is no strategic advantage in being the second-lowest price in town, and a huge one in being the most expensive. The fix is to restructure what you sell into an **offer**: raise its perceived value and make it unique to you, available only inside this special offer.

**The offer stack**: list each element with its value, total them, then give the price. The iPhone stack: Books App $750,000, Voxer $100,000, Other Apps $150,000, Contacts priceless; total $1,000,000; just $100,000.

**The 10x rule**: total value is at least ten times the price ($100 offer, $1,000 or more in value; $1,000 offer, $10,000). Price resistance doesn't mean the customer lacks money; it means you haven't raised the value until the price feels inexpensive. ($10,000 for shoes feels outrageous; $10,000 for a Ferrari feels like a steal.)

**Building the offer** (his "What Else Can We Add?" whiteboard):
1. Brainstorm everything you could give the dream customer to help guarantee their success. Write every idea down, however crazy.
2. Ask what your non-negotiables are, what you are not willing to do for this offer, and cross those off. (For the ClickFunnels free trial: SOP documents, 100 funnel templates, event recordings and funnel-building contracts stayed; Russell flying out to build their funnel was crossed off. Before, the offer had been only a 14-day trial.)
3. Pick the elements that make an offer so good the customer would be "certifiably insane" to say no.

Any commodity can be stacked. Kiana, a 12-year-old babysitter whose friends all charge about $5 an hour, brainstormed (wash dishes, clean the house, read books, play outside, help with homework, put kids to sleep, bring snacks, cook treats, take pictures, print a collage) and stacked: Babysitting 4 hrs ($20), Clean House ($60), Homework Done ($60), No Electronics ($40), Fun Activities & Books ($30), Snacks and Treats ($20), BONUS Memory Book ($30), BONUS Kids Asleep ($140); total $400, just $40. Three minutes in, Brunson offered her $20 an hour. With a stack like that, parents call weeks in advance and pay two to three times what other sitters get.

Get good at making offers; it is the most important job of a business owner. Every ad makes one ("Click here to see a video that will show you how to ___"), every funnel step makes one ("Give me your email address and I'll send you a free ___"), every value ladder level and every email makes one. Claude Hopkins's "scheme men" were paid $52,000 a year in 1907 (about $1,396,471 today) only to create offers; one took Schlitz from fifth place to a tie with Anheuser-Busch.

### Story

The story is what makes the offer worth more. It shows why you are uniquely qualified to give this result and builds the relationship and trust that raise the perceived value of every element. Drew Manning's Keto Jumpstart (meal plans and grocery lists for eight weeks, 35 recipes, a keto food list of 80+ foods, eight weeks of bodyweight workouts, a restaurant guide, a Facebook community) sells because of his story: he deliberately gained 75 pounds in six months and lost it over the next six (his story of going "fit to fat to fit"), so he understands the emotional and psychological journey his clients go through. Tell the story in the ads and on the landing page before the offer, and at every step: ads, landing pages, upsells, webinars. The story frameworks are the funnel scripts.

### Hook

Hooks are the headlines, images and videos placed where dream customers congregate to grab attention just long enough to tell the story (the first edition called them "Enquirer Interrupts," after the tabloid headlines at the grocery checkout). A hook gives no value by itself. Each story can carry dozens of hooks (for Drew: a Jay Leno clip, before-and-after photos, photos with the product, photos with people he helped, story videos, food pictures, headlines about weight loss and about weight gain). "We never know which ones will grab our market's attention," so keep throwing out hooks until you find the ones that work.

## Funnel hacking

"You can always tell who the pioneers are because they have arrows in their backs." Rule: before building any new funnel, find others with a successful funnel selling to your target market. If you can't find any, don't move forward. If you can, reverse-engineer it: that is **funnel hacking**, and its practitioners are **funnel hackers**.

"Funnel hacking is modeling a proven process or framework and then putting your product, your words, and your art into that framework." Model the framework (look and feel, layout, page order, price points: where they had a headline, you have a headline); copying their words or art is illegal. He modeled Armand Morin's software funnels for his ZIP Brander. Porter Stansberry's point: competitors try to undercut on price or invent a different model and fail, instead of modeling what already works.

The process:
1. Pick the funnel type you need.
2. Find as many examples as you can from three sources: **direct competitors** (similar thing to the same people), **indirect competitors** (a different thing to the same demographic), and **anyone selling through the same funnel type** in any market (since you model the framework, the market need not match).
3. Identify at least 10 funnels. What you can see of a business is the tip of the iceberg: the difference between a $10,000 website and a $10 million company is everything that happens *after* a buyer enters the first funnel, so modeling only the visible pages fails. Act like a customer: search the Google keywords you would use and click the paid ads; like related Facebook pages so Facebook shows you similar ads. Buy, so you see the whole process.
4. Sketch each step (ad → landing page → offer → upsell) and under each write its **Hook? Story? Offer?** Ask how they use hooks, what stories they tell to create interest and value, what the offer is and what they added to make it irresistible. Do this on presell pages, landing pages, video sales pages, order forms and upsell pages.
5. Go through at least half a dozen to see the trends. That is the baseline you must beat. Then put on the consultant's hat: how would you make the hook sexier, tell the story differently, add to the offers and upsells?
6. Build your own funnel inside the framework.
