# Follow-up funnels: Soap Opera Sequences and Daily Seinfeld Emails

In the customer's story (Donald Miller's StoryBrand), the customer is the hero and the brand is the guide, Yoda to their Luke. "You are the brand, you are the guide": the Attractive Character guides each hero up the value ladder. Funnel pages are where the scripts go; **follow-up funnels** are the communication that moves people from one step to the next after they join your list as a subscriber or a buyer.

Two kinds of message:
- **Soap Opera Sequence**: a fixed series sent right after someone enters a funnel. Its jobs: build the relationship with the Attractive Character quickly, pull the prospect through the funnel they just entered, and ascend them to the next funnel on the ladder.
- **Daily Seinfeld Emails**: broadcasts sent to everyone not currently in a follow-up sequence, to reengage them and bring them back into the ladder.

The map: each funnel on the staircase has its own Soap Opera Sequence after it. When a sequence ends, the person moves into the next funnel's sequence if there is one; if this is your only funnel, or they finish, they join the Seinfeld broadcast list.

Before building, map the follow-up: Who is my Attractive Character? How do I flesh out that character in the emails? What does each email in the Soap Opera Sequence say? What kinds of Seinfeld emails will I send? Then build one email per day of the sequence (five days, five emails), set the delays, and connect the opt-in form so anyone who gives an email starts receiving them.

## Soap Opera Sequence

Learned from Andre Chaperon. Soap operas are continuous, high-drama, open-ended stories whose characters are always getting into and out of trouble. Each email opens a loop that only the next email closes. "If your first email is boring, you're done": hook with an open storyline in the first email. Use a different story structure for each product on the ladder.

The five emails, one a day (his example sells the free-plus-shipping *Expert Secrets* book to people who opted in to learn about becoming an expert):

| Day | Email | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Set the Stage | welcome, what to expect, the first hook |
| 2 | High Drama → Backstory → Wall | start at the drama, go back to how you got there, stop at the wall |
| 3 | Epiphany (the One Thing) | the answer that got you over the wall, tied to the product |
| 4 | Hidden Benefits | benefits they haven't seen yet |
| 5 | Urgency and CTA | a real reason to act now |

**Email 1, Set the stage.** Sent the moment they subscribe. It sets the stage, says what to expect, and opens the first hook. Example ("[EXPERT] Ch. 1 of 5"): welcome to my world; a short backstory (selling potato-gun DVDs); his other businesses are the playground where he tests things and reports back. His promise is to give away better stuff free than others charge for, so they'll open every email as if they'd paid. Tomorrow he will ship one of his best products free, only to readers who open the email. Signed "Your New Marketing Buddy"; the P.S. names tomorrow's subject line ("Ch. 2 of 5: The day my education failed me").

**Email 2, High drama, backstory, wall.** Daegan Smith's rule: start any good story at the point of high drama; most people start at the beginning, but stories get good in the middle. Then the **backstory**: the events leading up to the drama, usually back to a place like where the reader is now (overweight, broke). Then the **wall**: you got stuck. Say you hit the wall and found the answer, but don't give the answer yet: open the loop and promise to close it in email 3. Example: his college graduation day, sitting in the auditorium asking "How did I get here?", classmates heading to $30,000 to $40,000 entry-level jobs with loans they can't discharge, while he already knew his next step (his business had made $250,000 his senior year and passed $1 million within a year of graduating). Would you like to know what I found out? Then look for my email tomorrow, with the next subject line. Signed "Wish I Had My Tuition Back"; the P.S. delivers yesterday's promised free product (just cover shipping).

**Email 3, Epiphany.** Reveal the epiphany, the moment everything turned around, and tie it to the product: the **One Thing**. Templates: "My epiphany was I needed to build a list, and that's when I learned about ___." "I had to get a support system to help me get over my addiction, and that's when I found ___." "I had to address the emotional roots of overeating, and that's when I found ___." Selling someone else's product, it's enough that the epiphany led you to discover it. Example: his professor earned about $25 an hour, while the author of a $50 how-to book that was 90 percent pictures sold 100 a day ($5,000 a day, $1,825,000 a year) from writing it once. He didn't want to sell his knowledge by the hour, but like that author: selling knowledge the right way is the One Thing. A link to the video, and a P.S. promising tomorrow's hidden benefits.

**Email 4, Hidden benefits.** If they haven't bought, they haven't seen the value yet, so point out benefits they haven't considered. Example: he had no credentials; helping people changed his own life (travel, meeting Tony Robbins and Richard Branson); the real hidden benefit is the fulfillment of watching someone change, "probably the same thing for you too—am I right?" Then the offer again: a free copy (normally $19.95) if they say where to ship it this week.

**Email 5, Urgency and call to action.** Earlier calls to action were casual; now light a little fire. Legitimate reasons: the webinar starts tomorrow; only 10 seats left at the event; you ordered only 1,000 books and most are gone; the video is coming offline. "Whatever the reason, your urgency needs to be real. Fake urgency will backfire on you, and you'll lose all credibility." Think about why you might actually run out; for an evergreen product, use a sale ending soon or a coupon expiring in 24 hours. Example ("Ch. 5 of 5: Last Call"): the offer goes away today, tomorrow it's full price, "You've been warned."

Every email pushes to the next one. Format: one or two sentences per line at most, lots of white space, no long paragraphs.

## Daily Seinfeld Emails

*Seinfeld* was a show about nothing. Daily content emails stopped working for him; after the Soap Opera Sequence has built the bond, readers respond to entertainment. Switching from 100 percent content to 90 percent entertainment and 10 percent content sent readership, opens, clicks and sales soaring.

**Frequency**: once a month got a horrible response; twice a month more than doubled his income; then weekly, twice a week, every other day. Now: "if I don't email my list every day, I lose money every day." Readers don't get annoyed because they're entertained.

**Structure**: each Seinfeld email is a Hook, Story, Offer. The subject line is the hook that gets the open; the body is the story that entertains; the ending is an offer tied to a core offer. "If you just send out entertaining emails and don't tie in your products or services, you won't make a dime." There is no sequence: each day a different hook and a different story make a different segment respond. Lead back to something you sell: your core offer, another product, or someone else's.

Three styles:
- **Episode**: what happened today; story-based "edu-taining," or a controversial take on your industry. Goal: get people back into a funnel.
- **Epiphany**: ideas that are inspirational, enlightening or thought-provoking, that challenge existing dogma or beliefs; the reader's epiphany ties to the core offer.
- **Educational**: checklists, how-tos, Q&A, FAQs, each pointing back to a core offer.

Two of his examples (each made more than $100,000):
- "[True Story] He FLUSHED $20 million down the toilet today...": a golf entrepreneur applied to the Inner Circle; in 10 minutes Brunson pulled his three main competitors' traffic sources, top three converting ads and funnels; the man (100,000 units sold on TV, 20 books read) said Brunson couldn't teach him anything. The turn: reading a million books on Jiu Jitsu won't help you in a street fight; these are the things you can't learn in a book. Then the Inner Circle application link, with a takeaway: "we only accept cool people."
- "Jiu Jitsu is like wrestling for old, fat guys (and other marketing stuff)": a funny story about cutting weight before a match. The turn: a six-figure week with no launch, no affiliates and him out of the office; want a business that runs as well when you're gone? He is personally working with a few more people next week; apply. Not a get-rich-quick scheme, a work-hard-and-build-an-awesome-company one.

They go fast to write; you can dictate them on your phone and transcribe. The Soap Opera Sequence is prewritten and runs by day; Seinfeld broadcasts can be prewritten and scheduled, but are usually tied to what is happening in the Attractive Character's life in real time. Point each one at whatever is current (a new book or event goes to that funnel; with nothing new, send your best-converting offer daily until the next big event). Ben Settle emailed the same core offer (a $97-a-month print newsletter) every day for 10 years with new hooks and new stories.
