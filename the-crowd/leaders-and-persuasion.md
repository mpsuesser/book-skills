# Leaders of crowds and their means of persuasion

## The leader

- **From the led.** The leader has usually started as one of the led and been hypnotised by the idea he now preaches, until everything outside it vanishes and every contrary opinion looks to him like error or superstition. Robespierre, hypnotised by Rousseau's philosophical ideas, propagated them with the methods of the Inquisition.
- **Men of action, not thinkers.** Leaders lack keen foresight, and must, because foresight breeds doubt and inactivity. They come especially from "those morbidly nervous, excitable, half-deranged persons who are bordering on madness." Their convictions are so strong that reasoning is lost on them. Contempt and persecution only excite them. They sacrifice their interest and their family, the instinct of self-preservation is erased, and often the only reward they ask is martyrdom. "The intensity of their faith gives great power of suggestion to their words." "Men gathered in a crowd lose all force of will, and turn instinctively to the person who possesses the quality they lack."
- **Apostles and rhetoricians.** Some leaders are subtle rhetoricians who seek only their own interest and persuade by flattering base instincts. Their influence can be great but is always ephemeral. The men of ardent conviction who have stirred the soul of crowds (Peter the Hermit, Luther, Savonarola, the men of the French Revolution) fascinated others only after a creed had first fascinated them. They can then call up faith, "which renders a man the absolute slave of his dream."
- **The arousing of faith**, whether religious, political or social, in a work, a person or an idea, has always been the great leaders' function. The great religions and empires were built by obscure believers with little but their faith, never by the learned, the philosophers or the sceptics.
- **A continuous series** runs from the great masters of men, few enough for history to count, down to the workman in a smoky inn who slowly fascinates his comrades by drumming into their ears a few set phrases he scarcely understands, which he believes must bring every dream and hope to pass.
- **Everyone outside his speciality.** In every social sphere, as soon as a man stops being isolated he falls under a leader, because most men have no clear, reasoned ideas on anything outside their own speciality. Periodicals can replace the leader, though very inefficiently, by manufacturing opinions and supplying ready-made phrases that spare their readers the trouble of reasoning.
- **Despotism as a condition.** "The leaders of crowds wield a very despotic authority, and this despotism indeed is a condition of their obtaining a following." Without any means of enforcing it they extort obedience from the most turbulent workers, fixing hours and wages and decreeing strikes that begin and end at the hour they set. As public authorities let themselves be questioned and weakened, these leaders usurp their place and are obeyed more docilely than any government. Remove them and the crowd falls back into a collectivity without cohesion or power of resistance: the arrest of the two leaders of the Parisian omnibus strike ended it at once.

### Two classes of leader

1. **Intermittent strength of will.** Energetic, violent, brave and audacious, these men are most useful to direct a violent enterprise decided on suddenly, to carry the masses through danger, and to turn yesterday's recruits into heroes: Ney and Murat under the First Empire, and Garibaldi, "a talentless but energetic adventurer" who took the kingdom of Naples with a handful of men against a disciplined army. Their energy is transitory and scarcely outlasts the exciting cause. Back in ordinary life they often show astonishing weakness of character and cannot manage the simplest affairs. They can lead only on condition that they are themselves led and continually stimulated, with a man or an idea as their beacon and a clearly traced line of conduct.
2. **Enduring strength of will.** Far rarer and less brilliant, these men have much more influence: the true founders of religions and great undertakings, such as St Paul, Mahomet, Christopher Columbus and de Lesseps. Whether they are intelligent or narrow-minded matters not at all: "the world belongs to them." A strong and continuous will is a rare and immensely powerful faculty to which everything yields: "neither nature, gods, nor man." De Lesseps joined two seas, a task the greatest sovereigns had attempted in vain for three thousand years, against the opposition of England, the hesitation of Egypt and France, a hostile French consul, an attempt to make his workmen desert by refusing them fresh water, and engineers and a Minister of Marine who calculated the day of his disaster "as an eclipse is foretold." He failed later in the same kind of enterprise because old age had come, "to which everything, even the will, succumbs."

## Short-term action: example

To stir a crowd to some act for a short time, act on it by rapid suggestion, of which example is the most powerful kind. The crowd must already have been prepared by circumstances, and the operator must have prestige.

## Long-term action: affirmation, repetition, contagion

**Affirmation.** The religious books and legal codes of every age rely on simple affirmation, and so do statesmen defending a cause and merchants advertising their goods. The more concise it is and the freer of any appearance of proof, the more weight it carries.

**Repetition.** The repeated statement settles into the deep unconscious regions where the motives of our actions are forged, and after a while we forget who said it and believe it. That is the power of advertising. Read a thousand times that X's chocolate is the best and you imagine you have heard it from many quarters and become certain of it. Read a thousand times that Y's flour has cured illustrious persons of obstinate maladies and you try it when you fall ill in the same way. Read always in the same papers that A is an arrant scamp and B a most honest man and you believe it, unless you also read a paper of the contrary opinion where the labels are reversed. Repetition works this way on the most enlightened minds too.

**Contagion.**

- An affirmation repeated enough, with unanimity, as by "certain famous financial undertakings rich enough to purchase every assistance", forms a **current of opinion**, and contagion takes over. Contagion is natural and seen in animals: when one horse in a stable takes to biting his manger the others imitate him, and a panic among a few sheep spreads to the flock. Among men in a crowd every emotion is quickly contagious, hence the suddenness of panics. Madness itself is contagious, as its frequency among doctors to the mad shows.
- Simultaneous presence is not needed. Contagion acts at a distance through events that give all minds the same trend, especially minds already prepared by the remote factors. The revolution of 1848 broke out in Paris and spread over much of Europe, shaking a number of thrones.
- **Imitation is an effect of contagion.** Imitation is a necessity for man, "provided always that the imitation is quite easy", and that necessity is the power of fashion in opinions, ideas, literature and dress. At every period a small number of individualities act on the rest and are imitated by the unconscious mass. They must not depart too far from received ideas, or imitating them would be too hard and their influence nil: "men who are too superior to their epoch are generally without influence upon it." Europeans have little influence on Eastern peoples for the same reason; they differ too much. The past and reciprocal imitation together make the men of one country and period so alike that even philosophers and men of letters share a "family air" by which their age is known at once.
- Contagion imposes modes of feeling as well as opinions. It explains why a work such as "Tannhauser" is despised in one period and, a few years later, admired by those who led the criticism.
- The opinions and beliefs of crowds spread by contagion, never by reasoning. The working classes' present conceptions were acquired in the public-house through affirmation, repetition and contagion, and crowd beliefs have formed that way in every age. Renan compared the first founders of Christianity to socialist workmen spreading their ideas from public-house to public-house. Voltaire noted that for more than a hundred years Christianity was "only embraced by the vilest riff-raff."

**Contagion from the bottom up.** Contagion that begins in the popular classes spreads to the higher ones: socialist doctrines are being taken up by those who will be their first victims, because personal interest itself disappears under contagion. Every opinion the populace adopts ends by implanting itself vigorously in the highest strata, however absurd it is. The cycle runs:

1. The crowd's belief originates, more or less, in some higher idea, which often had no influence in the sphere where it arose.
2. Leaders and agitators, subjugated by it, take hold of it and distort it.
3. They create a sect, which distorts it afresh and spreads it among the masses.
4. The masses carry the deformation further.
5. Having become a popular truth, the idea returns to its source and influences the upper classes of the nation.

"In the long run it is intelligence that shapes the destiny of the world, but very indirectly": the philosophers who evolved the ideas have long since turned to dust when the fruit of their reflection triumphs.

## Prestige

Ideas spread by affirmation, repetition and contagion acquire prestige in time, and that gives them great power. Whatever has ruled the world, ideas or men, has enforced its authority chiefly by prestige.

- **Not admiration or fear.** Prestige may involve admiration or fear, but it can exist without either. The dead, whom we do not fear (Alexander, Caesar, Mahomet, Buddha), have the most prestige of all, and the monstrous divinities of India's subterranean temples have great prestige though no one admires them.
- **Its effect is fascination.** The sentiment is inexplicable, like every sentiment, but seems to be of the same kind as the fascination of a magnetised subject. "Neither gods, kings, nor women have ever reigned without it."

### Acquired prestige

- **Acquired** (or artificial) prestige comes from name, fortune and reputation, and is much the most common. Holding a position, owning a fortune or bearing a title confers it, however slight the person's own worth. A soldier in uniform and a judge in his robes always have it. Pascal saw that judges need robes and wigs, without which they would lose half their authority. The most unbending socialist is somewhat impressed by a prince or a marquis, and a swindler who assumes such a title robs tradesmen easily. Titles, decorations and uniforms work in every country, even where the sense of personal independence is strongest: a travel writer described the most reasonable Englishmen reddening with pleasure, eyes shining, when a peer spoke to them, and books on the peerage found in all hands, like the Bible.
- **Prestige of opinions and works.** Opinions and literary and artistic works also have prestige, usually "the result of accumulated repetitions". Literary and artistic history is mostly the repetition of identical judgments that nobody tries to verify, until there are names and things no one dares touch. A modern reader is immensely bored by Homer, but who would say so? The Parthenon in its present state is a wretched ruin, yet we see it wrapped in its historical memories. "The special characteristic of prestige is to prevent us seeing things as they are and to entirely paralyse our judgment." "Crowds always, and individuals as a rule, stand in need of ready-made opinions on all subjects", and how popular those opinions are depends not on their truth or error but solely on their prestige.

### Personal prestige

- **Personal** prestige is "a faculty independent of all titles, of all authority". A small number of people have it, and it lets them exercise "a veritably magnetic fascination" over their social equals, without any ordinary means of domination. They are obeyed "as is the tamer of wild beasts by the animal that could easily devour him." Buddha, Jesus, Mahomet, Joan of Arc and Napoleon had it in a high degree. "Gods, heroes, and dogmas win their way in the world of their own inward strength. They are not to be discussed: they disappear, indeed, as soon as discussed."
- **It comes before fame.** These figures had their power of fascination long before they became illustrious and would never have become illustrious without it. Napoleon, an obscure general sent to command the army of Italy, met divisional generals such as Augereau, a proud swashbuckler, who were ready to receive the young intruder with insolence. He kept them waiting, came in wearing his sword, put on his hat, explained his measures, gave his orders and dismissed them. Augereau said nothing until he was outside, then admitted with Massena that "this little devil of a general" had inspired him with awe. General Vandamme, rougher still, said in 1815 that in Napoleon's presence he, who feared neither God nor devil, was ready to tremble like a child.
- **Abuse as display.** Napoleon knew that treating the great personages around him worse than stable lads added to his prestige. He insulted Beugnot in a Council of State like a valet, then asked, "Well, stupid, have you found your head again?" and took him by the ear, which Beugnot called "an intoxicating sign of favour." Davoust said that had the Emperor ordered Paris destroyed with no one allowed to escape, he would have left his own wife and children there sooner than let the secret out.
- **Elba.** On his return from Elba Napoleon, an isolated man facing all the organised forces of a great country, had only to look at the generals sent to seize him, who had sworn to do it, and all of them submitted without discussion. His prestige outlived him and made an emperor of his obscure nephew. "Ill-treat men as you will, massacre them by millions, be the cause of invasion upon invasion, all is permitted you if you possess prestige in a sufficient degree and the talent necessary to uphold it."
- **Modest origins.** Prestige need not rest on military glory or religious terror. De Lesseps overcame unanimous opposition by showing himself: he spoke briefly, and his opponents became his friends. England fought his scheme, he appeared there and won everyone over, and later the bells rang at Southampton as he passed.

### How prestige grows and dies

- **The series.** Prestige runs in a series from the founders of religions and empires to the private man who tries to dazzle his neighbours with a new coat or a decoration, and between them lie all the forms that arise from a civilisation's sciences, arts and literature. Prestige "constitutes the fundamental element of persuasion." Whatever has prestige, a being, an idea or a thing, is imitated at once through contagion and makes a whole generation adopt certain ways of feeling and expressing its thought. The imitation is unconscious, which is why it is perfect. Painters who copy the pale colouring and stiff attitudes of certain Primitives believe in their own sincerity; those who flood their canvases with violet see no more violet in nature than anyone did fifty years earlier, but they have been "suggestioned" by one master's prestige.
- **Success.** Success is one of the chief factors of prestige: "Every successful man, every idea that forces itself into recognition, ceases, ipso facto, to be called in question." When success disappears, prestige almost always goes with it. The hero acclaimed yesterday is insulted today if failure overtakes him, and the reaction is stronger the greater the prestige was, because the crowd now treats the fallen hero as an equal and takes revenge for having bowed to him. When the transfer of a few votes took power from Robespierre, he lost his prestige at once, and the crowd followed him to the guillotine with the same curses it had hurled at his victims. "Believers always break the statues of their former gods with every symptom of fury."
- **De Lesseps after Suez.** Having vanquished men, marshes, rocks and sandy wastes, he stopped believing in obstacles and tried to repeat Suez at Panama with the same methods. But he had aged, and "the faith that moves mountains does not move them if they are too lofty." The disaster destroyed his aureole. The magistrates of his country ranked him with the vilest criminals, his coffin passed unattended through an indifferent crowd, and only foreign sovereigns honoured his memory. A Viennese paper saw in this "the morality of success" and warned that "Genius cannot be prudent."
- **Discussion.** Prestige lost through failure vanishes quickly. Discussion wears it away more slowly but very surely: "From the moment prestige is called in question it ceases to be prestige." The gods and men who kept their prestige longest never tolerated discussion. "For the crowd to admire, it must be kept at a distance."
