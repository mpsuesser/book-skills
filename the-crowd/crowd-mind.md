# The mind of crowds: sentiments, morality, ideas, reasoning, imagination

Depth for each trait named in `SKILL.md`. Le Bon studies crowds that have attained "the phase of complete organisation": this shows "what crowds may become, but not what they invariably are." Character varies with environment: "It is only the uniformity of the environment that creates the apparent uniformity of characters", and every mental constitution holds possibilities of character that a sudden change of environment brings out. The savage members of the Convention were peaceable notaries and virtuous magistrates in ordinary times; the storm past, they became Napoleon's most docile servants.

Le Bon notes in passing that impulsiveness, irritability, incapacity to reason, the absence of judgment and of the critical spirit, and the exaggeration of the sentiments are traits of beings at inferior stages of evolution, "in women, savages, and children, for instance."

## Impulsiveness, mobility and irritability

- A crowd's acts are governed far more by the spinal cord than by the brain. They may be perfect in execution, but the exciting cause of the moment decides them. The isolated individual can dominate his reflex actions; a crowd cannot.
- Its impulses may be generous or cruel, heroic or cowardly, but they are always so imperious that even self-preservation does not override them. "A crowd may easily enact the part of an executioner, but not less easily that of a martyr." A general who had suddenly become popular (Boulanger) could have found a hundred thousand men ready to die for him.
- **No premeditation is possible.** Crowds are like leaves that a tempest whirls up and lets fall. Their wishes are frenzied but not durable. Only "the necessities of everyday life", an invisible regulator, let democracies last.
- **Baulked desire.** Like a savage, the crowd admits nothing between its desire and the realisation of it. Alone, a man knows he cannot set fire to a palace or loot a shop; in a crowd it suffices to suggest murder or pillage for him to yield. The normal condition of a crowd baulked in its wishes is furious passion.
- **Race sets the degree.** A Latin crowd and an Anglo-Saxon crowd differ strikingly. In France a telegram about a supposed insult to an ambassador set off a terrible war, and the telegraphed news of an insignificant reverse at Langson overthrew the government at once. A far graver English reverse at Khartoum caused only slight emotion, and no ministry fell. "Crowds are everywhere distinguished by feminine characteristics, but Latin crowds are the most feminine of all." Whoever trusts them may rise fast, but always skirts the Tarpeian rock.

## Suggestibility, credulity and collective hallucination

**The mechanism of a collective hallucination:**

1. A crowd in a state of **expectant attention**.
2. A first perversion of the truth by one individual, usually an illusion produced by vague reminiscences.
3. The affirmation of that illusion, which becomes the starting point of a contagious suggestion.
4. Contagion, which makes the perversion take the same shape in every mind, however different the temperaments.

The crowd thinks in images, each calling up others with no logical connection, and it hardly distinguishes the subjective from the objective. In an impressionable first observer a single detail (a scar, an article of dress) evokes the idea of another person; that idea becomes "the nucleus of a sort of crystallisation which invades the understanding and paralyses all critical faculty", and the observer sees the image in his mind instead of the object.

Examples:

- St George appeared on the walls of Jerusalem to all the Crusaders; certainly one of them saw him first.
- During the siege of Paris a candle in an upper story was taken for a signal to besiegers miles away.
- **The Belle Poule.** In broad daylight the watch of a frigate searching for a lost cruiser signalled a disabled vessel. Officers and sailors all saw a raft covered with men, towed by boats flying distress signals. The boat sent to the rescue saw men stretching out their hands and heard voices. It reached a few branches of trees swept from the coast.
- **Mr Davey's séance.** Davey convened distinguished observers, among them Mr Wallace, let them examine the objects and set seals where they wished, then produced the standard spiritualist phenomena by simple tricks. They wrote reports that the phenomena could only be supernatural. "He had such a power over the mind of the crowd that he could persuade it that it saw what it did not see." If minds of a superior order, invited to be suspicious, are deceived, ordinary crowds are deceived more easily.
- **False recognitions.** Two little girls found drowned in the Seine were recognised beyond doubt by half a dozen witnesses, and the death certificate was drawn up; they were alive and bore only a remote resemblance to the dead. A child's corpse at La Villette was recognised by a schoolboy, then by a woman (by its clothing and a scar), her brother-in-law, neighbours and the boy's schoolmaster (by a medal). Six weeks later it was identified as a boy murdered in Bordeaux.

**Mental quality does not protect.** "From the moment that they form part of a crowd the learned man and the ignoramus are equally incapable of observation." A few men of learning gathered together take on all the characteristics of crowds "with regard to matters outside their speciality."

**The worth of testimony:**

- Recognitions are most often made by women and children, the most impressionable witnesses. Magistrates repeat that children do not lie; children invariably lie, innocently, and it would be better to decide an accused man's fate by the toss of a coin than by a child's evidence.
- Thousands saw the cavalry charge at Sedan, yet who commanded it cannot be settled. Hundreds of witnesses attested facts about Waterloo that were gravely wrong. At Solferino the generals' reports were modified by the orderly officers, rewritten by the chief of staff, and replaced by the Marshal's own edition, until scarcely anything of the original remained.
- Treatises on logic rank the unanimity of numerous witnesses among the strongest proofs; on this point they need rewriting. "To say that a fact has been simultaneously verified by thousands of witnesses is to say, as a rule, that the real fact is very different from the accepted account of it."
- **History is legend.** Works of history are "fanciful accounts of ill-observed facts", and only the past's literary, artistic and monumental works tell us anything real about it. What has impressed crowds is the legendary hero, never the real one (Hercules, Buddha, Mahomet). Legends keep transforming with time and race: the Buddha of China shares no traits with the Buddha of India. Under the Bourbons Napoleon was an idyllic, liberal friend of the humble; thirty years later, a sanguinary despot who slaughtered three million men; and the legend was changing again. "History is scarcely capable of preserving the memory of anything except myths."

## Exaggeration and ingenuousness of sentiments

- A crowd's feelings are very simple and very exaggerated. It is blind to fine distinctions and intermediate phases. A feeling once shown spreads by suggestion and contagion, and the approval it meets increases its force.
- Impunity raises the violence, and more so the larger and more heterogeneous the crowd. The foolish, ignorant and envious lose the sense of their own insignificance and gain the notion of a brutal, temporary but immense strength.
- Exaggeration often fastens on bad sentiments, "atavistic residuum of the instincts of the primitive man", which the isolated individual curbs for fear of punishment. Skilfully influenced, a crowd is even more capable of heroism and devotion than an individual.
- **Only excessive sentiments impress a crowd.** "An orator wishing to move a crowd must make an abusive use of violent affirmations." It also demands exaggeration in its heroes: on the stage it requires courage, morality and virtue never found in real life.
- **Theatre.** The rules of the theatrical standpoint have little to do with common sense or logic. Managers cannot predict success because they would have to turn themselves into a crowd to judge. Coppee's "Pour la Couronne" was refused for ten years before its prodigious success. "Charley's Aunt", refused at every theatre and finally staged at a stockbroker's expense, ran two hundred performances in France and more than a thousand in London. A play that fires one country may fail in another of different race.
- Exaggeration applies to sentiment only, never to intelligence. Only in sentiment can a crowd rise very high or sink very low.

## Intolerance, dictatorialness and conservatism

- Beliefs induced by suggestion rather than reasoning are taken whole, as absolute truths or absolute errors. An individual may accept contradiction and discussion; a crowd never will. At public meetings the slightest contradiction brings howls, then blows, then expulsion; without the police the contradictor might be killed.
- In Latin crowds authoritativeness and intolerance have destroyed the sentiment of individual independence that is so strong in the Anglo-Saxon. They care only for the collective independence of their sect, which they take to mean bringing dissenters into immediate and violent subjection. The Jacobins of every epoch, "from those of the Inquisition downwards", have never had another conception of liberty.
- Crowds' sympathies have never gone to easy-going masters, only to tyrants who oppressed them vigorously; they raise these the loftiest statues. They trample a despot once he has lost his strength, because he is then among the feeble, "who are to be despised because they are not to be feared." Under an intermittent authority they swing from anarchy to servitude and back.
- **Revolutionary instincts are not predominant.** The tendency to violence deceives the observer. Left to themselves, crowds soon weary of disorder and turn to servitude: the proudest Jacobins acclaimed Bonaparte most loudly when he suppressed all liberty. Their mobility touches only superficial matters. Had democracies held their present power when mechanical looms, steam power and railways were introduced, those inventions would have been impossible, or achieved only at the cost of revolutions and massacres.

## The morality of crowds

- **Two senses of morality.** In the sense of "constant respect for certain social conventions, and the permanent repression of selfish impulses", crowds are too impulsive and mobile to be moral. If morality includes the transitory display of abnegation, self-sacrifice, disinterestedness, devotion and the need of equity, crowds may show a very lofty morality.
- Their ferocity comes from savage, destructive instincts dormant in everyone since the primitive ages, freed by impunity. The passion for the chase springs from the same source: the huntsmen gathered by dozens to watch hounds kill a stag.
- Appeals to **glory, honour and patriotism** work especially well on the individual in a crowd, often to the point of his giving his life. "Collectivities alone are capable of great disinterestedness and great devotion." Strikers obey an order more than they seek higher wages. In wars beyond their understanding, crowds let themselves be massacred like larks hypnotised by the hunter's mirror.
- **Moralisation by the crowd** is a frequent rule, not a constant one. The September murderers laid their victims' pocket-books and jewels on the committees' table. The ragged crowd that invaded the Tuileries in 1848 took nothing. Even an assembly of inferior elements is prudish: the debauchee murmurs at a risky line in a play.
- Crowds often have disinterestedness, resignation and devotion to a real or chimerical ideal "to a degree rarely attained by the wisest philosophers." Had they reasoned and consulted their immediate interests, perhaps no civilisation would have grown up.

## Ideas

- Every civilisation is the outcome of a small number of fundamental ideas, very rarely renewed; great historical perturbations come from changes in them.
- **Two classes of idea.** **Accidental and passing ideas** are created by the influences of the moment, such as an infatuation for an individual or a doctrine. **Fundamental ideas** are given great stability by environment, heredity and public opinion: the religious beliefs of the past, the social and democratic ideas of today. The fundamental ideas are the volume of a stream; the passing ones are the small waves on its surface, more visible but without real importance.
- **Contradiction without strain.** Image-like ideas, unconnected by any logical bond, replace one another like magic-lantern slides, so a crowd holds contradictory ideas at once and acts on whichever the moment brings up. The same is seen in individuals akin to primitive beings, such as fervent sectaries, and in educated Hindoos with European degrees, whose Western ideas overlie hereditary ones. Only hereditary ideas are strong enough to become motives of conduct; real contradiction in acts arises only in a man placed between different hereditary tendencies.
- **Lowering to reach the crowd.** Lofty philosophic or scientific ideas must be transformed, always "belittling and in the direction of simplification", before they become popular. Socially there is "scarcely any such thing as a hierarchy of ideas". The Christian ideas of the Middle Ages and the democratic and social ideas of the modern age are, philosophically, "somewhat sorry errors", and their power has been immense.
- **Proof is not enough.** Even when an idea's justness has been proved it does not produce action, even on cultivated minds, until it has entered the unconscious and become a sentiment. Once it has penetrated, its power is irresistible and opposition is bootless. The ideas behind the French Revolution took nearly a century to implant, then shook every throne and filled twenty years with hecatombs.

## Reasoning

- Crowd reasoning rests, like higher reasoning, on the association of ideas, but its bonds are only apparent analogy or succession. The Esquimaux, knowing that transparent ice melts in the mouth, concludes that transparent glass will too; the savage eats a brave foe's heart to gain his courage; the workman exploited by one employer concludes that all employers exploit their men.
- Those who know how to manage crowds present only such arguments, the only ones that work. A chain of logical argument is incomprehensible to a crowd. Speeches that read weakly had enormous effect because they were made to persuade collectivities, not to be read by philosophers: "twenty volumes of harangues... are not worth the few phrases which appealed to the brains it was required to convince."
- Lacking the critical spirit, crowds cannot tell truth from error. Many individuals never rise above this level: opinions spread easily because most men cannot form an opinion of their own based on their own reasoning.

## Imagination

- The figurative imagination of crowds is very powerful and active. Like a sleeper whose reason is suspended, a crowd lets images of extreme intensity arise. Lacking any notion of improbability, it is struck most by the most improbable things, so the marvellous and legendary side of events is what strikes it.
- **Theatre** shows the image in its most visible form, which is why it has such influence ("Bread and spectacular shows" were the Roman plebeians' ideal of happiness). A popular theatre that played only sombre dramas had to give the actor who played the traitor protection as he left, against spectators enraged by his imaginary crimes. "The unreal has almost as much influence on them as the real."
- **The basis of power.** "The power of conquerors and the strength of States is based on the popular imagination." The rise of Buddhism, Christianity and Islam, the Reformation, the French Revolution and socialism all come from strong impressions on the crowd's imagination. Napoleon: "It was by becoming a Catholic... that I terminated the Vendeen war. By becoming a Mussulman that I obtained a footing in Egypt." Striking the imagination was his constant preoccupation, in his victories, his harangues and all his acts.
- **Not by demonstration.** Antony did not raise the populace against Caesar's murderers by cunning rhetoric. He read the will and pointed to the corpse.
- **What strikes the imagination**: a startling, very clear image, freed of accessory explanation or accompanied only by a few marvellous or mysterious facts, such as a great victory, a great miracle, a great crime or a great hope. Lay things before the crowd as a whole and never indicate their genesis. An influenza epidemic that killed five thousand people in Paris made little impression because it came only as weekly statistics; an accident killing five hundred in one day, in public (the fall of the Eiffel Tower, say), would have made an immense one. The probable loss of one transatlantic liner gripped the public for a week, while the 850 sailing vessels and 203 steamers lost in 1894 went unnoticed. Facts must be condensed into "a startling image which fills and besets the mind."

## The religious shape of all convictions

- **The religious sentiment** has very simple characteristics: "worship of a being supposed superior, fear of the power with which the being is credited, blind submission to its commands, inability to discuss its dogmas, the desire to spread them, and a tendency to consider as enemies all by whom they are not accepted." Whether its object is an invisible God, an idol, a hero or a political conception, its essence is religious, and crowds credit the formula or the victorious leader of the moment with a mysterious power.
- "A person is not religious solely when he worships a divinity, but when he puts all the resources of his mind, the complete submission of his will, and the whole-souled ardour of fanaticism at the service of a cause or an individual who becomes the goal and guide of his thoughts and actions."
- **Intolerance and fanaticism are its necessary accompaniments**, shown by all who believe they hold the secret of earthly or eternal happiness. The Jacobins of the Terror were as religious as the Catholics of the Inquisition. "The methods of the Inquisition are those of all whose convictions are genuine and sturdy."
- **Founders of creeds** succeed by inspiring fanatical sentiments that make men find their happiness in worship and obedience. Fustel de Coulanges: the Roman Empire lasted five centuries not by force (thirty legions could not have held a hundred million men) but by the religious admiration it inspired; the emperor was worshipped with altars in the smallest townships, and sixty cities of Gaul built a temple to Augustus near Lyons. Napoleon was such a god for fifteen years.
- **The gods never disappear.** "Sentiment has never been vanquished in its eternal conflict with reason." Crowds reject the words divinity and religion yet have never had so many fetishes, statues and altars. Under Boulangism every country inn held the hero's portrait, and he was credited with power to remedy every injustice.
- It is "a very useless commonplace to assert that a religion is necessary for the masses", because every political, divine or social creed takes root among them only by assuming the religious shape, "a shape which obviates the danger of discussion." Atheism adopted by the masses would become a cult. Dostoiewsky's Nihilist smashed the images on a chapel altar, set the works of atheist philosophers in their place, and piously relit the candles: the object changed, the sentiment did not.
- **Reading history.** The Reformation, the massacre of Saint Bartholomew, the religious wars, the Inquisition and the Terror are phenomena of one kind: crowds animated by religious sentiment, extirpating by fire and sword whoever opposes the new faith. "The Revolution was merely the establishment of a new religious belief in the mind of the masses." Taine studied it as a naturalist and saw only a horde of epileptic savages. Such upheavals come from the soul of the masses, never from potentates: the most despotic monarch can only hasten or retard their appearance. Saint Bartholomew was no more the work of a king than the Terror was the work of Robespierre, Danton or Saint Just.
