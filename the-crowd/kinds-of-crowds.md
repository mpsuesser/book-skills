# Kinds of crowds: classification, criminal crowds, juries, electorates, parliaments, and the cycle of a civilisation

## Classification

Start from the simple **multitude**. Its lowest form is made of individuals of different races, held together only by the more or less respected will of a chief, like the barbarians of very diverse origin who invaded the Roman Empire. Higher are multitudes that have acquired common characteristics and formed a single race; they sometimes show crowd characteristics, but racial considerations overrule them to some degree. Either kind can be transformed into an organised or psychological crowd, and organised crowds divide as follows.

**A. Heterogeneous crowds**
1. Anonymous: street crowds, for example.
2. Not anonymous: juries, parliamentary assemblies, and the like.

**B. Homogeneous crowds**
1. **Sects** (political, religious): the first step in the organisation of homogeneous crowds. Members differ greatly in education, profession and class, and their only link is their common beliefs.
2. **Castes** (military, priestly, working): "the highest degree of organisation of which the crowd is susceptible." Members share a profession, and so a similar education and much the same social status.
3. **Classes** (middle, peasant or agricultural): members of diverse origin, linked neither by beliefs nor by profession but "by certain interests and certain habits of life and education almost identical."

Le Bon treats only heterogeneous crowds in this book and reserves sects, castes and classes for another volume.

**Reading a heterogeneous crowd:**

- **Race first.** A crowd of Englishmen or Chinamen gathered at random differs widely from a random crowd of Russians, Frenchmen or Spaniards. When circumstances bring nationalities together in one crowd in roughly equal numbers, the differences show at once, however identical their interests appear: the socialists' international congresses of workers always ended in the most pronounced discord. A Latin crowd, however revolutionary or conservative, always appeals to the State to realise its demands, tends toward centralisation and leans more or less toward dictatorship. An English or American crowd sets no store by the State and appeals to private initiative. "A French crowd lays particular weight on equality and an English crowd on liberty." Hence there are almost as many forms of socialism and democracy as there are nations.
- **The law of race.** "THE INFERIOR CHARACTERISTICS OF CROWDS ARE THE LESS ACCENTUATED IN PROPORTION AS THE SPIRIT OF THE RACE IS STRONG." The crowd state and the domination of crowds are the barbarian state, or a return to it. A race frees itself from the unreflecting power of crowds by acquiring "a solidly constituted collective spirit".
- **Anonymity.** The only other important division is into anonymous and not anonymous crowds. The sentiment of responsibility, absent from the first and developed in the second, often gives their acts very different tendencies.

## Crowds termed criminal crowds

- After a period of excitement crowds enter a purely automatic, unconscious state in which suggestion guides them, so calling them criminal is hardly correct. Le Bon keeps the term only because recent research has made it current. Some of their acts are criminal in themselves, but in the way a tiger is criminal when it devours a Hindoo after letting its young maul him for amusement.
- "The usual motive of the crimes of crowds is a powerful suggestion, and the individuals who take part in such crimes are afterwards convinced that they have acted in obedience to duty, which is far from being the case with the ordinary criminal." Such an act is a crime legally, not psychologically.
- Criminal crowds have the general characteristics of all crowds: openness to suggestion, credulity, mobility, the exaggeration of good or bad sentiments, and the display of certain forms of morality.

**The murder of de Launay**, governor of the Bastille. After the fortress fell, an excited crowd beat him from every side and proposed hanging him, beheading him or tying him to a horse's tail. Struggling, he accidentally kicked one of them. Someone suggested, to acclamation, that the man he had kicked should cut his throat. That man, an unemployed cook who had come out of idle curiosity, judged the act patriotic since everyone thought so, and believed he deserved a medal. The sword he was lent was too blunt, so he finished the work with his own small black-handled knife. The mechanism: obedience to a suggestion made stronger by its collective origin, and the murderer's conviction that his act was meritorious, which the unanimous approval of his fellow citizens made natural.

**The September massacres** resembled Saint Bartholomew. Who gave the order to empty the prisons by killing the prisoners is unknown, probably Danton; what matters is the powerful suggestion the crowd received.

- **Composition.** About three hundred people, a perfectly typical heterogeneous crowd: apart from a few professional scoundrels, shopkeepers and artisans of every trade, such as bootmakers, locksmiths, hairdressers, masons, clerks and messengers. They were judges and executioners at once and never thought themselves criminals.
- **Rudimentary justice.** They set up a sort of tribunal. Nobles, priests, officers and members of the king's household, whose profession alone proved their guilt to a good patriot, were to be killed in a body without separate decision; the rest were judged on appearance and reputation. That satisfied the crowd's rudimentary conscience and let it give its ferocity free rein "legally".
- **Contrary sentiments together.** A tenderness as extreme as the ferocity appeared beside it. One federate wanted to kill a gaoler who had left prisoners without water for twenty-six hours. Acquitted prisoners were embraced with transports of joy by guards and killers alike, and then the slaughter resumed. Gaiety reigned: there was dancing and singing around the corpses, and benches were set out "for the ladies". When a killer complained that the ladies could not see well and that too few present had the pleasure of striking, victims were made to walk slowly between two rows of killers who struck only with the backs of their swords to prolong the agony.
- **Scruples.** The killers refused to keep their victims' money and jewels and took them to the committees' table.
- **Rudimentary reasoning.** After 1,200 or 1,500 "enemies of the nation" had been killed, someone remarked that the other prisons, holding old beggars, vagabonds and young prisoners, held useless mouths better got rid of, and enemies of the people among them, such as a poisoner's widow who "must be furious at being in prison... she must have said so, she has said so." The argument convinced them, and everyone was massacred, including some fifty children aged twelve to seventeen.
- **Reward.** After a week's work the killers, sure they had deserved well of their country, asked the authorities for a recompense, and the most zealous claimed a medal.

The Commune of 1871 offers analogous facts. With the growing influence of crowds and the successive capitulations of authority before them, more are to come.

## Criminal juries

The jury of the Court of Assize is a heterogeneous crowd that is not anonymous. It shows suggestibility and little capacity for reasoning, it is open to the influence of leaders, and it is guided mainly by unconscious sentiments.

- **Composition does not change verdicts.** On any question not wholly technical, intelligence counts for nothing in a deliberative body: men of science or artists, by the mere fact of assembling, judge general subjects no differently from masons or grocers. Before 1848 French juries were picked from the enlightened classes (professors, functionaries, men of letters); later they were drawn mostly from small tradesmen, petty capitalists and employees. The verdicts stayed the same, as Berard des Glajeux, a former President of the Court of Assizes, admitted: "ITS VERDICTS HAVE REMAINED THE SAME." The great barrister Lachaud systematically challenged every intelligent juror. Experience proved the challenges useless, the Paris bar gave them up, and the verdicts were "neither better nor worse."
- **Sentiment over argument.** Juries are strongly moved by sentiment and barely by argument: "They cannot resist the sight... of a mother giving its child the breast, or of orphans." An agreeable-looking woman wins their benevolence.
- **Severe or indulgent.** Juries are pitiless toward crimes of which they might themselves be victims, which are also the most dangerous to society. They are very indulgent toward crimes of passion: they are rarely severe on infanticide by an abandoned girl-mother, or hard on the young woman who throws vitriol at the man who seduced and deserted her. They feel instinctively that society runs little danger from such crimes, and that where the law does not protect deserted girls, the girl's revenge frightens future seducers. That instinctive division of crimes into dangerous and not dangerous is just, since the object of criminal law is to protect society, not to avenge it. French magistrates, by contrast, are still imbued with the vengeance of primitive law. Many refuse to apply Berenger's law, under which a first offender serves his sentence only if he offends again, although statistics prove that a first punishment "infallibly leads to further crime." They would rather create a dangerous, confirmed criminal than leave society unavenged.
- **Prestige.** Juries are democratic in composition and aristocratic in their likes: "Name, birth, great wealth, celebrity, the assistance of an illustrious counsel, everything in the nature of distinction or that lends brilliancy to the accused, stands him in extremely good stead."

**How counsel should work a jury.** The chief concern is to work on the jurors' feelings and, as with every crowd, to argue little or only in rudimentary ways. A famous English assize barrister's method:

1. Watch the jury closely while pleading, reading the effect of each phrase on the jurors' faces and drawing conclusions from it.
2. First make sure which jurors are already favourable, and secure them, which is quick work.
3. Then turn to those who seem ill-disposed and find out why they are hostile. This is the delicate part, "for there may be an infinity of reasons for condemning a man, apart from the sentiment of justice."

That is the whole mechanism of oratory, and it is why a speech prepared in advance does so little: the terms have to change from moment to moment with the impression produced.

- **Win the leading spirits.** The orator need not convert every juror, only the leading spirits who will determine the general opinion: "one or two energetic men suffice to carry the rest of the jury with them." Convince those two or three by skilful suggestion, and "First of all, and above all, it is necessary to please them. The man forming part of a crowd whom one has succeeded in pleasing is on the point of being convinced", ready to accept any argument as excellent.
- **Lachaud and the seventh juror.** Lachaud never lost sight of the two or three jurors he knew or felt to be influential but obstinate. Once, in the provinces, he spent three-quarters of an hour of his most cunning arguments on the seventh juror, the first on the second bench, without effect. In the middle of a passionate passage he stopped and asked the President to have the curtain drawn: "The seventh juryman is blinded by the sun." The juror reddened, smiled and thanked him, and was won for the defence.

**Keep the jury.** Many writers campaign against the jury, yet it is the only protection against the frequent errors of an uncontrolled caste, the magistracy.

- A jury drawn only from the enlightened classes would return the same verdicts.
- Replacing jurors with judges forgets that the errors blamed on juries were made first by judges. An accused man reaches the jury already held guilty by the examining magistrate (juge d'instruction), the public prosecutor and the Court of Arraignment; judged by magistrates alone, he would lose his only chance of being found innocent. "The errors of juries have always been first of all the errors of magistrates." Faced with technical details it cannot follow, a jury naturally defers to the prosecutor, trusting that magistrates have investigated the affair.
- The jury "constitutes, perhaps, the only category of crowd that cannot be replaced by any individuality." It alone can temper the severity of a law that, equal for all, must in principle be blind to particular cases. A judge would give the burglar who murdered and the wretched girl driven to infanticide by poverty and desertion the same penalty. The jury feels that the seduced girl is much less guilty than her seducer, whom the law does not touch.
- The magistracy is the only administration under no control. France has no habeas corpus, and a young examining magistrate fresh from the university can imprison the most considerable persons on mere supposition for six months or a year and release them without indemnity or excuse: the warrant is a lettre de cachet in the hands of a whole class.
- "The power of crowds is to be dreaded, but the power of certain castes is to be dreaded yet more. Crowds are open to conviction; castes never are." Wrongly accused, Le Bon would always rather face a jury than magistrates.

## Electoral crowds

**Electoral crowds** are collectivities invested with the power of electing the holders of certain functions. They are heterogeneous, but because their action is confined to one clearly determined matter, choosing between candidates, they show only a few crowd characteristics: slight aptitude for reasoning, absence of the critical spirit, irritability, credulity and simplicity. The influence of leaders, and of affirmation, repetition, prestige and contagion, shows in their decisions. Their psychology can be deduced from the methods that win them over.

**The candidate's methods:**

1. **Prestige first.** "Personal prestige can only be replaced by that resulting from wealth. Talent and even genius are not elements of success of serious importance." Prestige means being able "to force himself upon the electorate without discussion." Working men and peasants rarely elect one of their own because such a man has no prestige among them. When they do, it is for subsidiary reasons, such as spiting an eminent man or an employer on whom the voter depends daily and whose master, for a moment, he has the illusion of becoming.
2. **Flatter greed and vanity.** Prestige is not enough. The elector must be overwhelmed with extravagant blandishments and given the most fantastic promises without hesitation. With working men it is impossible to go too far in insulting and stigmatising employers.
3. **Destroy the rival by affirmation.** Establish by affirmation, repetition and contagion that he is an arrant scoundrel and that his several crimes are common knowledge, with no semblance of proof. A rival who does not understand crowds will defend himself with arguments instead of answering one set of affirmations with another, and will have no chance at all.
4. **Two programmes.** Keep the written programme vague, since opponents can bring it up later. The verbal programme cannot be too exaggerated: promise the most important reforms fearlessly. The exaggerations work at the moment and bind no one, because the elector never troubles to check whether the man he elected kept the programme he applauded.
5. **Words and formulas.** Worn expressions such as "infamous capital", "vile exploiters", "the admirable working man" and "the socialisation of wealth" still work. "The candidate who hits on a new formula as devoid as possible of precise meaning, and apt in consequence to flatter the most varied aspirations, infallibly obtains a success."

**The federal republic.** The bloody Spanish revolution of 1873 sprang from one such phrase. The radicals discovered that a centralised republic was a monarchy in disguise, and to please them the Cortes unanimously proclaimed a FEDERAL REPUBLIC, though no member could have explained what he had voted for. The joy was delirious; a republican refused the title of federalist felt mortally insulted. Some took the phrase to mean provincial emancipation and American-style decentralisation. Others took it to mean the abolition of all authority and the great social liquidation. The socialists of Barcelona and Andalusia wanted ten thousand independent, self-legislating municipalities and the suppression of the police and the army. Villages that declared themselves cut their telegraph wires and railway lines, federation became cantonalism, and massacres and burnings spread across the country.

**No reasoning reaches the elector.** At an election meeting people exchange affirmations, invective and sometimes blows, never arguments. Silence falls only when some "tough customer" announces an embarrassing question for the candidate, and his voice is soon drowned by his opponents' uproar. This does not depend on the voters' class: "In every anonymous assembly whatever, though it be composed exclusively of highly educated persons, discussion always assumes the same shape." A meeting of students was as tumultuous as any meeting of workmen.

**The committees.** "Crowds have opinions that have been imposed upon them, but they never boast reasoned opinions." Voters' opinions and votes are in the hands of the election committees, whose leading spirits are usually publicans, powerful over the workmen they give credit to. Scherer: "France is governed to-day by the election committees." Committees, clubs and syndicates of every name are perhaps the most redoubtable danger in the power of crowds: "the most impersonal and, in consequence, the most oppressive form of tyranny", since their leaders speak in the name of a collectivity and so escape all responsibility. The committees of the Revolution decimated the Convention at their pleasure. Robespierre was absolute while he spoke in their name and lost the moment he broke with them out of personal pride. "The reign of crowds is the reign of committees, that is, of the leaders of crowds. A severer despotism cannot be imagined." Influencing the committees is not hard for a candidate who is acceptable in himself and has enough money: by its donors' own account, three million francs secured General Boulanger's repeated elections.

**Universal suffrage.** The psychology of electoral crowds is that of all crowds, neither better nor worse, and Le Bon would keep universal suffrage as it is, for practical reasons drawn from that psychology.

- **Its weak side.** Civilisation is the work of a small minority of superior intelligences at the top of a pyramid that widens as mental power decreases, and its greatness cannot depend on the votes of inferior elements with only numbers on their side. Crowd votes have already cost several invasions.
- **The dogma.** These objections lose all force in practice against the invincible strength of an idea turned into dogma. The sovereignty of crowds is as philosophically indefensible as the religious dogmas of the Middle Ages and as absolute in its power. A freethinker transported to the Middle Ages and facing a judge who meant to burn him for a pact with the devil would not have thought of questioning the devil's existence. "It were as wise to oppose cyclones with discussion as the beliefs of crowds." Time alone can act on it. It also has an appearance of reason in its favour. Tocqueville: in an era of equality men have no faith in each other because they are all alike, yet that same likeness gives them "an almost limitless confidence in the judgment of the public", since it seems improbable that truth should not go with the greater number.
- **Restricting it would change nothing.** A suffrage limited to the intellectually capable would vote no better: "on general questions, a vote, recorded by forty academicians is no better than that of forty water-carriers." Knowing Greek or mathematics, or being an architect, vet, doctor or barrister, gives no special understanding of social questions. The political economists, nearly all professors or academicians, cannot agree on a single general question such as protection or bimetallism, because "their science is only a very attenuated form of our universal ignorance." Social problems hold so many unknown quantities that men are substantially equally ignorant of them. An electorate of the learned would vote by sentiment and party spirit, spare none of the present difficulties, and add the oppressive tyranny of castes.
- **What it expresses.** Restricted or universal, under a republic or a monarchy, in France, Belgium, Greece, Portugal or Spain, the suffrage is everywhere the same: "the expression of the unconscious aspirations and needs of the race." The average opinions of those elected represent the genius of the race and barely change from one generation to the next. "Race and the slavery of our daily necessities are the mysterious master-causes that rule our destiny."

## Parliamentary assemblies

Parliamentary assemblies are heterogeneous crowds that are not anonymous. Whatever the mode of election, their characteristics are much the same across Greece, Italy, Portugal, Spain, France and America: race weakens or exaggerates them but does not prevent them. The parliamentary system expresses an idea that is "psychologically erroneous, but generally admitted, that a large gathering of men is much more capable than a small number of coming to a wise and independent decision." Assemblies show intellectual simplicity, irritability, suggestibility, exaggerated sentiments and the preponderating influence of a few leaders, with features of their own.

- **Simplicity of opinions.** Assemblies, Latin ones especially, tend to solve the most complicated social problems by the simplest abstract principles and general laws applied to every case, to exaggerate the worth of their principles and to push them to extreme consequences. Parliaments therefore represent extreme opinions above all. The Jacobins were the perfect example: dogmatic and logical, full of vague generalities, they applied fixed principles without regard to events, "went through the Revolution without witnessing it", and thought they could recast society from top to bottom, while in fact they only destroyed what stood in their way. Girondists, Mountain and Thermidorians shared the spirit.
- **Fixed opinions on local interests.** Suggestion comes from leaders with prestige, but it has clear limits. On every question of local or regional interest, such as protection or the privilege of distilling alcohol, where influential electors' interests are involved, each member has fixed opinions that no argument shakes, and the talent of a Demosthenes could not change his vote: the electors' suggestion, received before the vote, outweighs every other. An English parliamentarian of fifty years at Westminster said that of thousands of speeches, few had changed his opinion and "not one of them has changed my vote."
- **Indecision on general questions.** On general questions, such as overthrowing a Cabinet or imposing a tax, opinion is not fixed and the leaders' suggestions work. Each party's leaders often have equal influence, though, so the deputy stands between two contrary suggestions and hesitates. He votes opposite ways within a quarter of an hour, or adds an article that nullifies a law, as when an assembly withdrew employers' right to choose and dismiss their workmen and then nearly annulled the measure by an amendment. Every Chamber therefore has some very stable opinions and some very shifting ones, and since general questions are the more numerous, indecision predominates. It comes from the ever-present fear of the elector, whose latent suggestion counterbalances the leaders.
- **Leaders rule.** On the many matters where members have no strong preconceived opinion, the leaders are the masters. They exist in every country's assembly as "heads of groups", and they are its real rulers, so "the votes of an assembly only represent, as a rule, the opinions of a small minority." Their influence owes little to their arguments and much to their prestige, as their loss of influence the moment they lose prestige proves.
- **Prestige independent of fame.** Jules Simon, a member of the Assembly of 1848, recorded that Louis Napoleon was entirely without importance two months before he became all-powerful, that Victor Hugo failed at the tribune, and that Edgar Quinet, for all his powerful intelligence, was held in no esteem there: "The splendour of genius makes itself less felt in political assemblies than anywhere else." Assemblies give homage only under urgent danger (to Lamartine in 1848, to Thiers in 1871) and forget their gratitude the moment the danger has passed. A crowd would stop being a crowd if it credited its leaders with their services; it submits to their prestige, not out of interest or gratitude.
- **Almost absolute power.** A leader with enough prestige wields almost absolute power. A celebrated deputy (Clemenceau, per the translator's note) could bring down Cabinets with a signal, and a writer charged him with making France pay three times too much for Tonkin, keeping it on a precarious footing in Madagascar, costing it an empire on the Lower Niger and losing its preponderance in Egypt: his theories "cost us more territories than the disasters of Napoleon I." Yet much of his influence came from following public opinion. "A leader is seldom in advance of public opinion; almost always all he does is to follow it and to espouse all its errors."
- **The leader's eloquence.** Beyond his prestige, the leader must understand crowd psychology, if only unconsciously, and know "the fascinating influence of words, phrases, and images." His eloquence is made of "energetic affirmations—unburdened with proofs—and impressive images, accompanied by very summary arguments." It is found in every assembly, the English Parliament included. Maine observed that Commons debates often amount to "an exchange of rather weak generalities and rather violent personalities", and that crowds accept general assertions in striking terms that have never been, and perhaps cannot be, verified.
- **Striking terms.** Choose words that evoke very vivid images. One leader pictured a single ship carrying "the politician of shady reputation and the anarchist guilty of murder" to the fever-haunted penal settlements, conversing as two complementary aspects of one state of society. Every opponent felt threatened, since the category of politicians menaced was ill-defined enough to include him, just as the Convention, threatened with the guillotine by Robespierre's vague speeches, always yielded to him.
- **Improbable exaggeration pays.** The same speaker could affirm, without violent protest, that bankers and priests had subsidised bomb-throwers and that the directors of great financial companies deserved the punishment of anarchists. "The affirmation is never too violent, the declamation never too threatening." Nothing intimidates an audience more, because each listener fears that protesting will mark him as a traitor or accomplice. In crises the effect grows: the orators of the Revolution kept pausing to denounce crime and exalt virtue, cursed tyrants and swore to live free or perish, and the house rose, applauded furiously and sat down again.
- **Intelligence harms the leader.** A leader may be intelligent and highly educated, but these qualities usually do him more harm than good. "By showing how complex things are, by allowing of explanation and promoting comprehension, intelligence always renders its owner indulgent, and blunts, in a large measure, that intensity and violence of conviction needful for apostles." The great leaders of every age, and of the Revolution above all, were of "lamentably narrow intellect", and those of the most restricted intelligence had the most influence. Robespierre's speeches, read today, astonish by their incoherence and explain nothing of his power. Strong conviction combined with extreme narrowness of mind gives a man with prestige a terrible power, and those conditions are needed for a man to ignore obstacles and show great strength of will. "Crowds instinctively recognise in men of energy and conviction the masters they are always in need of."
- **The speaker without prestige.** The success of a speech in an assembly "depends almost solely on the prestige possessed by the speaker, and not at all on the arguments he brings forward." An unknown speaker with good arguments, and only arguments, will at best be heard. The deputy Desaubes drew his portrait. He spreads his document out methodically, sure that his weighed arguments, figures and proofs will convince colleagues whose only wish must be to embrace the truth. He is surprised by the restlessness and noise. He frowns and stops, then starts again louder at the President's urging, and is listened to less. He adds emphasis and gestures, and the noise grows until he cannot hear himself. Fearing the cry of "The Closure!", he presses on into an unbearable clamour.
- **The automatic state.** At a certain pitch of excitement an assembly becomes identical with an ordinary heterogeneous crowd: its sentiments are always extreme, and members vote measures most adverse to their own interests. The nobility renounced its privileges in one night of the Constituent Assembly. The Convention renounced its inviolability and decimated itself, knowing that the scaffold for colleagues today might be its members' own tomorrow. Billaud-Varennes: the decisions "WERE NOT DESIRED BY US TWO DAYS, A SINGLE DAY BEFORE THEY WERE TAKEN: IT WAS THE CRISIS AND NOTHING ELSE THAT GAVE RISE TO THEM." Taine: unanimously and amid applause the Left, backed by the Right, sent Danton, its natural chief, to the scaffold. The Right, backed by the Left, voted the worst revolutionary decrees, and both kept in office a homicidal government each detested, consenting to their own suicide. Spuller wrote of the Assembly of 1848 that its members were "More fluid than water", reflecting every line and assuming every shape, as promptly discouraged as exalted, never in the mood the situation required.
- **When an assembly is not a crowd.** Assemblies are crowds only at certain moments. Their members keep their individuality in many cases, which is why an assembly can produce excellent technical laws. Such a law is really the work of an individual, a specialist who prepared it in the quiet of his study, and it is the best kind; it turns disastrous only when a series of amendments makes it a collective effort. "The work of a crowd is always inferior, whatever its nature, to that of an isolated individual." Specialists keep assemblies from passing ill-advised or unworkable measures: the specialist is "a temporary leader of crowds. The Assembly is without influence on him, but he has influence over the Assembly."

**The verdict on parliaments.** Despite all this, parliamentary assemblies are "the best form of government mankind has discovered as yet", and above all the best means found so far to escape the yoke of personal tyranny. They are the ideal government for philosophers, thinkers, writers, artists and the learned, the cream of a civilisation. They carry two serious dangers:

1. **Financial waste**, inevitable, with two causes. First, the exigencies and lack of foresight of electoral crowds: when a member proposes a measure that seems to satisfy democratic ideas, such as old-age pensions for all workers or higher pay for some class of State employees, the other deputies, dreading their electors, dare not vote against it, though they know it strains the Budget and needs new taxes. The cost is remote and does not fall on them personally, while a negative vote might come to light at the next election. Second, the necessity of voting every grant for local purposes, since each deputy gets his own constituency's grants only by voting for his colleagues'. Railways were voted costing 15 million francs to join Langayes, a mountain town of 3,000 inhabitants, to Puy, and 7 millions to join Oust, a village of 523, to Seix, of 1,200; in 1895 alone 90 millions went to railways of only local utility. Workmen's pensions were estimated at 165 to 800 millions a year. Several European states had already gone bankrupt, and their bondholders had accepted the loss of four-fifths of their coupons.
2. **The progressive restriction of liberty**, less obvious but very real. Parliaments feel obliged to vote innumerable laws, nearly all restrictive, and their shortsightedness blinds them to the consequences. Even England has not escaped. Herbert Spencer showed that the increase of apparent liberty must be followed by a decrease of real liberty: every year more regulations constrain citizens in matters once free or force acts once optional, and heavier public and local burdens shrink the share of a man's earnings he may spend as he chooses. Le Bon adds what Spencer missed. Every such law increases the number, power and influence of the functionaries who apply it, and they become the real masters of civilised countries, because amid every change of government the administrative caste alone has "irresponsibility, impersonality, and perpetuity. There is no more oppressive despotism than that which presents itself under this triple form." Hemmed in by formalities over their pettiest acts, and "Victims of the delusion that equality and liberty are the better assured by the multiplication of laws", nations accept ever heavier trammels. Used to every yoke, they come to desire servitude and lose all spontaneity and energy, becoming "passive, unresisting and powerless automata". The individual then seeks outside himself the forces he no longer finds within. Government functions grow as citizens' indifference and helplessness grow, and the State must supply the initiative and enterprise private persons lack: "The State becomes an all-powerful god." The power of such gods has never been very durable or very strong. This restriction of liberty, under an outward license that preserves the illusion of it, seems as much a consequence of a people's old age as of any system, and it is a forerunner of the decadent phase no civilisation has yet escaped.

## The cycle of the life of a people

Several modern civilisations have reached the extreme old age that precedes decadence, and since all peoples seem to pass through the same phases, the cycle explains the power crowds now hold:

1. **Barbarism.** At the dawn of a civilisation, migrations, invasions and conquests bring together a swarm of men of different blood, languages and beliefs, bound only by the half-recognised law of a chief. Crowd characteristics are present in an eminent degree: transient cohesion, heroism, weakness, impulsiveness, violence. "They are barbarians."
2. **A race forms.** Time does its work. Identity of surroundings, repeated intermingling and the necessities of common life blend the dissimilar units into a race, with common characteristics and sentiments that heredity makes ever more fixed. "The crowd has become a people."
3. **An ideal.** The people fully leaves barbarism only when, after long efforts, repeated struggles and countless new beginnings, it has acquired an ideal. Its nature matters little (the cult of Rome, the might of Athens, the triumph of Allah); it is enough that it gives every individual of the forming race perfect unity of sentiment and thought.
4. **Civilisation.** A civilisation is born, with its institutions, beliefs and arts, and in pursuit of its ideal the race gains splendour, vigour and grandeur. It may still be a crowd at times, but beneath the crowd's shifting traits now lies a solid substratum, the genius of the race, which confines the nation's transformations within narrow limits and overrules chance.
5. **Old age.** Having reached a certain level of strength and complexity, the civilisation stops growing and is condemned to a speedy decline. The hour is always marked by the weakening of the ideal that held the race together, and every religious, political and social structure it inspired begins to shake.
6. **Dissolution.** As the ideal perishes, the race loses its cohesion, unity and strength. Individual personality and intelligence may grow, but the collective egoism of the race gives way to an excessive egoism of the individual, with weaker character and less capacity for action. The people becomes an agglomeration of individuals held together artificially for a time by traditions and institutions. Divided by their interests and unable to govern themselves, men need directing in their pettiest acts, and the State's influence becomes absorbing.
7. **Back to the crowd.** With the final loss of the ideal, the genius of the race disappears and the people returns to its original state, a swarm of isolated individuals, a crowd, without consistency or future. "The populace is sovereign, and the tide of barbarism mounts." The civilisation may still look brilliant from the front a long past built, but it is a crumbling edifice that will fall in at the first storm.

"To pass in pursuit of an ideal from the barbarous to the civilised state, and then, when this ideal has lost its virtue, to decline and die, such is the cycle of the life of a people."
