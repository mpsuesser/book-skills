# The opinions and beliefs of crowds: factors, words, illusions, fixed and changeable beliefs

Depth for the remote and immediate factors and for the two classes of belief in `SKILL.md`. "Crowds are somewhat like the sphinx of ancient fable: it is necessary to arrive at a solution of the problems offered by their psychology or to resign ourselves to being devoured by them."

## Remote factors

### Race

- Race stands first, far above every other factor. Once an **historical race** has formed its character through heredity, its beliefs, institutions and arts are "merely the outward expression of its genius", and no element of civilisation (language, religion, arts) passes from one people to another without profound transformation.
- Environment, circumstances and events are "the social suggestions of the moment": they may be strong, but their influence is always momentary when it runs against the suggestions of the race, which a nation inherits from the whole series of its ancestors.
- Racial influence dominates the characteristics peculiar to crowds, so the crowds of different countries differ greatly in beliefs and conduct and are not to be influenced in the same way. Napoleon understood the French masses and misread other races. Talleyrand assured him Spain "would receive his soldiers as liberators"; it received them as beasts of prey, which anyone who knew the hereditary instincts of the Spanish race would have foreseen. Spain, and above all Russia, began his ruin.

### Traditions

- "Traditions represent the ideas, the needs, and the sentiments of the past. They are the synthesis of the race, and weigh upon us with immense force."
- "A people is an organism created by the past, and, like every other organism, it can only be modified by slow hereditary accumulations." Many statesmen are still where the theorists of the last century were, believing a society could break with its past and be recast by the light of reason alone.
- Tradition guides men, above all in crowds, and the changes they can easily make in it bear only on names and outward forms.
- **The equilibrium problem.** "Civilisation is impossible without traditions, and progress impossible without the destruction of those traditions." The immense difficulty is to find the balance between stability and variability. A people whose customs root too firmly can no longer change and becomes, like China, incapable of improvement. Violent revolution does not help: either the broken chain is pieced together and the past resumes its empire unchanged, or the pieces stay apart and "decadence soon succeeds anarchy."
- **The ideal** is to keep the institutions of the past and change them "insensibly and little by little." The Romans in ancient times and the English in modern times are almost alone in having done it.
- Crowds, and castes above all, cling hardest to tradition. With churches wrecked and priests exiled or guillotined, it looked as though the old religion had lost its strength; within a few years public worship had to be re-established because people everywhere demanded it. The ex-Conventionist Fourcroy reported that the great majority of men "stand in need of religion, public worship, and priests."
- "The most redoubtable idols do not dwell in temples, nor the most despotic tyrants in palaces; both the one and the other can be broken in an instant. But the invisible masters that reign in our innermost selves are safe from every effort at revolt, and only yield to the slow wearing away of centuries."

### Time

- Time is "the sole real creator and the sole great destroyer." It built mountains from grains of sand, and an ant with enough time could level Mount Blanc.
- It causes the birth, growth and death of every belief, and it prepares the soil they grow in: "This is why certain ideas are realisable at one epoch and not at another." Ideas grow from an immense detritus of earlier beliefs and thoughts. To understand an idea's genesis, search the past: ideas "are the daughters of the past and the mothers of the future, but throughout the slaves of time."
- "Time... is our veritable master." Left free to act, it will restore equilibrium even after the threatening aspirations of the masses. Lavisse: no form of government was founded in a day; the feudal system and absolute monarchy each existed for centuries, shapeless and troubled, before finding their laws.

### Political and social institutions

- **The delusion**: that institutions can remedy the defects of societies, that national progress follows from better institutions and governments, and that social change can be effected by decree. It was the starting point of the French Revolution and underlies the social theories of today.
- Institutions are the outcome of ideas, sentiments and customs, and these are not recast by recasting legislative codes. "A nation does not choose its institutions at will any more than it chooses the colour of its hair or its eyes." Institutions "are not the creators of an epoch, but are created by it." Centuries are needed to form a political system and centuries to change it. "Institutions have no intrinsic virtue: in themselves they are neither good nor bad"; what suits one people may be extremely harmful to another.
- **Names are labels.** A people can change the name of its institutions by violent revolution, but their essence remains. England, the most democratic country in the world, lives under a monarchy; the most oppressive despotisms are the Spanish-American republics, with their republican constitutions. The United States prospers under democratic institutions while the Spanish-American republics languish in anarchy under the same ones. "Peoples are governed by their character, and all institutions which are not intimately modelled on that character merely represent a borrowed garment, a transitory disguise."
- Manufacturing cut-and-dried constitutions is "a puerile task, the useless labour of an ignorant rhetorician." Necessity and time elaborate constitutions when they are allowed to act. **Macaulay's rules**, which guided England's Parliaments from John to Victoria: "To think nothing of symmetry and much of convenience; never to remove an anomaly merely because it is an anomaly; never to innovate except when some grievance is felt; never to innovate except so far as to get rid of the grievance; never to lay down any proposition of wider extent than the particular case for which it is necessary to provide."
- **The centralisation test.** When a people made of very different races spends a thousand years attaining centralisation, and a revolution aimed at destroying every past institution is forced to respect and even strengthen it, that centralisation is "the outcome of imperious needs" and a condition of the nation's existence. Destroying it would bring a frightful civil war and then a new centralisation more oppressive than the old. Apply the same test to any institution: one that survives a revolution that tried to sweep it away is an expression of the race.
- Wars and revolutions are fought to impose institutions credited, like saints' relics, with a supernatural power of creating welfare. What moves the crowd in them is not the institutions but illusions and words, "especially words".

### Instruction and education

- **The democratic dogma**: that instruction changes men considerably, improves them and even makes them equal. Repetition has made it as hard to attack as the dogmas of the Church once were.
- Psychology and experience disagree. Instruction makes a man neither more moral nor happier and changes neither his instincts nor his hereditary passions; badly directed, it is far more pernicious than useful. Criminality rises as instruction spreads, and anarchists are recruited among school prize-winners. The magistrate Adolphe Guillot counted 3,000 educated criminals for every 1,000 illiterate delinquents. In fifty years the criminal proportion of the population rose from 227 to 552 per 100,000 inhabitants, an increase of 133 per cent, and the rise was steepest among the young, for whom free and compulsory schooling had replaced apprenticeship in France.
- Well-directed instruction can still develop professional capacity.
- **The Latin error**: the belief that intelligence is developed by learning text-books by heart. From primary school to university the young man memorises, and his judgment and initiative are never called on: "Education consists for him in reciting by heart and obeying." Jules Simon called it "a ludicrous form of education whose every effort is an act of faith tacitly admitting the infallibility of the master."
- **Its graver danger** is that it gives people a violent dislike of the station they were born to. The workman no longer wants to stay a workman, nor the peasant a peasant, and the humble middle classes want nothing for their sons but State posts. At the bottom it breeds an army of proletarians discontented with their lot and always ready to revolt. At the top it breeds a frivolous bourgeoisie, at once sceptical and credulous, with a superstitious confidence in the State as a kind of Providence, yet always hostile to it, laying its own faults at the Government's door and incapable of any enterprise without the authorities.
- **The surplus of diplomas.** The State can employ only a few of the diploma-holders it makes and has the rest as its enemies. There were 20,000 unemployed schoolmasters and mistresses in the department of the Seine alone, while a businessman could hardly find an agent for the colonies. "The acquisition of knowledge for which no use can be found is a sure method of driving a man to revolt." The same is seen in China, with its mandarins selected by recitation of bulky manuals, and in India, where the educated Baboos who find no post become irreconcilable enemies of English rule.
- **What succeeds in life**: "judgment, experience, initiative, and character—qualities which are not bestowed by books. Books are dictionaries, which it is useful to consult, but of which it is perfectly useless to have lengthy portions in one's head."
- **Taine's case for professional instruction.** Ideas form only in their natural surroundings, through the innumerable impressions a young man receives daily in the workshop, the mine, the law court, the builder's yard and the hospital: tools, materials, customers, work done well or ill, costly or lucrative. These small perceptions, picked up involuntarily and silently worked over, later suggest a new combination, simplification, economy, improvement or invention. Of the young Frenchman's years of examination study, at least nine in ten are wasted. Half to two-thirds of candidates fail, and half to two-thirds of those who pass are overworked: they are living repertories of knowledge for two hours on examination day, and a month later no longer. The Anglo-Saxons train by object lessons and apprenticeship. The engineer learns in the workshop and rises to workman, foreman or engineer as far as his aptitudes take him, after a short general course that gives him a framework for his observations, and with technical courses in his spare hours to co-ordinate his daily experience. At twenty-five he is "not only a part of a machine, but also a motor."
- **Why this belongs to crowd psychology**: education shows how the ground is being prepared. "The instruction given the youth of a country allows of a knowledge of what that country will one day be." It is in part through instruction that the mind of the masses improves or deteriorates: "Education is the only means at our disposal of influencing to some extent the mind of a nation." The system has turned the mass of the indifferent and neutral into an army of the discontented, ready to obey every utopian and rhetorician.

## Immediate factors

### Images, words and formulas

- Images do not always lie ready to hand, but "the judicious employment of words and formulas" can evoke them. Handled with art, words have the mysterious power the adepts of magic once credited them with, raising and stilling the most formidable tempests in the minds of crowds.
- **The power of words is bound up with the images they evoke, independently of their real sense.** The vaguest words are often the strongest: democracy, socialism, equality, liberty, whose meaning bulky volumes cannot fix. These short syllables seem to hold the solution of every problem: "They synthesise the most diverse unconscious aspirations and the hope of their realisation." Reason and argument cannot fight them. Uttered solemnly before a crowd, they bow every head, and their vagueness only adds to their mysterious power.
- **The electric bell.** The images attached to a word vary from age to age and people to people while the formula stays the same: "the word is merely as it were the button of an electric bell that calls them up."
- **Worn words.** Some words lose their power with use and become "vain sounds, whose principal utility is to relieve the person who employs them of the obligation of thinking." With a small stock of formulas and commonplaces learnt young, a man can go through life without reflecting on anything.
- **Meanings drift by age.** Words change slowly while the images they evoke change ceaselessly, so absolute translation, above all of a dead language, is impossible: we put modern images in place of ancient ones. The men of the Revolution gave ancient words a sense they never had. A Greek republic was an aristocracy of petty despots ruling over slaves. "Liberty" meant nothing like the modern word in a city where discussing its gods, laws and customs was the gravest crime. "Fatherland" meant the cult of Athens or Sparta, not Greece. For the royalist emigrants it meant the sovereign, since feudal law bound the vassal to his lord, not to the soil.
- **Use the crowd's current meaning.** "Words, then, have only mobile and transitory significations which change from age to age and people to people." To influence a crowd, know the meaning the crowd gives a word at that moment, not what it once meant or means to minds of a different constitution.
- **Rename what the crowd hates.** When political upheaval or a change of belief has given the crowd a deep antipathy for the images certain words evoke, "the first duty of the true statesman is to change the words without, of course, laying hands on the things themselves", which are too bound up with the inherited constitution to change. Tocqueville observed that the Consulate and the Empire chiefly clothed the institutions of the past in new words. The taille became the land tax, the gabelle the tax on salt, the aids the indirect contributions and consolidated duties, and the tax on trade companies and guilds the license. "One of the most essential functions of statesmen consists, then, in baptizing with popular or, at any rate, indifferent words things the crowd cannot endure under their old names." Well-chosen words make the most odious things acceptable: by invoking liberty and fraternity the Jacobins installed, in Taine's words, "a despotism worthy of Dahomey."
- "The art of those who govern, as is the case with the art of advocates, consists above all in the science of employing words." The difficulty is that within one society the different classes use the same words with different meanings: they "employ in appearance the same words, but never speak the same language."
- **Meanings differ by race.** The words the masses use most differ most between peoples. To the Latin peoples **democracy** means the subordination of the individual's will and initiative to the community represented by the State, which directs, centralises, monopolises and manufactures everything, and to which every party appeals. To the Anglo-Saxons, and above all the Americans, it means the intense development of individual will and the fullest possible subordination of the State, which is left nothing to direct except the police, the army and diplomatic relations, not even public instruction.

### Illusions

- Crowds have always lived under illusions and have raised more temples, statues and altars to the creators of illusions than to any other class of men. Religious illusions in the past, philosophic and social ones now, stand at the head of every civilisation. Man overthrows them at the cost of terrible disturbances and seems condemned to set them up again. Without them he would never have left barbarism, and without them he would soon return to it.
- Daniel Lesueur: "To give to men that portion of hope and illusion without which they cannot live, such is the reason for the existence of gods, heroes, and poets." Science has been compromised in hearts hungry for an ideal "because it does not dare to be lavish enough of promises, because it cannot lie."
- The philosophers of the last century destroyed the old illusions, dried up the springs of hope and resignation, and left men facing the blind, silent forces of nature. Philosophy has offered the masses no ideal that charms them, so they turn, "as the insect seeks the light", to the rhetoricians who give them what they want.
- "Not truth, but error has always been the chief factor in the evolution of nations." Socialism is powerful because it is "the last illusion that is still vital", championed by minds ignorant enough of reality to promise mankind happiness boldly. "The masses have never thirsted after truth. They turn aside from evidence that is not to their taste, preferring to deify error, if error seduce them."

### Experience

- Experience is "almost the only effective process by which a truth may be solidly established in the mind of the masses, and illusions grown too dangerous be destroyed." It must happen on a very large scale and be repeated very often.
- One generation's experiences are as a rule useless to the next, which is why historical facts cited as demonstrations serve no purpose.
- The costs: to learn that a society cannot be refashioned from top to bottom by pure reason took several million dead and twenty years of European upheaval. To learn that dictators cost the nations that acclaim them dearly took two ruinous experiences in fifty years: the first cost three million men and an invasion, the second a loss of territory and the need for permanent armies. A third was nearly attempted. To admit that the German army was not a harmless national guard took a terrible war. To recognise that Protection ruins the nations that adopt it would take at least twenty years of disastrous experience.
- The national-guard error shows crowd reasoning at work: the French national guard was a body of undisciplined shopkeepers, so anything bearing the same name was thought harmless. The leaders shared the error. Thiers, "a statesman who often followed the opinion of the crowd but was never in advance of it", told the Chamber on 31 December 1867 that Prussia had only such a national guard, a judgment about as accurate as his prediction of an insignificant future for railways.

### Reason

- Reason is listed only for "the negative value of its influence." Crowds understand only rough-and-ready associations of ideas, and orators who move them always appeal to sentiment.
- The siege-of-Paris case, where an apparent concession saved a man whom logic would have got lynched, is under the orator's procedure in `SKILL.md`.
- Logical minds keep reasoning with crowds and are always surprised that it fails. A syllogism would compel the assent even of an inorganic mass if that mass could follow associations of identities, and a crowd can follow them no better. For nearly two thousand years the most luminous geniuses bowed before superstitions that contradicted the simplest logic. The Middle Ages and the Renaissance had many enlightened men and not one who reasoned his way to doubting the misdeeds of the devil or the need to burn sorcerers.
- **No regret.** Reason would not have driven humanity along the path of civilisation with the ardour its illusions did. "Every race carries in its mental constitution the laws of its destiny", obeyed as the acorn becomes an oak. Seek these forces in the general course of a people's evolution, not in isolated facts, which make history look like a run of improbable chances: a Galilean carpenter becoming an all-powerful God for two thousand years, bands of Arabs conquering most of the Graeco-Roman world, an obscure lieutenant of artillery reigning over peoples and kings.
- "Let us leave reason, then, to philosophers." The sentiments that are the mainsprings of civilisation (honour, self-sacrifice, religious faith, patriotism, the love of glory) are created not by reason but most often in spite of it.

## Fixed beliefs

- **Anatomy as the model.** Living beings have invariable or barely variable characteristics, which only geological ages change, alongside very changeable ones that a breeder easily modifies. Moral characteristics are the same: beneath a people's beliefs lies "a fixed groundwork on which are engrafted opinions as changing as the surface sand on a rock."
- **General beliefs** are very few. Their rise and fall are the high points of every historic race's history, and they "constitute the real framework of civilisation." When one becomes the new dogma, it is the source of a civilisation's institutions, arts and way of life: men of action seek only to realise it, legislators to apply it, philosophers, artists and writers to express it. Even the accessory ideas that arise from it bear its stamp. The Egyptian, medieval European and Arab civilisations each came from a small number of religious beliefs that marked even their least elements.
- **Their life cycle.** A passing opinion is easy to implant, a lasting belief very hard, and once established a belief is as hard to uproot. It changes only at the cost of violent revolution, and revolution succeeds only when the belief has almost lost its hold, sweeping away what habit alone kept in place. After a belief is shaken, the institutions built on it keep their strength and disappear slowly. When it has lost its force entirely, everything resting on it falls. A nation that changes its beliefs must transform every element of its civilisation, and it stays in anarchy until it has found and accepted a new general belief. "General beliefs are the indispensable pillars of civilisations; they determine the trend of ideas. They alone are capable of inspiring faith and creating a sense of duty." The fanatical cult of Rome made the Romans masters of the world, and when it died Rome was doomed. The barbarians emerged from anarchy only on acquiring commonly accepted beliefs.
- **Intolerance as a virtue.** Philosophically open to criticism, the intolerance with which nations defend their beliefs "represents in the life of a people the most necessary of virtues." For it heretics went to the stake, innovators died in despair and millions died on battlefields.
- **The hypnotising effect.** Once implanted, a general belief is long invincible and, however false, imposes itself on the most luminous minds. For more than fifteen centuries Europe held as incontrovertible religious legends that, examined closely, are "as barbarous as those of Moloch" (barbarous philosophically, though in practice they created a whole civilisation). Galileo, Newton and Leibnitz never supposed for an instant that such dogmas could be questioned.
- **The only real tyranny.** Beliefs and the customs they produce regulate the smallest acts, and the most independent spirit cannot escape them. "The tyranny exercised unconsciously on men's minds is the only real tyranny, because it cannot be fought against." From their graves, Moses, Buddha, Jesus and Mahomet exercised a profounder despotism than Tiberius, Ghengis Khan or Napoleon. A conspiracy can overthrow a tyrant, not a belief: in its struggle with Roman Catholicism the French Revolution was beaten. "The only real tyrants that humanity has known have always been the memories of its dead or the illusions it has forged itself."
- **Earthly promises expire.** Religious beliefs offered happiness in a future life, beyond anyone's power to contest. A belief that promises happiness on earth, as socialism does, grows in strength until it triumphs and its realisation begins; then the vanity of its promises appears at once and it loses its prestige. Such a belief can destroy but cannot create.

## Changeable opinions

- Over the substratum of fixed beliefs grows a layer of opinions, ideas and thoughts that are constantly born and dying, some within a day, the more important within about a generation. Their changes are often more superficial than real, and race always shapes them. French royalists, radicals, imperialists and socialists have at bottom an identical ideal, fixed by the mental structure of the French race; other races hold a contrary ideal under similar names. The men of the Revolution adopted the Roman Republic's laws, fasces and togas without becoming Romans.
- **The philosopher's test**: "to investigate what it is which subsists of ancient beliefs beneath their apparent changes, and to identify amid the moving flux of opinions the part determined by general beliefs and the genius of the race." Without it, crowds seem to change their beliefs at will. Between 1790 and 1820, a single generation, the French crowd went from monarchical to revolutionary to imperialist to monarchical again, and in religion from Catholicism to atheism to deism and back to the most pronounced Catholicism. The men of the Convention, who would have neither gods nor masters, became Napoleon's humble servants and then carried candles in processions under Louis XVIII. "Perfidious Albion" became an ally, and Russia a friend. In literature, art and philosophy, schools such as romanticism, naturalism and mysticism rise and die faster still. Applied, the test shows that every change opposed to the general beliefs and the sentiments of the race was transient.
- **Why opinions are more changeable now**, for three reasons:
  1. The old general beliefs are losing their influence and no longer shape the opinions of the moment, which leaves "a crop of haphazard opinions without a past or a future."
  2. The power of crowds is growing and less and less counterbalanced, so their mobility of ideas shows itself unhindered.
  3. The newspaper press keeps the most contrary opinions before the crowd, and each suggestion is cancelled by an opposite one: "An opinion nowadays dies out before it has found a sufficiently wide acceptance to become general."
- **Governments can no longer direct opinion**, a phenomenon new in history. Governments, a few writers and a very few newspapers once shaped public opinion. Now the writers have lost their influence, the newspapers only reflect opinion, and statesmen follow it, dreading it to the point of terror and taking an utterly unstable course. The opinion of crowds has become the supreme guiding principle of politics: it forced the Franco-Russian alliance, and popes, kings and emperors give interviews to submit their views to it.
- **The press has humbled itself too.** It keeps its influence only by reflecting the opinions of crowds and all their variations. It has become a mere supply of information and has given up enforcing any idea or doctrine, because competition would cost it its readers. The old staid organs have vanished or become modern papers, with news packed between light articles, society gossip and financial puffs. Readers suspect every affirmation of being prompted by speculation. Critics can harm a book or play but not make one, and (in the French press) literary criticism has shrunk to a title and a two- or three-line puff. Watching the course of opinion, the effect of every event, bill and speech, has become the chief preoccupation of press and government alike, and nothing is more changeable: crowds execrate today what they applauded yesterday.
- **Divergence and indifference.** With opinion undirected and general beliefs destroyed, convictions diverge in every direction and crowds grow indifferent to whatever does not plainly touch their immediate interests. A doctrine such as socialism finds genuine believers only among the quite illiterate, such as miners and factory workers; the lower middle class and the partly instructed workmen have become utterly sceptical or very unstable. A generation earlier opinions still followed from a fundamental belief: a monarchist knew that men were not descended from monkeys and a republican knew they were, each had names to utter with devotion and names never to mention without invective, and even the Sorbonne taught history that way. Now discussion and analysis wear every opinion's prestige away: "The man of modern times is more and more a prey to indifference."
- **The double edge.** This wearing away is a symptom of decadence, and men of strong convictions exert far more force than critics and the indifferent. Yet given the present power of crowds, an opinion with enough prestige to impose itself generally would become so tyrannical that "the era of free discussion would be closed for a long time." A civilisation dominated by crowds is at the mercy of too many chances to last, and if anything could postpone its ruin for a while it would be the extreme instability of the crowd's opinions and its growing indifference to all general beliefs.
