# The Crowd (Gustave Le Bon)

The age now opening is the **ERA OF CROWDS**: the power of crowds is "the last surviving sovereign force of modern times", the only force that nothing menaces while the old beliefs totter. "The divine right of the masses is about to replace the divine right of kings." The great upheavals of history are the visible effects of invisible changes in ideas, and crowds are their agent: "Little adapted to reasoning, crowds, on the contrary, are quick to act." Civilisations have only been created and directed by a small intellectual aristocracy, never by crowds; "Crowds are only powerful for destruction", acting like microbes that hasten the dissolution of enfeebled bodies. A knowledge of the psychology of crowds is "the last resource of the statesman who wishes not to govern them... but at any rate not to be too much governed by them". Crowds are led not by rules of pure equity but "by seeking what produces an impression on them and what seduces them", because "men never shape their conduct upon the teaching of pure reason." The founders of religions and empires, and even the chiefs of small groups, have always been **unconscious psychologists** with an instinctive, often very sure knowledge of the crowd's character. Example: the most unjust tax may be the best for the masses. An indirect tax, however exorbitant, paid daily in fractions of a farthing on objects of consumption, passes unperceived. Replace it with a proportional tax on income paid in a lump sum, ten times less burdensome in theory, and the large single sum strikes the imagination and raises unanimous protest.

## The psychological crowd

- **Psychological crowd** (or **organised crowd**): an agglomeration in which, under certain given circumstances and only those, "the sentiments and ideas of all the persons in the gathering take one and the same direction, and their conscious personality vanishes." A collective mind forms, transitory but clearly defined. "It forms a single being, and is subjected to the LAW OF THE MENTAL UNITY OF CROWDS."
- **Number and place are not the test.** A thousand people gathered by accident in a public place with no determined object are no crowd. Half a dozen men may be one; thousands of isolated individuals may become one under a violent emotion such as a great national event; an entire nation may become a crowd with no visible agglomeration. What is needed is the action of **predisposing causes**.
- **Combination, not average.** Against Herbert Spencer: a crowd is no summing-up or average of its members but a combination that creates new characteristics, like an acid and a base forming a new body, or cells forming a living being.
- **The unconscious substratum.** Conscious acts come from an unconscious substratum, mostly hereditary, the **genius of a race**. Men differ in intellect but resemble each other in instincts, passions and sentiments: "an abyss may exist between a great mathematician and his boot maker" in intellect, but in character the difference is slight. In a crowd these common qualities become common property: "The heterogeneous is swamped by the homogeneous, and the unconscious qualities obtain the upper hand." Hence "In crowds it is stupidity and not mother-wit that is accumulated", and an assembly of distinguished specialists decides general questions no better than "a gathering of imbeciles".

**Three causes** produce the special characteristics:

1. **Number**: the individual acquires "a sentiment of invincible power" and, the crowd being anonymous and therefore irresponsible, the sentiment of responsibility disappears.
2. **Contagion**: of a hypnotic order. Every sentiment and act is contagious, to the point that a man sacrifices his personal interest to the collective interest.
3. **Suggestibility**: "by far the most important"; contagion is its effect. The man in a crowd resembles a hypnotised subject: his brain paralysed, he is the slave of the unconscious activities of his spinal cord, which the operator directs. Because the suggestion is the same for all, it gains strength by reciprocity. The few strong personalities can at most attempt a diversion by a different suggestion: "a happy expression, an image opportunely evoked, have occasionally deterred crowds from the most bloodthirsty acts."

The result: disappearance of the conscious personality, predominance of the unconscious, the turning of feelings and ideas in one direction by suggestion and contagion, and the tendency to transform suggested ideas immediately into acts. "He is no longer himself, but has become an automaton who has ceased to be guided by his will." By joining an organised crowd "a man descends several rungs in the ladder of civilisation": isolated, cultivated; in a crowd, a barbarian, "a grain of sand amid other grains of sand, which the wind stirs up at will". Juries return verdicts each juror would disapprove; assemblies pass laws each member would disapprove; the miser becomes a spendthrift, the sceptic a believer, the coward a hero.

**The governing conclusion**: "the crowd is always intellectually inferior to the isolated individual, but... from the point of view of feelings and of the acts these feelings provoke, the crowd may, according to circumstances, be better or worse." All depends on the nature of the suggestion. A crowd is as easily heroic as criminal; the Crusades and the volunteers of '93 are crowd heroism, "it is of such heroism that history is made."

## The mind of crowds

Each trait in brief; the mechanisms and examples are in [crowd-mind.md](crowd-mind.md).

- **Impulsive, mobile, irritable**: at the mercy of every exciting cause, unable to dominate its reflexes; "as incapable of willing as of thinking for any length of time." "The notion of impossibility disappears"; an obstacle is destroyed with frenzied rage.
- **Suggestible and credulous**: usually in "a state of expectant attention". The first suggestion formulated spreads by contagion. "The improbable does not exist for a crowd." The learned man and the ignoramus are equally incapable of observation.
- **Exaggerated and ingenuous sentiments**: very simple and very exaggerated; no doubt, no intermediate phases. "A suspicion transforms itself as soon as announced into incontrovertible evidence"; antipathy becomes furious hatred, sympathy adoration.
- **Intolerant, dictatorial, conservative**: accepts or rejects ideas whole, as absolute truths or absolute errors. "Crowds exhibit a docile respect for force, and are but slightly impressed by kindness, which for them is scarcely other than a form of weakness." The hero dear to crowds "will always have the semblance of a Caesar." Ready to revolt against a feeble authority and bow before a strong one. Beneath their violence they are extremely conservative, with a fetish-like respect for tradition; revolutions change the names of institutions, not their essence.
- **Morality**: too impulsive for morality in the sense of steady respect for conventions and repression of selfish impulses, but capable of abnegation, devotion and disinterestedness beyond the isolated individual. "Personal interest is very rarely a powerful motive force with crowds."
- **Ideas**: an idea works on crowds only in "a very absolute, uncompromising, and simple shape", as an image. Image-like ideas succeed each other like magic-lantern slides, so contradictory ideas coexist. The intrinsic worth of an idea is socially without importance; what counts is its effects. An idea acts only when it has entered the unconscious and become a sentiment, which takes much time.
- **Reasoning**: only "the association of dissimilar things possessing a merely apparent connection between each other, and the immediate generalisation of particular cases."
- **Imagination**: "Crowds being only capable of thinking in images are only to be impressed by images." The marvellous and the legendary are the true supports of a civilisation; "the unreal is always of greater moment than the real."
- **Religious shape**: every conviction of crowds takes the form of the **religious sentiment** (worship, fear, blind submission, inability to discuss, the desire to spread, enmity to all who refuse), whether its object is a god, a hero or a political formula. "The crowd demands a god before everything else."

## Remote and immediate factors

The opinions and beliefs of crowds have two kinds of cause (depth in [opinions-and-beliefs.md](opinions-and-beliefs.md)):

- **Remote factors** render crowds capable of adopting certain convictions "and absolutely refractory to the acceptance of others." They prepare the ground, so that a belief that bursts out suddenly is "only spontaneous in appearance": behind it lies "a preliminary and preparatory action of long duration." They are **race** (first in rank, far above the rest), **traditions** ("the synthesis of the race"), **time** ("the sole real creator and the sole great destroyer"), **political and social institutions** (effects, not causes) and **instruction and education**.
- **Immediate factors** come on top of that preparation, without which they would remain without effect, and set the idea loose: they make a riot break out, a strike be decided, or "enormous majorities invest one man with power to overthrow a government." They are **images, words and formulas**, **illusions**, **experience** and **reason** (whose influence is nil, listed only for its negative value).

The French Revolution's remote factors were the writings of the philosophers, the exactions of the nobility and the progress of scientific thought; its immediate factors were the speeches of orators and the court party's resistance to insignificant reforms.

## Leaders and their means of action

Any gathering of living beings places itself under a chief. "A crowd is a servile flock that is incapable of ever doing without a master"; "It is the need not of liberty but of servitude that is always predominant in the soul of crowds." The leader's will is "the nucleus around which the opinions of the crowd are grouped and attain to identity." He has usually started as one of the led, himself hypnotised by the idea whose apostle he became. Leaders are men of action rather than thinkers, often morbidly nervous and half-deranged; the intensity of their faith gives their words great power of suggestion, and their function is **the arousing of faith**: "To endow a man with faith is to multiply his strength tenfold." Their authority is despotic, and that despotism is a condition of their following. Types of leader, and the full treatment of prestige, are in [leaders-and-persuasion.md](leaders-and-persuasion.md).

**To stir a crowd to an act for a short time** (pillage a palace, die at a barricade): rapid suggestion, of which **example** is the most powerful form. The crowd must have been prepared by circumstances, and the operator must possess prestige.

**To imbue a crowd with ideas and beliefs**: three expedients, slow in action but lasting in effect.

1. **Affirmation**: "Affirmation pure and simple, kept free of all reasoning and all proof, is one of the surest means of making an idea enter the mind of crowds. The conciser an affirmation is, the more destitute of every appearance of proof and demonstration, the more weight it carries."
2. **Repetition**: affirmation has no real influence unless constantly repeated, "so far as possible in the same terms." Napoleon held repetition the only figure of rhetoric of serious importance. The repeated statement sinks into the unconscious where motives are forged; we forget who said it and end by believing it. "Affirmation and repetition are alone powerful enough to combat each other."
3. **Contagion**: when an affirmation has been repeated enough, with unanimity, a **current of opinion** forms and contagion takes over, spreading ideas "as intense as that of microbes", without need of physical presence. **Imitation** is a mere effect of contagion; "It is by examples not by arguments that crowds are guided."

**Prestige** crowns them: "a sort of domination exercised on our mind by an individual, a work, or an idea", which "entirely paralyses our critical faculty, and fills our soul with astonishment and respect." It is "the mainspring of all authority". **Acquired prestige** comes from name, fortune, reputation, title, uniform; **personal prestige** is a magnetic faculty independent of all of these. Success is one of its main stepping-stones; failure destroys it quickly, and discussion destroys it slowly but surely: "From the moment prestige is called in question it ceases to be prestige." "For the crowd to admire, it must be kept at a distance."

## Fixed beliefs and changeable opinions

Opinions and beliefs fall into two classes, like the deep water of a stream against the waves on its surface:

- **Great permanent beliefs** endure for centuries and carry a civilisation (feudalism, Christianity, Protestantism; in Le Bon's day the nationalist principle and democratic and social ideas). They are few. A lasting belief is very hard to implant and just as hard to uproot. "The beginning of a revolution is in reality the end of a belief." The moment a belief is doomed "is the moment when its value begins to be called in question": every general belief, being little else than a fiction, survives only so long as it is not examined. Philosophic absurdity has never obstructed one; their triumph seems to need "some mysterious absurdity."
- **Changeable opinions** grow over that substratum and die in a day or a generation. Any change that opposes the general beliefs and the sentiments of the race is transient: "the diverted stream soon resumes its course." Opinions tied to no general belief, formed by suggestion and contagion, shift like wind-blown sandhills.

## Applying the book

Work the user's case through these questions, in order, in Le Bon's terms:

1. **Is it a psychological crowd yet?** Look for the turning of sentiments in one direction and the vanishing of conscious personality, not for numbers. Expect isolated individuals, a committee or a whole nation to qualify once the predisposing causes and an exciting cause are present.
2. **Which kind of crowd?** Heterogeneous, anonymous (street crowds) or not anonymous (juries, parliamentary assemblies, electorates), or homogeneous (sect, caste, class). Anonymity removes the sentiment of responsibility. Read the race next: "THE INFERIOR CHARACTERISTICS OF CROWDS ARE THE LESS ACCENTUATED IN PROPORTION AS THE SPIRIT OF THE RACE IS STRONG", and crowds of different races are "not to be influenced in the same manner." The kinds are in [kinds-of-crowds.md](kinds-of-crowds.md).
3. **What has prepared the ground?** Name the remote factors. A sudden outburst that the remote factors do not explain has not been understood.
4. **What is the exciting suggestion, and who is the leader?** Find the first affirmation or image and the person whose will it carries. Ask whether he has prestige and of which kind.
5. **Is the matter a fixed belief or a changeable opinion?** Fixed beliefs yield only to time and to experience repeated on a large scale. Opinions yield to fresh suggestion.
6. **Act or predict with the levers.** To move the crowd, use images, words and formulas, affirmation, repetition, contagion and prestige, and follow the orator's procedure below. To explain an outcome, trace which of these produced it. To judge a reform, ask whether it fits the character of the race and the existing beliefs, or merely changes names.

**The orator's procedure.** "The laws of logic have no action on crowds." To bring conviction to a crowd:

1. Thoroughly comprehend the sentiments by which it is animated.
2. Pretend to share those sentiments.
3. Modify them by calling up, through rudimentary associations, certain eminently suggestive notions.
4. Be able, if need be, to go back to the point of view you started from.
5. Above all, divine from instant to instant the sentiments your discourse is giving birth to, and vary your language accordingly.

This is why a prepared and studied harangue loses all efficacy: the orator follows his own line of thought, not his hearers'. During the siege of Paris a furious crowd dragged Marshal V—— to the Louvre, sure it had caught him selling the plans of the fortifications to the Prussians. The proof of the charge's absurdity was at hand: he had helped build the fortifications, and the plans were on sale at every bookseller's. The orator who came out used none of it. He cried, "Justice shall be done... and pitiless justice", and promised that the Government would conclude the inquiry and keep the prisoner in custody meanwhile. Calmed by this apparent concession, the crowd broke up, and a quarter of an hour later the Marshal went home. The logical argument would have got him torn to pieces. Speak in striking images, "freed from all accessory explanation", laid before the crowd as a whole with their genesis never indicated. Exaggerate, affirm, repeat, and never attempt to prove anything by reasoning. A single great crime, accident or hope strikes the imagination where a hundred small ones, or a column of statistics, do not: "It is not, then, the facts in themselves that strike the popular imagination, but the way in which they take place and are brought under notice." "To know the art of impressing the imagination of crowds is to know at the same time the art of governing them."

**Diagnostics to keep at hand**

- Unanimity of numerous witnesses is "one of the worst proofs that can be invoked to establish a fact": the events with most doubt about them are those observed by the most people, and a collective observation is usually "the illusion of an individual who, by a process of contagion, has suggestioned his fellows."
- A crowd's judgments are "judgments forced upon them and never judgments adopted after discussion."
- A converted educated man, seen again a few days later, "will put forward afresh his old arguments in exactly the same terms": demonstration does not reach the sentiments that drive conduct.
- Crowds are "several generations behind learned men and philosophers"; statesmen must govern by principles they no longer believe in.
- "Whoever can supply them with illusions is easily their master; whoever attempts to destroy their illusions is always their victim."
- When the crowd hates the image a word evokes, change the word and leave the thing alone.
- Institutions are labels: "The destinies of peoples are determined by their character and not by their government."
- A leader "is seldom in advance of public opinion; almost always all he does is to follow it and to espouse all its errors."
- The work of a crowd is always inferior to that of an isolated individual; a good law is the work of a specialist in his study, spoiled when amendments make it collective.
